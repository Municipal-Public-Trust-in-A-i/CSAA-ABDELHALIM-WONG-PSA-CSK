# Remand and Retrial: Full Record and Evidence Confrontation Plan

**Matter context:** Franciscus Dylan Rosario v. Subhi Abdelhalim, et al.  
**Superior Court:** CGC-21-594102  
**Court of Appeal:** First Appellate District, Division Three, A173827  

**Purpose:** This document converts a remand checklist into an operating plan of roughly seven thousand words. It explains *why* each cluster of tasks matters, how it supports the strategic goal that the defense must confront a complete, authenticated, and procedurally fair record, and it adds suggestions that often separate thorough remand practice from reactive trial court drift. Use it as a single scrollable reference for partners, associates, paralegals, and appellate liaison staff.

**Scope and disclaimer:** This is an internal litigation organization and strategy guide. It is not legal advice. Every item must be adapted to the **exact appellate mandate**, current **operative pleadings**, **discovery orders**, **department** practices, and **case type**. California civil procedure and evidence rules apply; local San Francisco Superior Court e-filing and departmental rules layer on top.

**Relationship to Appeal-MASTER:** Your appellate workflow already emphasized **record augmentation**, certified clerk’s and reporter’s transcripts, and disciplined citation. Carry that same discipline into the superior court on remand so a potential **second appeal** is clean and so the trial record cannot be fragmented by informal stipulations, incomplete productions, or “we never saw that” narratives.

---

## Part A. Strategic frame: what “full record” means and why it fails without a plan

### A.1 Definition you are actually pursuing

“Full record” in trial court is not “every document that exists on earth.” It is a narrower, enforceable idea:

1. **Completeness for issues still live** after the Court of Appeal speaks: every element, every affirmative defense actually tried, and every damages category the jury or court may decide.
2. **Authentication and admissibility** sufficient to survive focused objections, not merely attached to moving papers.
3. **Procedural fairness artifacts** that prove the other side had **notice**, **opportunity to respond**, and **adequate discovery** on the same subjects they later contest.
4. **Preservation** so rulings can be reviewed again if needed: objections, offers of proof, instruction conferences, and verdict form disputes tied to reporter’s transcript pages or settled statements where appropriate.

### A.2 Why remand is a second high-stakes phase

Teams often treat remand as a continuation. Psychologically it is; legally it is often **more dangerous** than the first trial pass. The defense has seen your theories, your witness order, your exhibit numbering, and your appellate arguments. They will **narrow**, **re-frame**, and sometimes **sanitize** the record unless you systematize confrontation.

### A.3 The failure modes this plan targets

| Failure mode | What it looks like | Why it hurts |
|--------------|-------------------|--------------|
| Mandate drift | You litigate issues the opinion foreclosed | Wasted leverage, possible disobedience arguments |
| Record fragmentation | Key items only in motion papers, not admitted at trial | Weak second appeal; confused jury |
| Discovery asymmetry | You stop discovery because “we already tried the case” | Defense escapes new custodians, new documents, post-trial admissions |
| Stipulation regret | You stipulate away authenticity or timing to be “reasonable” | You hand the defense a clean story |
| Instruction slippage | You fight facts but under-invest in CACI and special instructions | Same legal error species can repeat |
| Augmentation debt | Trial exhibits and sidebars never make it into a certifiable form | Second appeal fights over a thin record again |

### A.4 How to use this document

Treat each Part as a **module**. Assign an owner, a deadline column, and a “done means” definition. Weekly partner review should ask only three questions: **What did the mandate require?** **What record are we building this week?** **What are we preserving for the next ruling?**

### A.5 Long-cycle strategy: budget, sequencing, and “quarterly” remand reviews

Remand trials reward **sequencing**. Discovery should generally **lead** depositions, depositions should **lead** expert refinement, expert refinement should **lead** dispositive opposition and **motions in limine**, and those motions should **lead** final witness lists and **verdict** design. When teams run those streams in **parallel** without checkpoints, you get **conflicts**: an expert assumes a fact a deposition later disproves, or a motion in limine **concedes** a document path you intended to **contest**. A **quarterly** remand review (even if the case is shorter than three months, compress the same idea into **monthly** sprints) should reconcile **budget** to **plan**: transcripts, **e-discovery** vendors, **trial** tech, **graphics**, and **surplus** counsel days. Underfunding discovery on remand is a **classic** mistake because the team emotionally believes the first trial “already found” the facts. Appellate reversal often means **some** fact-finding or **legal** framing was **flawed**; that is not the same as **complete** fact development.

**Strategic reasoning:** Sequencing reduces **rework**. Rework destroys **morale** and **client trust**. A visible plan also helps **client** decision-making on **settlement** timing: clients who see a **credible** remand machine may **settle** on better terms; clients who see **drift** may **settle** from **fear** or **fight** from **anger**, neither of which is ideal.

**Importance:** Courts notice **prepared** parties. Preparedness is not **aggression**; it is **professionalism**. Professionalism buys you **hearing** time, **compel** grants, and **careful** instruction rulings.

### A.6 Metrics: how you know the plan is working

Use simple **metrics** internally: number of **deposition** admissions tied to **elements**; percentage of **core** exhibits with **authentication** packets complete; number of **unresolved** discovery disputes older than **30** days; number of **instruction** disputes **briefed** before the instruction conference; number of **RFAs** admitted versus denied; **page-line** impeachment entries completed per defense witness. Metrics should **never** substitute for judgment, but they **surface** bottlenecks early. Refresh the dashboard after every **major** hearing so the team does not **fly blind** into the next **cutoff**.

## Part B. Decode and operationalize the mandate (non-negotiable first module)

### B.1 Checklist

- [ ] Collect the **final opinion** and any **order modifying** after rehearing or partial publication issues.
- [ ] Build a **mandate control sheet** (one page): disposition (reverse, remand, new trial, dismiss, conditional relief), issues decided, issues explicitly not decided, and any **footnoted** limitations.
- [ ] Mark **law of the case** candidates: holdings trial court must follow unless clearly wrong on a new record (calibrate with counsel; standards vary).
- [ ] Mark **forfeiture** and **lack of preservation** language. Do not assume those issues resurrect automatically on remand.
- [ ] Identify **who is bound**: named parties, Doe slots, indemnitors, insurers in the case (as parties or non-parties), and any **successor** entities if corporate changes occurred during appeal.
- [ ] Map **remand type**: plenary new trial, liability-only retrial, damages-only retrial, or limited remand for a discrete ruling (fees, post-trial motion, etc.).
- [ ] Track **remittitur** and **superior court reassignment** rules; note whether the case returns to the same judicial officer.
- [ ] Before **amending** the complaint, verify **each** added or revised **cause of action** against the **mandate** and **res judicata** limits (see **Part D.5**).

### B.2 Strategic reasoning

The mandate is your **north star** and your **fence**. Every motion you file on remand should start with a short paragraph that ties the relief you seek to **what the Court of Appeal ordered or implied**. If you overreach, you invite “disobedience of mandate” rhetoric and you burn credibility. If you underreach, you leave defense room to re-litigate from a posture that already failed once on appeal.

### B.3 Importance

Misreading the mandate produces the most expensive kind of error: you win the appeal and **lose the peace**. You also risk **waiving** arguments that the appellate court left open but that you fail to re-raise clearly in the trial court.

### B.4 Additional suggestions

- Maintain a **single “mandate citation appendix”** (pinpoints to opinion PDF pages) that trial counsel paste into major motions for consistency.
- If the opinion **remands for a new trial** but **narrows** issues, draft a **joint statement** only if it truly helps; otherwise file a **unilateral** case management statement that frames issues accurately and forces the defense to disagree on the record.
- If corporate defendants merged or rebranded during appeal, **early amendment** or **relation-back** analysis may be necessary so later judgments bind the right entity.

## Part C. Superior court intake: establish the record baseline the day remittitur arrives

### C.1 Checklist

- [ ] Obtain **certified remittitur** and comply with any **local** filing or notice requirements upon return.
- [ ] Reconcile **court register** with **endorsed-filed** PDFs; fix gaps with clerk inquiries early.
- [ ] Build a **master index** of: clerk’s transcripts, reporter’s transcripts, augmented volumes, trial exhibits (admitted and offered), **lodged** materials, and sealed filings.
- [ ] Flag **stricken** pleadings, **sustained** objections that kept evidence out, and **unfiled** sidebars (if any were transcribed).
- [ ] Create a **one-page chronology** from complaint through mandate; update monthly.

### C.2 Strategic reasoning

The baseline index is how you defeat **“we never received it”** and **“that was not evidence”** narratives. It also forces your own team to see holes **before** the defense does.

### C.3 Importance

Trial courts manage hundreds of cases. Your index becomes the **external memory** for the department, for mediators, and for your own associates when partners rotate.

### C.4 Additional suggestions

- Mirror the **numbering scheme** you used on appeal (volume, page) when referring to **prior trial** materials to reduce translation errors between appellate and trial teams.
- If demonstratives or animations were used but **not admitted**, decide early whether to **re-offer** with proper foundation or to retire them to avoid confusion.
- Order **key hearing transcripts** from the first trial if they are not already in your possession (law and motion, MSJ, expert challenges, instruction conference), subject to availability and budget.

## Part D. Pleadings: align the operative complaint with the mandate and proof plan

### D.1 Checklist

- [ ] Confirm **operative pleading** matches claims the appellate court left alive; remove or amend **dead** theories to reduce confusion.
- [ ] Decide whether remand requires a **formal amended complaint** (versus a **narrow** errata or **clarifying** amendment only); see **D.5** for the full amendment workflow.
- [ ] If you **modify causes of action**, maintain a **redline** and a **causes-of-action table** (old count, new count, theory, elements source) for the team and for **demurrer** defense.
- [ ] Verify **special pleading** rules: fraud, punitive damages, professional negligence, and similar heightened standards.
- [ ] Update **Doe** allegations and **unknown defendant** theories if entities have been identified during appeal.
- [ ] Build a **pleading-to-proof matrix**: each element, each defense, each damages category mapped to witness and document classes.

### D.2 Strategic reasoning

Pleadings are the **menu** of what the jury may hear. Sloppy alignment between **what you allege** and **what you prove** creates **demurrer** or **nonsuit** pressure even after an appellate win.

### D.3 Importance

On remand, defense counsel often **re-open** pleading attacks that failed before, especially if your amendments introduced new facts or if local culture encourages aggressive pretrial motion practice.

### D.4 Additional suggestions

- If the appellate opinion **changed** the legal landscape (for example, error in a jury instruction or evidentiary ruling), update the **prayer** and **damages** presentation to match the **correct** elements without sounding like a new case.
- Consider a **short case management brief** attaching the **pleading matrix** for the court’s eyes only if the department welcomes it; otherwise keep it internal.

### D.5 Amended complaint and modification of causes of action (remand plan)

Remand is often the right moment to **re-align** the operative complaint with the **mandate**, the **evidence** you can actually prove on retrial, and the **defenses** you now know the opposition will stress. Treat an amended complaint as a **project**, not a filing event: drafting, **leave** analysis, **service**, **responsive** motion practice, **discovery** updates, and **instruction** alignment all move together.

#### D.5.1 Checklist: decision and drafting

- [ ] Build a **causes-of-action control sheet** with one row per theory: **keep as-is**, **revise facts or elements**, **merge** redundant counts, **drop** claims foreclosed by the **mandate** or **res judicata**, or **add** only where law and facts support it.
- [ ] Anchor every **retained** or **revised** cause in the **appellate opinion** and **mandate** text where relevant; note **express** holdings that **bar** or **narrow** theories so you do not invite **disobedience-of-mandate** arguments.
- [ ] For each **new** or **substantially expanded** allegation, analyze **statute of limitations**, **tolling**, and **relation-back** (including **CCP § 378** where new parties or claims may relate to the **same** general event or transaction).
- [ ] Satisfy **heightened pleading** for **fraud**, **punitive damages**, **professional negligence**, and any other **special** rules that apply to your case type; attach or reference **exhibits** where permitted and useful.
- [ ] Update **parties** (real names for **Does**, **successors**, **agents**, **indemnitors** if sued or identified), **capacity**, **agency**, and **vicarious** liability paragraphs so they match **discovery** and **deposition** targets.
- [ ] Revise **prayer** for **damages**, **equitable** relief, and **fees** so it matches **operative** theories and **mandate** scope (for example, **narrow** remand on damages only).
- [ ] Prepare a **clean** operative PDF and a **redline** against the prior complaint for **internal** use and for **meet-and-confer** transparency if you seek a **stipulation** to amend.

#### D.5.2 Checklist: procedure, filing, and opposition readiness

- [ ] Determine whether **leave to amend** is required: **stipulation**, **court order** from a prior motion, **number** of prior amendments, and **local** rules or **standing** orders on amendments after **MSJ** or **trial**-adjacent settings.
- [ ] If **leave** is contested, draft a **motion for leave to amend** (or combine with a **motion** to file the **first amended complaint** as permitted) with a concise **good cause** or **prejudice** story tied to **new** facts, **mandate** effect, or **first-trial** record insights.
- [ ] Calendar **demurrer** and **motion to strike** risk: pre-draft **opposition** themes and **special demurrer** responses for **uncertainty** or **duplicative** counts before you file.
- [ ] Assess **anti-SLAPP** exposure for any **new** claim that touches **protected** activity; if risk exists, **front-load** **prima facie** facts in the amendment where **truthful** and **supported**.
- [ ] File **proof of service** consistent with **all** represented parties; update the **court register** and your **master index** so later **MSJ** and **trial** papers cite the **correct** operative pleading date.
- [ ] Immediately **update** **discovery** plans: **supplemental** interrogatories and **RFPD** to match **new** allegations; refresh **disclosure** obligations if your **CMO** ties them to **operative** pleading.

#### D.5.3 Strategic reasoning

An amended complaint is how you **force** the defense to answer **clear** theories on the **face** of the pleading. Vague **kitchen-sink** amendments **buy** demurrers and **burn** months. **Surgical** amendments that **track** the mandate and the **record** you intend to prove **tighten** the case and **reduce** **nonsuit** risk. **Modifying** causes of action is also how you **remove** **dead** branches that otherwise **clutter** **voir dire**, **instructions**, and **verdict** forms. When the Court of Appeal **changed** the **legal** standard, your **elements** paragraphs should **say so** in **plain** terms tied to **facts**, not **argument**, so the jury later hears **one** coherent story.

#### D.5.4 Importance

Defense counsel **will** use amendments as a **second** bite at **demurrer** and **strike** practice, and sometimes as a **narrative** that you are **shifting** theories unfairly. A **disciplined** amendment file, including a **redline**, **relation-back** memo, and **meet-and-confer** letter, answers that **narrative** before it **sticks**. Amendments also **trigger** **discovery** duties; failing to **update** interrogatories or **document** requests leaves **gaps** the defense will **exploit** at **trial**.

#### D.5.5 Additional suggestions

- Seek a **stipulation** to amend when possible; attach your **proposed** amended complaint as an **exhibit** to the **stipulation** to **avoid** **wording** disputes.
- If the trial court **limits** you to **certain** theories, **comply** and **preserve** only what **mandate** and **law** allow; do not **fight** the trial court on **breadth** when the **Court of Appeal** already **drew** lines.
- After amendment, **rebuild** the **pleading-to-proof matrix** and **verdict-form** outline in **one** pass so **instructions** and **special** interrogatories stay **synchronized**.
- Keep a **single** **“operative complaint”** **bookmark** in the trial binder; **version** errors are a **common** source of **excluded** exhibits offered under the **wrong** theory label.

## Part E. Discovery: re-open, supplement, and close gaps the first trial exposed

### E.1 Checklist

- [ ] Compare appellate **factual recitations** to your first-trial proof; prioritize discovery on facts the Court of Appeal treated as significant.
- [ ] Serve **Form Interrogatories** and **Special Interrogatories** on: identity of persons with knowledge, document custodians, incident reporting chains, insurance layers, data systems, and **post-incident** remedial measures (knowing that subsequent remedial measures rules may limit **some** uses).
- [ ] Serve **Requests for Production** for **native** files where proportionate, **complete communications threads**, internal investigations, maintenance records, training records, vendor contracts, and third-party reports.
- [ ] Serve **Requests for Admission** on **narrow** facts that consumed trial time: dates, identities, authenticity of core documents, and non-controversial links in a chain of custody.
- [ ] Document **meet and confer** in writing with **specific** deficiencies cited.
- [ ] Calendar **motion to compel** only when the record shows **good faith** efforts and **prejudice** from delay.

### E.2 Strategic reasoning

Discovery is the primary engine that **forces confrontation** under oath and in writing. A deposition answer binds a witness more tightly than a press-ready narrative in an opening statement. Strategic discovery also **smokes out** whether the defense will change theories on remand.

### E.3 Importance

If you treat discovery as closed because you “already tried the case,” you hand the defense a **time advantage** and sometimes a **new narrative** built on facts you never locked down after appeal.

### E.4 Additional suggestions

- Add **follow-up** discovery targeted at **anything cited in the respondent’s appellate brief** that was thin at trial; those citations are a roadmap of what they believe matters.
- If **spoliation** was an issue, consider **focused** discovery on retention policies, IT migration, and custodian changes during the appeal window.
- Use **RFAs** sparingly but sharply; overuse dilutes impact and annoys judges.

## Part F. Depositions: design for lock-in, impeachment, and corporate accountability

### F.1 Checklist

- [ ] **Person most knowledgeable** or corporate designee depositions under CCP §2025.230 with **numbered topics** aligned to interrogatories and document requests.
- [ ] **Individual percipient** depositions for every important person on prior witness lists who did not testify or testified narrowly.
- [ ] **Expert depositions** on schedule; plan **rebuttal** if defense swaps methodologies.
- [ ] For each deposition, maintain a **one-page summary**: admissions, contradictions with documents, contradictions with prior sworn statements, and **trial exhibit** mapping.

### F.2 Strategic reasoning

Depositions convert **documents** into **human commitments**. They also give you **impeachment** ammunition if a witness tries to **upgrade** testimony on remand.

### F.3 Importance

Juries often decide cases on **credibility**. A deposition transcript read at trial with calm precision is one of the most **durable** ways to confront a witness with their own record.

### F.4 Additional suggestions

- Notice **remote** vs **in-person** deposition early; disputes waste time.
- For **corporate** witnesses, attach the **document production** as exhibits to the deposition notice where rules allow, so “I do not recall” answers face immediate **document confrontation**.
- Consider **video** depositions for key witnesses if budget permits; Clips can be powerful if the court permits and foundation is clean.

## Part G. Experts: CCP §2034 exchange as structural discipline

### G.1 Checklist

- [ ] Build a **2034 calendar** from the first case management order: initial exchange, supplemental exchange, discovery cutoff, expert discovery cutoff, and motion cutoff.
- [ ] Require **all opinions, bases, data considered, and writings** as the statute and interpretive case law demand.
- [ ] If defense adds **new opinions** late, plan **motion to strike** or **exclude** and evaluate **continuance** requests only for **fairness**, not for **tactical drift**.
- [ ] Align **damages** assumptions across medical, economic, vocational, and life-care experts.

### G.2 Strategic reasoning

Expert disputes are not only about **admissibility**. They are about **forcing** the defense to disclose the **intellectual chain** from data to conclusion. Weak chains are **Sargon** vulnerabilities.

### G.3 Importance

In personal injury and similar cases, **damages** are often where appeals live after liability is established. Expert discipline on remand protects both **trial** and **appellate** fronts.

### G.4 Additional suggestions

- Maintain an **expert dependency chart**: if Witness X’s testimony falls, which expert assumptions need **re-baselining**.
- If the appellate opinion **changed** a legal element, ask your experts for **short errata** or supplemental reports that **explicitly** incorporate the corrected standard (without sounding argumentative).

## Part H. Dispositive and gatekeeping motions: plaintiff-side preparation, not only defense

### H.1 Checklist

- [ ] Maintain an **opposition bank** keyed to appellate holdings; cite mandate where appropriate as **law of the case** or binding guidance, calibrate with counsel.
- [ ] Prepare **separate statement** shells for **MSJ** opposition before defense files.
- [ ] Track **Sargon**, **Kelly/Frye** (if applicable), and **foundational** weaknesses for each expert on both sides.
- [ ] If punitive damages remain, map **financial discovery** limits and **clear and convincing** evidence planning.

### H.2 Strategic reasoning

You may not file many dispositive motions as plaintiff, but you **will** defend them. Pre-drafting separates **calm** record-building from **panic** typing.

### H.3 Importance

A remand MSJ sometimes lands because defense counsel **must** try something new. Your opposition should feel **inevitable**: facts pinned in discovery, documents bates-stamped, and deposition lines highlighted.

### H.4 Additional suggestions

- If the Court of Appeal **reversed** on a **legal** ground, consider whether a **partial adjudication** motion helps **you** on narrow issues; only file if the mandate and timing support it.

## Part I. Judicial notice and official records: make “law” and “indisputable fact” clean

### I.1 Checklist

- [ ] Separate **mandatory** judicial notice requests under Evidence Code §§ 451–453 from **discretionary** requests under §§ 452–460.
- [ ] For **local ordinances**, **traffic control orders**, **MUTCD** excerpts, and engineering standards, assemble **clean PDFs** with **authentication** declarations where needed.
- [ ] For **out-of-state law**, plan **notice** or **ascertainment** through permissible sources.
- [ ] Avoid **argumentative** judicial notice requests; they invite denial and waste credibility.

### I.2 Strategic reasoning

Judicial notice is how you **strip** unnecessary foundation battles for **stable** propositions so trial time focuses on **genuine** disputes.

### I.3 Importance

In street-design, signage, and municipal cases, **official records** often carry disproportionate weight. If you fight them ad hoc at trial, you risk **piecemeal** rulings and weak preservation.

### I.4 Additional suggestions

- Pair each major notice request with the **CACI** instruction that depends on that legal or factual proposition, where applicable.
- For **web** content, plan **foundation** that survives **authentication** challenges; printouts alone are a common failure point.

## Part J. Evidentiary motions: in limine, authentication, hearsay, and offers of proof

### J.1 Checklist

- [ ] Draft **motions in limine** as a **set**: exclude improper prejudice, improper character evidence, golden-rule arguments, and **unbounded** speculation; secure rulings on **core** plaintiff exhibits.
- [ ] Build an **authentication packet** per exhibit class: business records, public records, photos, videos, maps, medical records, EHR exports, and GPS or device data where used.
- [ ] Maintain a **hearsay matrix**: exhibit, declarant, exception, and witness who will lay foundation.
- [ ] Prepare **Evid. Code § 402** foundations for expert case-specific hearsay if used.
- [ ] For excluded evidence, draft **offer of proof** scripts that are **minimal** but **adequate** for appellate review.

### J.2 Strategic reasoning

In limine practice shapes **jury intuition** before opening statements. It also forces the court to **commit** to rulings that you can **preserve** clearly.

### J.3 Importance

A second appeal after remand often turns on whether trial counsel **objected**, **proposed** the correct instruction, and made an **offer of proof**. This module is pure **insurance**.

### J.4 Additional suggestions

- Create a **redaction battle plan** if defense over-redacts: meet and confer, then motion template.
- If **social media** or **text** evidence matters, plan **chain of custody** and **sender identification** early.

## Part K. Trial documents: exhibit lists, witness lists, stipulations, and deposition designations

### K.1 Checklist

- [ ] **Exhibit list** with consistent **numbering**; one **master index** for court, witnesses, and internal trial team.
- [ ] **Witness list** with topics and time estimates; align with **deposition designations**.
- [ ] **Stipulation** strategy: stipulate **narrowly**; avoid stipulations that erase **disputed** narrative edges you need for impeachment.
- [ ] **Trial binders**: court copy, working copy, impeachment copy; keep versions synchronized.

### K.2 Strategic reasoning

Stipulations are **contracts** with evidentiary effect. A “helpful” stipulation that **removes** a timeline dispute may **remove** your best impeachment.

### K.3 Importance

Juries notice **organization**. Chaos in exhibits reads as **weakness** even when the underlying facts are strong.

### K.4 Additional suggestions

- Pre-mark **impeachment** exhibits separately so you do not fumble during cross.
- Build a **cross-examination outline** tied to **deposition page-line** references for every major defense witness.

---

## Part L. Jury instructions, jury questions, and verdict forms: mandate-aligned law delivery

### L.1 Checklist

- [ ] Rebuild **CACI** from zero with the **appellate opinion** as the top note for any corrected legal standards.
- [ ] Draft **special instructions** only where necessary; avoid **argumentative** specials.
- [ ] Prepare **written objections** to defense instructions with **legal** support; log **oral** objections at conference.
- [ ] Design **verdict form** paths that match **causes of action** and **comparative fault** math.
- [ ] Prepare a **protocol memo** for **jury questions**: neutral answers, no new facts, proper read-backs.

### L.2 Strategic reasoning

Your Appeal-MASTER materials already treated **proposed jury instructions** and **jury notes** as record-critical. On remand, instructions are how you **implement** what the Court of Appeal said was wrong the first time.

### L.3 Importance

Instruction error is a **classic** reversible category. If you fix the law in the briefing but **lose** the instruction battle at trial, you **recreate** the same species of appeal.

### L.4 Additional suggestions

- Maintain a **parallel set**: “instruction we want,” “instruction defense wants,” “CACI base,” and **pinpoint** authority for disputes.
- If **multiple defendants** with distinct theories, ensure **apportionment** language does not confuse the jury.

## Part M. Pretrial statements and conferences: pin down issues and prevent sandbagging

### M.1 Checklist

- [ ] Treat pretrial statements as **binding issue-framing** where local rules support that culture; where not, still use them as **advocacy**.
- [ ] Force clarity on **affirmative defenses** actually tried.
- [ ] Object in writing to **late** defense theories that violate discovery cutoffs or cause **prejudice**.

### M.2 Strategic reasoning

Sandbagging thrives in **vague** issue lists. Precision helps the trial judge **manage** the case and helps you **preserve** complaints about unfair surprise.

### M.3 Importance

Surprise at trial is not only unfair; it is **expensive** in juror attention and in **mistrial** risk.

### M.4 Additional suggestions

- Attach a **one-page issue list** as an exhibit to your pretrial statement when allowed: **liability**, **causation**, **comparative fault**, **damages categories**, **punitive** (if any).

---

## Part N. Trial execution: offers, objections, and the “no ambiguity” record

### N.1 Checklist

- [ ] **Offer** each critical exhibit with **short** foundation; obtain **rulings**; make **offers of proof** when excluded.
- [ ] **Read** deposition designations with **page-line** precision; log objections.
- [ ] **Cross-examination** from **documents first**, then **sworn statements**; close loops before moving on.
- [ ] **Demonstratives**: seek early rulings; match **scale** and **accuracy** to underlying evidence.

### N.2 Strategic reasoning

Ambiguity helps the **losing** side on appeal. Your job is a **clean** record: what was offered, what was excluded, why it mattered.

### N.3 Importance

Appellate courts are not triers of fact. They review **what made it in** and **what the jury was told** the law was.

### N.4 Additional suggestions

- Assign one paralegal or junior lawyer as **real-time** “objection and ruling” scribe; compare nightly to **reporter’s rough** if available.

## Part O. Post-trial: JNOV, new trial, fees, costs, and second-appeal preservation

### O.1 Checklist

- [ ] Draft **conditional** motions as needed depending on outcome.
- [ ] Preserve **instruction** error with **specific** objections and **correct** instructions on the record.
- [ ] Begin **fee** and **cost** motions early if statutory or contractual rights attach.
- [ ] Open a **second appeal** folder the night of verdict: issues list, transcript targets, exhibit list, instruction battles.

### O.2 Strategic reasoning

Post-trial motion practice is **not** an afterthought; it is where you **repair** or **cement** the judgment.

### O.3 Importance

Some errors are **preserved** only if raised in **timely** post-trial motions. Know which ones apply to your case type.

### O.4 Additional suggestions

- If **additur/remittitur** doctrines could matter, build damages **spreadsheet** models during trial, not after.

## Part P. Insurance, indemnity, and third-party records (where the “full record” often hides)

### P.1 Checklist

- [ ] Map **insurance** relationships: duty to defend, reservations of rights, independent counsel, indemnity agreements.
- [ ] Subpoena **third-party** holders of objective records: agencies, contractors, vendors, hospitals, tow operators, and others as law and proportionality allow.

### P.2 Strategic reasoning

Some of the most important facts are **not** in the defendant’s possession. Third-party records reduce **wiggle room**.

### P.3 Importance

## Part T. Closing cautions and ethical boundaries

Aggressive discovery and deposition plans must stay **proportionate** and **non-harassing**. Bad-faith discovery invites **protective orders**, **sanctions**, and **jury-level** sympathy for opponents. The goal is **complete confrontation**, not **pressure for its own sake**. Always align with **mandate scope**, **discovery limits**, and **privilege** rules.

### T.1 Why ethics and strategy converge here

California discovery practice assumes **good faith** meet-and-confer effort. When you build a “full record” plan, you are also building a **reputation** with the trial court. A judge who believes you are **hiding** discovery disputes or **papering** the file with marginal motions will give you less oxygen on the issues that truly matter. Conversely, a judge who sees **clean** letters, **specific** deficiencies, and **proportionate** requests will be more willing to **compel** and to **sanction** genuine obstruction. Your external narrative to the court should always match your internal narrative to the team: we want **admissible**, **authenticated**, **timely** evidence, not endless friction.

### T.2 Red lines

Do not use discovery to **harass** individuals unrelated to the claims. Do not leak **protected** health information in public filings; use **sealing** motions and **minimal** redacted exhibits for the public register when the law requires. Do not instruct witnesses to **conceal** discoverable facts; fix problems through **objections**, **motions for protective order**, and **privilege logs**, not through gamesmanship that can **explode** credibility if exposed.

## Appendix C. Meet-and-confer letter ingredients, privilege logs, and motion-to-compel scaffolding

This appendix adds **tactical templates** in prose form. Adapt to facts and to your department’s expectations.

### C.1 Meet-and-confer letter: what “good” contains

A strong meet-and-confer letter is **short**, **numbered**, and **anchored** in **specific** discovery requests. It should: identify each **deficient** response by **request number**; quote or paraphrase the **evasive** answer; explain **why** it fails under the discovery statutes; propose a **cure** (production date, supplemental verification, native format, rolling production schedule); and propose **conference** dates. It should attach **only** what helps: a **privilege log** sample dispute, a **redacted** example of what you believe is **non-privileged**, or a **timeline** showing **prior** delays. Judges read hundreds of these. **Clarity** beats **volume**.

**Strategic reasoning:** Meet-and-confer letters are often **exhibits** to later motions. They should read **reasonable** to a neutral reader. If you sound **angry** but **vague**, you lose **both** the motion and **credibility**.

**Importance:** Many courts will not hear a motion to compel without **genuine** meet-and-confer effort. Your letter is the **proof** of that effort.

### C.2 Privilege log disputes: narrow the fight

When defendants withhold large categories under **privilege**, consider **targeted** challenges: **form** of log (fields missing), **overbroad** withholdings on **non-legal** business documents, **attorney-client** claims on **non-confidential** communications, and **work-product** claims on **factual** investigations. Calibrate with counsel; privilege law is **fact-intensive**.

**Additional suggestions:** Ask for **non-privileged** substitutes where **redacted** versions can be produced. Sometimes **quick** resolution of **one** representative document unlocks **dozens** of similar items.

### C.3 Motion to compel: record-first structure

Your motion should begin with **procedural** compliance: dates of requests, responses, meet-and-confer timeline. Then **substantive** sections per **category** of withheld material. Then **prejudice**: how the lack of materials impairs **depositions**, **expert** opinions, **dispositive** opposition, or **trial** preparation. End with a **narrow** request for relief: **specific** documents, **specific** interrogatory answers, **sanctions** only if the record supports **willful** or **persistent** failure.

**Importance:** Overbroad relief invites **denial**. Narrow relief invites **grants** and **follow-up** motions if needed.

### C.4 Sanctions: when and how to ask

Sanctions motions are **double-edged**. They can **align** the court’s irritation with your opponent’s discovery abuse, or they can **paint** you as **litigious**. Ask for sanctions when: there is a **pattern**; there is **prior** court involvement; there is **prejudice** with **dates**; and **lesser** remedies failed. Always consider **lesser** remedies first in the **meet-and-confer** chain to show **reasonableness**.

## Appendix D. “Confrontation audit”: thirty-two questions before trial starts

2. Do we have **custodian** testimony or **records** declarations for **business records**?

4. Have we **deposed** every person the defense listed with **knowledge**?
5. Have we **deposed** every **corporate** topic we need?
6. Do **expert** assumptions match **deposition** and **document** facts?
7. Are **damages** models **sensitive** to **comparative** fault scenarios?
8. Have we **read** defense **expert** reports for **internal** inconsistencies?
9. Do we have **impeachment** page-line cites for **key** defense witnesses?
10. Are **CACI** instructions **aligned** with **mandate** corrections?
11. Have we **objected in writing** to **defense** instructions that **misstate** law?
12. Is the **verdict form** **mathematically** consistent with **instructions**?
13. Have we **noticed** all **judicial** notice materials with **proper** requests?
14. Are **demonstratives** **scaled** and **sourced** to **underlying** evidence?
15. Do we have **backup** trial exhibits if **primary** exhibits are **excluded**?
16. Are **deposition** designations **within** time limits and **properly** noticed?
17. Have we **logged** **hearsay** exceptions for **every** exhibit **cluster**?
18. Do we know which **facts** are **undisputed** for **MSJ** risk?
19. Do we know which **facts** are **disputed** for **trial** focus?
20. Have we **subpoenaed** **third parties** with **objective** records?
21. Are **privilege** disputes **resolved** or **narrowed** before **trial**?
22. Is **ESI** production **complete** for **key** custodians?
23. Do we have **photos** or **video** with **time** and **location** foundation?

32. After amendment, are **discovery** requests and **responses** **updated** so **no** theory lacks **document** and **deposition** support?

**Strategic reasoning:** This audit converts abstract “full record” language into **binary** readiness checks. ## Appendix F. Trial technology, “hot seat,” and courtroom logistics

Modern trial presentation is part of **record completeness** because juries **understand** visual sequencing when it is **tethered** to **admitted** exhibits. Poor tech creates **gaps** in comprehension that **benefit** the side arguing **confusion** or **unfair prejudice**.

### F.1 Checklist

- [ ] Confirm **courtroom** equipment: **ELMO**, **HDMI**, **PDF** presentation software, **backup** laptop, **offline** copies of **all** trial exhibits.
- [ ] Assign a **hot-seat** operator who **rehearses** with **trial** counsel; rehearse **impeachment** sequences so **page-line** reads sync with **screen** highlights.
- [ ] Build **chronology** slides only from **admitted** or **judicially noticed** facts unless the court permits **opening** graphics with **appropriate** caveats.
- [ ] Prepare **paper** binders as **fail-safe** even if you are **digital-first**; some witnesses and judges prefer **paper**.

### F.2 Strategic reasoning

Technology failures **eat** trial time and **fray** jury patience. The defense benefits when your **story** feels **disorganized**. A **rehearsed** hot seat is **advocacy**.

### F.3 Importance

On **retrial**, jurors may **compare** unconsciously to the first trial’s **clarity**. Meet or **exceed** that standard.

### F.4 Additional suggestions

- **Color** and **font** choices should meet **accessibility** needs; **small** text **excludes** jurors with **vision** limitations.
- **Video** clips require **foundational** **clips** and **transcripts** for **read-along** where needed.
