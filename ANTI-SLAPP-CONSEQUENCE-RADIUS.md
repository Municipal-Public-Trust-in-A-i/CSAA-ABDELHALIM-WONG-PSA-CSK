## I. The Door That Was Already Shut: Section 425.17(c) as Threshold Bar

The opening proposition of this narrative is statutory rather than tactical. Code of Civil Procedure section 425.17, subdivision (c), removes a defined category of cases from the reach of [Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16](CASE-LAW-AND-STATUTES/statutes-cal/CCP-425.16.md), subdivision (b), entirely. Where the predicates of section 425.17(c) are present on the face of the operative pleading, the special motion to strike is not a motion the defense may lose on the merits; it is a motion the defense is not permitted to bring. The carve-out operates at the threshold, before the two-step inquiry of section 425.16(b)(1) is reached, and before any prong-one or prong-two showing is engaged.

The text of section 425.17(c) sets the conditions:

> Section 425.16 does not apply to any cause of action brought against a person primarily engaged in the business of selling or leasing goods or services, including, but not limited to, insurance, securities, or financial instruments, arising from any statement or conduct by that person if both of the following conditions exist:

(Code Civ. Proc., sec. 425.17, subd. (c).)

The first condition narrows the regulated speech and conduct that the carve-out reaches:

> The statement or conduct consists of representations of fact about that person's or a business competitor's business operations, goods, or services, that is made for the purpose of obtaining approval for, promoting, or securing sales or leases of, or commercial transactions in, the person's goods or services, or the statement or conduct was made in the course of delivering the person's goods or services.

(Code Civ. Proc., sec. 425.17, subd. (c)(1).)

The second condition fixes the audience:

> The intended audience is an actual or potential buyer or customer, or a person likely to repeat the statement to, or otherwise influence, an actual or potential buyer or customer, or the statement or conduct arose out of or within the context of a regulatory approval process, proceeding, or investigation, except where the statement or conduct was made by a telephone corporation in the course of a proceeding before the California Public Utilities Commission and is the subject of a lawsuit brought by a competitor, notwithstanding that the conduct or statement concerns an important public issue.

(Code Civ. Proc., sec. 425.17, subd. (c)(2).)

The California Supreme Court's most cited interpretive distillation of the carve-out comes from Simpson Strong-Tie Co., Inc. v. Gore (2010) 49 Cal.4th 12, 30. The Court walked the four predicates and announced the operative formulation:

> We interpret section 425.17(c) to exempt from the anti-SLAPP law a cause of action arising from commercial speech when (1) the cause of action is against a person primarily engaged in the business of selling or leasing goods or services; (2) the cause of action arises from a statement or conduct by that person consisting of representations of fact about that person's or a business competitor's business operations, goods, or services; (3) the statement or conduct was made either for the purpose of obtaining approval for, promoting, or securing sales or leases of, or commercial transactions in, the person's goods or services or in the course of delivering the person's goods or services; and (4) the intended audience for the statement or conduct meets the definition set forth in section 425.17(c)(2).

(Simpson Strong-Tie Co., Inc. v. Gore (2010) 49 Cal.4th 12, 30.)

The same opinion fixes the burden of proof. The Court invoked a familiar canon of construction:

> It is a 'familiar' and 'longstanding' legal principle that 'when a proviso . . . carves an exception out of the body of a statute or contract those who set up such exception must prove it.' Likewise, in California, 'it has been declared that where the statute has exemptions, exceptions or matters which will avoid the statute the burden is on the claimant to show that he falls within that category.'

(Simpson Strong-Tie Co., Inc. v. Gore (2010) 49 Cal.4th 12, 30.)

Section 425.17, subdivision (e), adds a structural cost: a denial premised on the carve-out is not subject to interlocutory appeal under section 425.16(i) or under section 904.1(a)(13). The defense forfeits the immediate appeal it would otherwise enjoy from a denial under section 425.16(b). The strategic value of an interlocutory appeal as a delay-and-leverage device disappears at the moment the denial rests on the carve-out.

The placement of the insurance industry in the enumerated list of section 425.17(c) is not incidental. The Legislature named "insurance, securities, or financial instruments." CSAA Insurance Exchange is a licensed California insurer, judicially noticeable from the Department of Insurance public licensure record and pleaded on the face of the First Amended Complaint in CGC-25-631802. The first predicate of section 425.17(c) is satisfied without dispute.

What this filing accomplishes mechanically, regardless of intent, is the erection of a threshold defense the plaintiff need only invoke. Once invoked, section 425.17(c) shifts the operative inquiry from whether the defense has met its prong-one burden under section 425.16(b)(1) to whether the defense has carried the burden Simpson Strong-Tie places on a movant who would defeat the carve-out. The defense did not file a brief that tried to negate the four predicates. It filed a brief that proceeded as if section 425.17(c) did not exist. That choice is the subject of every section that follows.

## II. The Decision to File: Why Anti-SLAPP Was the Wrong Weapon

Two case authorities define the prong-one inquiry that the defense was required to engage. Park v. Bd. of Trustees (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1063, supplies the modern formulation:

> A claim arises from protected activity when that activity underlies or forms the basis for the claim. Critically, 'the defendant's act underlying the plaintiff's cause of action must itself have been an act in furtherance of the right of petition or free speech.' '[T]he mere fact that an action was filed after protected activity took place does not mean the action arose from that activity for the purposes of the anti-SLAPP statute.' Instead, the focus is on determining what 'the defendant's activity [is] that gives rise to his or her asserted liability' and whether that activity constitutes protected speech or petitioning.

(Park v. Bd. of Trustees (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1063.)

Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82, 92, supplies the definitional axis:

> The anti-SLAPP statute's definitional focus is not the form of the plaintiff's cause of action but, rather, the defendant's activity that gives rise to his or her asserted liability, and whether that activity constitutes protected speech or petitioning.

(Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82, 92.)

The defense's papers neither characterized the regulated insurance claim-handling conduct as the activity underlying the operative claims nor explained why that conduct falls inside section 425.16(e). The First Amended Complaint pleads denial of an insurance claim, premature file closure, omission of statutory notice, and failure to investigate. None of those acts is petitioning activity within the meaning of Park or Navellier; each is regulated commercial conduct within the meaning of section 425.17(c).

The strategic logic implied by the filing pattern is to read section 425.16(e)(2) as reaching every claim-handling communication on the theory that any such communication is incident to anticipated litigation. That theory ignores Park's gravamen test and section 425.17(c) entirely, and converts a category of regulated insurance conduct into a category of constitutional speech by labeling alone.

## III. The Discovery Stay Backfire: Section 425.16(g) Froze the Defense's Own Window

Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, subdivision (g), provides that all discovery proceedings in the action are stayed upon the filing of a notice of motion made pursuant to that section, and that the stay remains in effect until notice of entry of the order ruling on the motion. The court may, on noticed motion and for good cause shown, order specified discovery to proceed; absent that order, the stay is comprehensive.

The stay is automatic. It is anchored to the filing of the special motion, not to any court order. It is also bilateral: it freezes the discovery rights of every party in the action, including the moving defendant.

The defense filed the anti-SLAPP motions in CGC-25-631801 and CGC-25-631802 on April 9, 2026. From that moment, the defense lost its access to the ordinary discovery devices through which it would have built its prong-one fact record: written discovery to the plaintiff, deposition notices to fact witnesses, third-party subpoenas to claim-file custodians, and document demands. It could not use the discovery regime to prove the affirmative case it was required to make at prong one.

The strategic logic implied by the timing is that the special motion was filed on the calculation that it could be won on the four corners of the operative pleading and on the defense's own declarations, without recourse to discovery. That calculation is not unreasonable where the protected activity is on the face of the complaint. It is unreasonable where the gravamen is regulated insurance claim-handling conduct and where the section 425.17(c) carve-out is plainly available on the face of the operative pleading.

Two things follow from this choice. First, the defense waived the discovery window it would otherwise have had to develop affirmative facts on prong one. Section 425.16(b)(2) limits the court to the pleadings and supporting and opposing affidavits; the defense cannot revisit that record by propounding discovery during the pendency of the motion. Second, the stay does not insulate the defense from a discovery default that accrued before the motion was filed. Section 425.16(g) stays proceedings going forward; it does not erase a failure that had already accrued.

What this filing accomplishes mechanically, regardless of intent, is the conversion of the defense's preferred fact-finding posture into a frozen one, while leaving the plaintiff's discovery defaults open as live questions for the deem-admit and compel motions filed April 23. The defense walked into a trap of its own design and locked the door behind itself.

## IV. Conscription of the Claim Record: Anti-SLAPP as Confession by Procedural Volunteer

The act of filing a special motion to strike under section 425.16(b)(1) commits the moving defendant to a particular characterization of the operative pleading. The motion is an affirmative pleading that declares: this claim arises from protected activity. The court reads the operative pleading and the defense's characterization side by side; it reads the gravamen, not the defense's preferred narrative.

The Park gravamen test maps directly onto the operative pleading in CGC-25-631802. The First Amended Complaint pleads CSAA Insurance Exchange's premature claim-file closure within approximately twenty-one days of a February 4, 2021 motor vehicle collision; the omission of the Department of Insurance review notice required by 10 California Code of Regulations, section 2695.7, subdivision (b)(3); the failure to retrieve reasonably available silent-witness materials before denial; and the issuance of a denial letter on February 25, 2021, that recited a "carefully reviewed" conclusion without identifying any reviewed material. Each of those acts is regulated commercial conduct within the meaning of section 425.17(c). None is petitioning activity within the meaning of section 425.16(e).

The defense's moving papers conscripted the claim record as the testing ground. Section 425.16(b)(2) requires the court to consider the pleadings and supporting and opposing affidavits. Once the defense placed the operative pleading at the center of prong one, it lost the ability to argue that the gravamen is anything other than what the operative pleading actually pleads. Park does not allow the defense to redefine the gravamen by labeling the conduct "in connection with" anticipated litigation; Park looks at what the operative pleading describes.

Navellier forecloses the relabel route by directing the inquiry to the activity itself, not the form of the cause of action. (Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82, 92.) The defense did not engage either authority on its own terms.

Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 396, narrows the maneuver further. The Court explained that the prong-one analysis must operate at the level of the allegations of protected activity that are asserted as grounds for relief, not at the level of an entire cause of action wholesale or a single paragraph carved out of context:

> When the Legislature declared that a 'cause of action' arising from activity furthering the rights of petition or free speech may be stricken unless the plaintiff establishes a probability of prevailing, it had in mind allegations of protected activity that are asserted as grounds for relief.

(Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 396.)

Baral does not authorize the movant to designate one paragraph of a multi-paragraph cause of action as the gravamen of the entire action, then claim victory on that designation. Baral requires the movant to identify the specific allegations of protected activity that supply the elements of liability and to test those allegations against the prong-two evidentiary standard. The defense's moving papers did not perform that operation.

By committing to a prong-one theory that requires the gravamen to be petitioning activity, the defense placed the operative pleading on a record where the gravamen test will be applied de novo on appellate review under Park. (Park v. Bd. of Trustees (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1067.) If the trial court denies the motion on the gravamen ground, the defense has built an appellate record under section 425.17(e) that forecloses interlocutory appeal and locks in the trial court's framing.

On the documented record, the most economical reading of this move is that the defense attempted to substitute label for substance and produced a record that documents the substitution. The pleading remains what it was. The motion records what the defense argued it was. The disjunction is on the docket.

## V. The Omitted Authorities: Candor Toward the Tribunal and the Strategic Cost of Silence

The defense's moving papers on the anti-SLAPP motion did not address Code of Civil Procedure section 425.17, subdivision (c). They did not engage Simpson Strong-Tie Co., Inc. v. Gore (2010) 49 Cal.4th 12, 30. They did not engage the gravamen test of Park v. Bd. of Trustees (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1063. They did not engage the claim-by-claim framework of Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 396. The omission is documentary, recorded on the face of the moving papers filed April 9, 2026.

Each omitted authority is directly adverse and controlling. Section 425.17(c) is the statutory bar enacted by the Legislature to address insurance and commercial defendants invoking the special motion against consumer claims; the statute names insurance among the enumerated categories. Simpson Strong-Tie is the Supreme Court's interpretive distillation of the section 425.17(c) elements and places the burden of negating the carve-out on the movant. Park is the controlling formulation of the gravamen test. Baral governs the analysis when a single cause of action contains both protected and unprotected allegations, and forecloses the maneuver of designating a single paragraph as the gravamen of the entire action.

The omission of these four authorities from the moving papers is documentary; the inference is bounded by what that omission, in context, tends to show, namely, that the defense filed a brief that proceeded as if the controlling carve-out and the controlling gravamen authorities did not exist.

The duty of candor toward the tribunal sets the framework for evaluating the omission. California Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 3.3(a)(2), summarized, prohibits a lawyer from knowingly failing to disclose to the tribunal legal authority in the controlling jurisdiction known to the lawyer to be directly adverse to the position of the client and not disclosed by opposing counsel. Business and Professions Code, section 6068, subdivision (d), summarized, imposes on every member of the State Bar of California the duty to employ such means only as are consistent with truth, and never to seek to mislead the judge or any judicial officer by an artifice or false statement of fact or law. Both provisions reach an attorney who files a brief that asks the court to rule on a question without disclosing the controlling authority that resolves the question against the client.

The structural cost of the silence is twofold. First, the silence fixed the state of the record on the section 425.17(c) issue. The defense's reply was the last paper before the hearing under California Rules of Court, rule 3.1300; once the reply closed without the four authorities being addressed, the section 425.17(c) carve-out trap was set. The defense cannot retroactively engage the omission through a sur-reply, a supplemental brief, or oral argument unaccompanied by leave of court. Second, the silence created the record that the plaintiff's May 8, 2026, [section 128.7](CASE-LAW-AND-STATUTES/statutes-cal/CCP-128.7.md)(c)(1) safe-harbor letter invoked. The letter identifies specific contentions in the May 6 reply as not warranted by existing law and, by reference to the omitted authorities, supplies the ground for a sanctions motion ripe to file on May 29, 2026, if the contentions are not withdrawn or appropriately corrected.

The hypothesis that counsel knew of the controlling authorities and elected not to cite them is consistent with signals that the brief is otherwise competently drafted, that the firm has handled prior anti-SLAPP matters, and that the omission is uniform across both companion cases on identical theories. The hypothesis that counsel did not know of the controlling authorities is consistent with the absence of any cite to section 425.17 in the moving papers; it is inconsistent with a firm profile that represents California civil litigation experience.

What the omission accomplishes mechanically, regardless of intent, is the placement of the controlling adverse authority in the plaintiff's papers and the corresponding elevation of the plaintiff's section 425.17 framework into the operative record. The defense's silence on the carve-out converted the carve-out into a one-sided argument, and a one-sided argument under Simpson Strong-Tie is one the movant has already lost.

## VII. The Discovery Backdoor: Deem-Admit and Compel as Independent Channels

The plaintiff opened a discovery front on April 23, 2026, with motions to deem admitted under Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.280, subdivision (b), in both CGC-25-631801 and CGC-25-631802, and parallel motions to compel further responses under Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300, subdivision (a), and Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310, subdivision (a). The two channels operate independently. The section 425.16(g) stay addresses propounding and responding obligations going forward; it does not retroactively excuse a default that had accrued before the stay took effect.

The deem-admit mechanism is structured for mandatory action. Section 2033.280(c) provides that the court shall make the deem-admitted order unless the responding party has, before the hearing on the motion, served a proposed response in substantial compliance with section 2033.220(a). The monetary sanction is mandatory. The cure window stays open until the hearing, but it requires service of substantially compliant responses; mere delay does not cure.

The compel mechanism is structured for further responses where responses have been served but are deficient. Sections 2030.300(d) and 2031.310(h) authorize monetary sanctions against the party engaging in misuse of the discovery process. Section 2023.030(a) authorizes monetary sanctions to pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, incurred as a result of that conduct.

The two channels function as an independent pair. A defense response to one motion does not resolve the other. Both sit on the calendar awaiting hearing.

The strategic logic implied by the filing pattern is that the plaintiff opened the discovery front fourteen days after the anti-SLAPP filing, while the stay was in effect, on the theory that the stay does not retroactively cure a prior default. The choice forces the court to decide the temporal question: when did the response deadline expire relative to the filing of the special motion? If the deadline expired before the motion was filed, the default is complete and the deem-admit motion is granted on its mandatory terms. Either way, the motion is on the calendar, and the court's resolution will produce a written ruling.

What this filing accomplishes mechanically, regardless of intent, is the assertion of a discovery channel that the section 425.16(g) stay does not cleanly close, and the placement of a mandatory-sanction motion on a docket where discretionary discovery rulings would otherwise dominate.

## VIII. The Reply Declaration and the Section 1005 Trap: New Matter Inviting Section 128.7

The defense's May 5, 2026 reply declaration in CGC-25-631801, signed by Tyler J. O'Connell, introduced two matters absent from the moving papers filed April 9, 2026: a fee prayer of $10,218 and a recharacterization of paragraph 22F of the First Amended Complaint as the gravamen of the action under Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 396. Both raise structural problems under Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subdivision (b), and Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7, subdivision (b).

Section 1005(b) requires the moving party to give notice of the relief sought in the moving papers, sixteen court days before the hearing. The fee prayer of $10,218 is new relief; it did not appear in the notice of motion or the supporting memorandum. The plaintiff identified this defect in the May 8, 2026 safe-harbor letter served under section 128.7(c)(1):

> Defendant's Reply introduces for the first time a fee prayer of $10,218.00 in connection with the anti-SLAPP motion. This fee prayer was not included in the original moving papers filed April 9, 2026 (seqs 015 to 018). Raising a specific monetary demand for the first time on Reply violates CCP section 1005(b), which requires that the notice of motion state the nature of the order being sought; the opposing party is entitled to notice of the relief sought in the moving papers so that the opposition can address it. The fee prayer raised on Reply deprives Plaintiff of the opportunity to oppose it in the ordinary course.

The recharacterization of paragraph 22F has the same defect on different ground. Baral requires the moving defendant to identify all allegations of protected activity and the claims for relief supported by them; it does not invite a unilateral selection of the most convenient paragraph as the gravamen of the entire action. The recharacterization is a new theory raised on reply with the same notice defect under section 1005(b).

A due-process bar compounds the section 1005(b) defect. New evidence and arguments raised on reply violate the principle stated in Jay v. Mahaffey (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1522, 1537-1538:

> The court explained that permitting new evidence in reply papers effectively deprives the opposing party of the ability to contest that evidence.

The trial court has the duty to disregard the new matter, or to grant a continuance or supplemental briefing.

The combined effect of the section 1005(b) defect and the Jay v. Mahaffey bar is to invite the section 128.7(c)(1) safe-harbor process. The plaintiff served the safe-harbor letter on May 8, 2026. The twenty-one-day window expires May 29, 2026. If the defense does not withdraw or correct the new matter, the sanctions motion is ripe to file.

## IX. Reading the Defense Lawyer's Mind (As Inference, Not Quotation): Four Candidate Explanations

The conduct documented in Sections II through VIII admits four candidate explanations. Each is evaluated against documentary signals. None is asserted as fact. The framework is the four-hypothesis inference structure: claim, signals consistent with, signals inconsistent with, weight.

### IX-A. Ignorance

The hypothesis that counsel did not know of the controlling carve-out and the controlling gravamen authority would be consistent with a brief that proceeds as if those authorities did not exist, which is what the moving papers filed April 9, 2026, in fact do. It would also be consistent with a defense practice that does not regularly handle insurance bad-faith litigation in California and that defaults to the section 425.16, subdivision (e)(2), formulation when characterizing claim-handling communications. Signals against the ignorance hypothesis include that Porter Scott is a California civil litigation firm with a documented anti-SLAPP practice; that the firm has counsel of record on multiple defense motions in both companion cases; and that the operative pleading expressly invokes Insurance Code section 790.03, subdivision (h), and the Fair Claims Settlement Practices Regulations on its face, which would alert any reasonably attentive lawyer to the regulated-commercial-conduct posture of the case. The hypothesis that counsel knew or counsel did not know of the controlling carve-out is consistent with signals that the brief is otherwise competently drafted on its face, and is inconsistent with the breadth of the omission across both cases on identical theories. On balance, the ignorance hypothesis carries the lightest documentary weight, because the firm's professional profile and the face of the pleading both point against it.

### IX-B. Arrogance

The hypothesis that counsel knew of the controlling carve-out and the controlling gravamen authority and elected to file the motion notwithstanding would be consistent with a defense practice that treats the special motion as a bargaining device rather than as a merits motion, and that calculates the cost of an adverse ruling against the value of the discovery stay and the fee-shift threat. All three procedural levers are valuable to a defense even where the motion is unlikely to prevail on the merits. The signals against the arrogance hypothesis include that the defense did not preserve a record of negotiation predicated on the special motion's leverage value, did not engage on the meet-and-confer plane on the anti-SLAPP question, and did not engage section 425.17 in reply even after the plaintiff's opposition raised it. Each of these signals is consistent with the alternative hypothesis that the motion was filed without strategic calibration. The arrogance hypothesis carries moderate documentary weight: the procedural-lever theory fits the timing, but the absence of any leverage-deployment record cuts against the inference.

### IX-C. Honest Error

The hypothesis that counsel honestly misclassified the gravamen of the operative pleading would be consistent with a brief that characterizes claim-handling communications as statements made in connection with anticipated litigation under section 425.16(e)(2), and that proceeds on that characterization without engaging Park's gravamen test. It would also be consistent with a misreading of Baral that permits the movant to designate a single paragraph as the gravamen of the entire action. The signals against the honest-error hypothesis include that the recharacterization of paragraph 22F as the gravamen was raised for the first time in the May 5, 2026 reply, after the plaintiff's opposition had explicitly invoked Park and Baral; an honest error would have prompted a corrective acknowledgment in the reply or an attempt to engage Park's framework on its own terms. The hypothesis is also weakened by the breadth of the omission: an honest misclassification of one cause of action might explain a partial brief, but four omitted controlling authorities across both cases on identical theories is a pattern. Signals consistent with the honest-error hypothesis include that the brief consistently uses the section 425.16(e)(2) formulation and never explicitly disclaims the Park gravamen test. The honest-error hypothesis carries moderate weight, but the documentary record reflects a brief that doubles down on the original classification rather than corrects, which is the opposite of the corrective trajectory an honest error normally produces.

### IX-D. Strategic Delay or Ratification

The hypothesis that the defense filed the motion as a delay-and-leverage device, and that the firm and its insurer-client ratified the approach, would be consistent with the procedural-lever theory in IX-B and with the timing of the synchronized salvos of April 9, April 13, and April 22. On balance, the strategic-delay hypothesis carries the heaviest documentary weight, because the procedural mechanics align with the filing pattern, and because the alternative hypotheses each have inconsistent signals that the strategic-delay hypothesis does not.
