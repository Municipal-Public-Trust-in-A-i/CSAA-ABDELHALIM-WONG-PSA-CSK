A. The Fallacy of Unilateral Judicial Waiver Labels
Respondent's invited-error theory depends upon treating the trial court's characterization of Plaintiff's position as though it were Plaintiff's own knowing stipulation. California law does not permit that transformation.

A trial judge's characterization of a party's position as "waiver" or "acquiescence" is a judicial conclusion, not a binding party stipulation. In People v. Saunders (1993) 5 Cal.4th 580, the California Supreme Court distinguished forfeiture (the failure to make a timely assertion of a right) from waiver, which requires the intentional relinquishment of a known right. (Id. at p. 589.)

California courts have likewise recognized that a valid waiver must be clear, unequivocal, and intentional. (City of Ukiah v. Fones (1966) 64 Cal.2d 104, 107.) Consequently, when the trial court stated, "You've waived that request, sir" (RT 11, p. 7, ll. 16-22), that statement reflected the court's legal conclusion regarding Plaintiff's conduct. It did not transform the court's characterization into an affirmative stipulation by Plaintiff.

The subsequent proceedings demonstrate precisely why the court's characterization cannot be treated as dispositive. Once the court reporter was present, Plaintiff expressly disputed the court's assertion that he had waived the issue, requested that his objections be placed on the record, and sought reconstruction of the omitted proceedings. Those actions are fundamentally inconsistent with an intentional relinquishment of a known right. At a minimum, they establish that whether Plaintiff intentionally waived the instructional issue remains a disputed appellate question to be resolved from the entire record rather than from the trial court's unilateral characterization of an incomplete colloquy.

Accordingly, Respondent cannot satisfy the stringent requirements for waiver simply by repeating the trial court's characterization. The issue on appeal is whether the record demonstrates a clear and intentional relinquishment of the instructional claim. The Day 11 transcript demonstrates the opposite: contemporaneous disagreement over what had occurred, immediate efforts to preserve objections, and an express challenge to the court's own waiver characterization.

B. Off-the-Record Conferences and Rule 8.137 Demonstrate Plaintiff's Effort to Preserve the Record
The Day 11 transcript further demonstrates that critical discussions concerning Jury Questions #1 and #2 occurred outside the presence of the court reporter. When the court later asserted that Plaintiff had acquiesced, Plaintiff immediately objected, explaining that material portions of the discussion had occurred off the record. (RT 11, p. 11, ll. 27-28; p. 12, ll. 8-9.)

Plaintiff expressly invoked California Rules of Court, rule 8.137, requesting that the record be corrected, supplemented, or reconstructed to reflect the objections made during the unreported discussions. (RT 11, p. 11, l. 23 to p. 12, l. 13.)

California law recognizes California Rules of Court, rule 8.137 as the established procedure for reconstructing material portions of an incomplete or unreported record. (People v. Young (2005) 34 Cal.4th 1149, 1170; People v. Gzikowski (1982) 32 Cal.3d 580, 585, fn. 2.) Where an appellant promptly identifies omissions in the reporter's transcript, requests reconstruction under rule 8.137, and explains that material objections occurred during unreported discussions, the reviewing court should not presume from the incomplete record that the appellant intentionally relinquished a known right. Rather, the incomplete record itself becomes part of the preservation analysis, and any claim of waiver must be evaluated in light of the appellant's contemporaneous efforts to reconstruct and correct the record.

Here, the trial court permitted Plaintiff to briefly describe his objections, noted those objections, overruled them, and declined any further reconstruction of the disputed proceedings. (RT 11, p. 12, l. 14 to p. 13, l. 4.) Thus, the record affirmatively demonstrates Plaintiff's effort to preserve both the accuracy of the appellate record and his objections to the court's responses to Jury Questions #1 and #2. Respondent cannot now rely upon the incompleteness of that same record as affirmative proof of a knowing and intentional waiver.

C. Plaintiff's Renewed Objections and Mistrial Motion Are Inconsistent with Any Intentional Waiver
The remainder of the Day 11 proceedings further confirms that Plaintiff consistently attempted to preserve the instructional issue.

Following the record-reconstruction exchange, Plaintiff expressly renewed his objections to the court's handling of Jury Question #1 and renewed his motion for a mistrial. (RT 11, p. 13, ll. 5-21.) The trial court overruled those objections, denied the mistrial motion, and terminated further argument. (RT 11, p. 13, l. 27 to p. 14, l. 9.)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 647, formal exceptions to a ruling are unnecessary; it is sufficient that the objection is brought to the trial court's attention. Likewise, once the trial court has definitively ruled and makes clear that further argument will not alter its decision, a party is not ordinarily required to continue repeating the same objection in order to preserve the issue for appellate review. (People v. Brooks (2017) 3 Cal.5th 1, 63; City of Long Beach v. Farmers & Merchants Bank (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 780, 784-785.)

The procedural sequence therefore reflects a continuous effort to preserve the issue. Plaintiff objected to the court's waiver characterization, invoked rule 8.137 to reconstruct omitted proceedings, renewed his objections to the court's responses to Jury Questions #1 and #2, renewed his motion for mistrial, and continued disputing the court's characterization of his position until the court terminated further argument. Those actions are fundamentally inconsistent with Respondent's assertion that Plaintiff clearly and intentionally relinquished the instructional issue.

Indeed, the transcript demonstrates the opposite. Plaintiff repeatedly sought to ensure that the appellate record accurately reflected his objections before the jury resumed deliberations. His conduct is consistent with preservation, not abandonment.

Accordingly, Respondent's invited-error theory depends not upon an express waiver by Plaintiff, but upon accepting the trial court's disputed characterization of an incomplete record as though it were an undisputed party stipulation. California law does not permit that substitution. Whether Plaintiff intentionally relinquished the instructional issue remains an appellate question to be resolved from the entire record, including Plaintiff's contemporaneous objections, his request for reconstruction under rule 8.137, his renewed mistrial motion, and his repeated efforts to preserve the issue.

The trial court declined to provide the requested legal guidance.

Accordingly, the central question on appeal is not whether Plaintiff employed technically precise legal terminology while objecting to the court's ruling. The dispositive question is whether, after the jury expressly requested instruction on governing statutory duties, the trial court fulfilled its obligation to provide legally correct guidance to assist the jury in resolving the issues before it.
