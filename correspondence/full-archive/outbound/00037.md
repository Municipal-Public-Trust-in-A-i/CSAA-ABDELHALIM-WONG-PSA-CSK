>> must be dismissed. California law is more nuanced than that, and for good
>> reason.
>>
>> Courts preserve the doctrine of extrinsic fraud because the integrity of
>> adjudication depends on more than the opportunity to cross-examine
>> witnesses before a jury. It also depends on the tribunal receiving truthful
>> information when it decides foundational procedural matters. A trial can be
>> corrupted not only by what the jury hears, but by what the court is falsely
>> led to believe when ruling on continuances, evidentiary access,
>> authentication, and trial management. That is why *Aldrich* and *Baltins* matter.
>> They recognize that process corruption can amount to extrinsic fraud when
>> it prevents a fair contest.
>>
>> The defense position would effectively insulate that category of wrong
>> whenever it is carried out through advocacy conduct. But courts have never
>> held that an attorney may preserve a judgment by invoking privilege and
>> anti-SLAPP whenever the alleged fraud occurred through filings or in-court
>> statements. To the contrary, *Rusheen* and *Finton* recognize the
>> special status of fraud upon the court.
>>
>> Nor can the defense avoid the issue by arguing that Plaintiff "did
>> present" some of the challenged evidence. That response mistakes quantity
>> for fairness. A litigant can be allowed to present a distorted, hobbled, or
>> mistimed version of proof after procedural damage has already been done.
>> The equity action alleges exactly that kind of prejudice: that false
>> defense-side representations caused the court to manage the evidence and
>> the trial under false premises, thereby depriving Plaintiff of a fair
>> opportunity to present his case coherently and effectively.

>> Finally, your letter treats the complaint as though it should already

>> assumes properly pleaded facts are true and asks whether those facts state
>> a cognizable claim. (*Blank v. Kirwan*, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318.)
>> Here they plainly do. And if the court were to identify any defect in
>> defendant-specific detail, the longstanding California policy favoring
>> leave to amend would apply. (See *Goodman v. Kennedy* (1976) 18 Cal.3d
>> 335, 349.)

>> Ground-by-Ground Summary

>> For clarity, the objections may be summarized briefly as follows:
>>
>> Your "protected conduct" objection fails because the gravamen of the
>> action is alleged fraud upon the court, not ordinary petitioning, and
>> because the litigation privilege does not categorically bar
>> fraud-on-the-court claims. (*Rusheen*, supra, 37 Cal.4th at p. 1065;
>> *Finton*, supra, 238 Cal.App.4th at p. 213.)
>>
>> Your section 473(b) objection fails because California recognizes an
>> independent action in equity for extrinsic fraud, governed by equitable
>> diligence rather than the six-month statutory cap. (*Kulchar*, supra, 1
>> Cal.3d at pp. 470-472; *Olivera*, supra, 19 Cal.2d at pp. 576-577;
>> *Aldrich*, supra, 170 Cal.App.3d at p. 738.)
>>
>> Your standing objection fails because *Shaolian* concerns direct
>> insurance claims, not fraud-on-the-court claims, and because standing here
>> derives from Plaintiff's injury as the litigant allegedly deprived of a
>> fair hearing. Agency principles and participation allegations supply the
>> basis for naming defense-side entities and actors. (Civ. Code, sec. 2338;
>> *Howard*, supra, 187 Cal.App.4th at pp. 526-528.)
>>
>> Your jurisdiction objection fails because an independent equity action is
>> not an appeal. It is an original proceeding invoking the superior court's
>> equitable power to set aside a judgment procured by extrinsic fraud. (
>> *Kulchar*, supra, 1 Cal.3d at p. 472; Code Civ. Proc., sec. 187.)
>>
>> Your "another action pending" objection fails because a pending appeal is
>> not the same cause of action, between the same parties, for the same relief
>> as an independent equity claim for fraudulent procurement of judgment. (
>> *Olivera*, supra, 19 Cal.2d at pp. 576-577.)
>>
>> Your misjoinder objection fails because the named defendants are
>> connected to the same alleged course of conduct and common factual
>> questions, and because any request for greater differentiation goes to
>> pleading refinement, not dismissal. (Code Civ. Proc., sec. 379.)
>>
>> Your failure-to-state-facts objection fails because the complaint alleges
>> the who, what, when, where, to whom, and how of the challenged
>> misrepresentations and ties them to judicial reliance and prejudice,
>> satisfying *Lazar*, *Beckwith*, and *Khoury*. (*Lazar*, supra, 12
>> Cal.4th at p. 645; *Beckwith*, supra, 205 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1059-1060;
>> *Khoury*, supra, 14 Cal.App.4th at p. 616.)
>>
>> Your uncertainty objection fails because the complaint is intelligible
>> enough for a detailed response and because California strictly construes
>> uncertainty demurrers. (*Khoury*, supra, 14 Cal.App.4th at p. 616.)
