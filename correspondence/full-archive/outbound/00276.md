*1. Usurpation of the Jury's Province*

A court usurps the jury's province when it prevents the jury from
performing its exclusive constitutional function: deciding disputed facts
under the governing law. The constitutional role of the judge is to declare
the law. The constitutional role of the jury is to apply that law to the
evidence. Judge Wong's ruling collapsed those separate functions.

The jury asked for Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a) because it
believed those statutes were necessary to decide Defendant's liability.
Rather than declaring the law and allowing the jury to apply it, the Court
instructed, "You may not consider Vehicle Code Sections 22107 and or 21804
subdivision A, nor will the Court instruct the Jury on those provisions."
By eliminating the legal standard itself, the Court predetermined that the
jury would never decide whether Defendant violated those statutory duties.
The jury could not determine whether Defendant violated duties it was
forbidden to consider. That question constitutionally belonged to the jury.

*2. Structural Constitutional Error*

Although California civil courts generally analyze prejudice under Article
VI, section 13 and *Soule v. General Motors Corp.* rather than applying
automatic reversal for structural error, the nature of this defect is
structural because it affected the framework within which the trial
proceeded rather than a single evidentiary ruling. The Court did not merely
exclude one exhibit or decline one instruction. It deprived the
deliberating jury of the governing legal standard after the jury
specifically requested it. The framework of adjudication depends upon
evidence being evaluated under the applicable law. Here, that framework
collapsed because the governing law was affirmatively and expressly
withheld from the jury after their request.

*3. Denial of a Meaningful Jury Trial*

Article I, section 16 of the California Constitution guarantees more than
the physical presence of jurors. It guarantees that jurors will decide
disputed facts by applying the governing law declared by the court. The
jury expressly requested the Vehicle Code duties governing Defendant's
conduct. Instead of receiving those legal standards, it was instructed that
it could not consider them. A jury that is denied the governing law cannot
constitutionally perform its function. The result is not merely an
imperfect jury trial but the denial of a meaningful jury trial.

*4. Judicial Nullification of the Governing Law*

Although not a formal doctrine, this accurately describes what occurred.
Courts frequently discuss jury nullification, where jurors refuse to follow
the law. Here, the opposite occurred. The jury requested the governing law
and demonstrated its willingness to apply it. The Court refused to provide
that law and instead prohibited the jury from considering it. The
Legislature enacted Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a). The jury
sought to apply those statutes. The Court nullified their application by
forbidding their consideration.

*5. Misallocation of Constitutional Functions and Category Error*

California's constitutional structure assigns distinct responsibilities.
The Legislature enacts statutes. The judiciary declares those statutes as
governing law. The jury determines disputed facts and applies the law to
those facts. Judge Wong's ruling disrupted each of those assignments.

The Court expressly stated that Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a)
"were not part of the evidence" and that the jury was "not permitted to
consider that as evidence...period." That reasoning demonstrates a
fundamental legal category error. Statutes are not evidence. Statutes are
law. Evidence consists of testimony, documents, photographs, and physical
exhibits introduced to prove facts. Statutes establish the legal standards
by which those facts are judged. Whether a California statute was
physically admitted as an exhibit is legally irrelevant to its status as
governing law. By treating statutes as though they were evidentiary
exhibits, the Court confused two entirely separate legal categories and
used that confusion as the basis for withholding the governing law. The
constitutional allocation of authority was fundamentally altered.

*6. Failure to Declare the Governing Law / Judicial Abdication*

One of the trial judge's most fundamental responsibilities is to declare
the law applicable to the issues presented by the pleadings and evidence.
That responsibility becomes especially important when a deliberating jury
asks a direct legal question. The jury specifically identified Vehicle Code
sections 22107 and 21804(a) by section number and requested instruction
concerning those statutes.

Code of Civil Procedure section 614 requires the court to provide
appropriate legal guidance when a deliberating jury requests clarification
concerning a point of law. The jury's request triggered that mandatory
judicial duty. Rather than declaring the applicable law, the Court
expressly stated that it would not instruct the jury on those provisions
and imposed a categorical prohibition against considering them. That
refusal was not an exercise of discretion concerning instructional wording.
It was an abdication of the mandatory judicial responsibility imposed by
California law.

*7. Denial of the Rule of Law*

The rule of law requires that legal disputes be decided according to
governing legal standards rather than judicial preference or omission. The
Legislature enacted Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a) as mandatory
standards governing turning movements and right-of-way. Those statutes
