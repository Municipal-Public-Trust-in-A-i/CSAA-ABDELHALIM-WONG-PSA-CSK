*I. Issue Statement and Structural Overview*
The central question presented on appeal is whether the trial court
committed reversible instructional and constitutional error when, after the
deliberating jury specifically asked whether it should consider Vehicle
Code sections 22107 and 21804(a) and requested to be instructed on those
provisions, the court categorically prohibited the jury from considering
those statutes, refused to instruct on them, and justified that prohibition
by declaring that enacted statutes were non-evidence. Plaintiff Franciscus
Dylan Rosario explicitly pleaded turning and yielding duties under Vehicle
Code sections 22107 and 21804(a), but the trial court omitted those
specific statutory duties from the initial charge. During deliberations,
Defense Exhibit 500, which was the plaintiff's complaint designated as an
exhibit by the defense, placed those exact statute numbers before the jury.
Confronting that instructional gap, the deliberating jury submitted Jury
Question #1, asking for the statutes by number and requesting judicial
instruction. In response, the trial court issued a dual prohibition:
ordering that the jury may not consider those statutes and declaring that
the court would not instruct upon them. That categorical ban is the core
legal error under review, and Respondent's waiver theories represent an
improper attempt to prevent judicial review of that trial court error.

To ensure a systematic destruction of Respondent's arguments, this reply
brief is organized into two primary procedural and substantive spines,
followed by targeted responses to Respondent's tertiary affirmative
defenses. Argument I demonstrates that the trial court's response to Jury
Question #1 constituted a constitutional and statutory misallocation of
adjudicative functions under Code of Civil Procedure section 614, Evidence
Code section 310, California Constitution article I, sections 7 and 16, and
the Fourteenth Amendment. Argument II systematically dismantles
Respondent's invited-error and waiver arguments, establishing that
Plaintiff never invited the dual instructional ban and that an incomplete
off-record colloquy cannot transform a dispute over juror self-help into
consent to a categorical ban on governing law. Argument III dismantles

Argument II dismantles the procedural gate Respondent attempts to erect.


*II. Argument I: The Categorical Prohibition as Constitutional
Misallocation of Adjudicative Functions (Spine A)*

*A1. The Record Event and the Dual Prohibition*The reversible event on
appeal is the content of the trial court's answer to the deliberating jury,
rather than a charge-conference quarrel over pre-deliberation form numbers.
During deliberations, the jury submitted Jury Question #1, asking whether,
in deciding the question of negligence, it should consider the provisions
of Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a), and whether it could be
instructed on those provisions. Plaintiff claimed an unsafe right turn by
Respondent that struck Plaintiff in a crosswalk, while Respondent disputed
impact and path. Those competing accounts made Vehicle Code section 22107,
governing roadway turns, and section 21804(a), governing yielding to
approaching traffic, the concrete statutory yardstick for ordinary care.
Defense Exhibit 500 placed those statute numbers directly before the jury,
revealing the instructional gap created when the initial charge omitted
those pleaded duties.

The trial court answered Jury Question #1 with a dual categorical
prohibition that withdrew the governing law from the jury. On the record at
Reporter's Transcript Volume 11, page 7, lines 24 to 27, the court stated:
"You may not consider Vehicle Code Sections 22107 and or 21804 subdivision
A, nor will the Court instruct the Jury on those provisions." The court
then supplied its rationale at page 8, lines 5 to 9, asserting that neither
section was part of the evidence and that jurors were "not permitted to
consider that as evidence in the case, period." This ruling did not select
between competing correct formulations of law, nor did it declare the
statutes legally inapplicable after reasoned analysis. It forbade
consideration, refused instruction, and justified the ban by misclassifying
enacted statutes as unadmitted evidence.


*A2. The Three Constitutional Movements: Refusal, Misclassification, and
Foreclosure*The trial court's ruling contains three distinct constitutional
movements that systematically dismantled the adjudicative structure. The
first movement, Refusal, occurred when the court informed the jury that it
could not consider Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a) and that the
court would not instruct on them. Due process under California Constitution
article I, section 7 and the Fourteenth Amendment requires a meaningful
hearing, which exists only when the factfinder applies correct legal rules
of decision. Article I, section 16 preserves the fundamental right to a
jury trial, requiring disputes to be resolved by jurors applying
court-declared legal standards. Code of Civil Procedure section 614
implements that constitutional guarantee during deliberations by mandating
that required legal information must be given when requested. The court's
categorical refusal directly violated that statutory and constitutional
mandate.

The second movement, Misclassification, occurred when the court asserted
that the statutes were not part of the evidence and could not be considered
as evidence. Evidence Code section 310, subdivision (a), explicitly assigns
all questions of law, including the construction and applicability of
statutes, exclusively to the court. Treating an enacted statute as
unadmitted evidence collapses the constitutional boundary between law and
fact. Whether a California statute was physically marked as an exhibit is
legally irrelevant to its status as governing law. The third movement,
Foreclosure, occurred when the court added the word "period," closing the
door on the governing legal framework. Together, these three movements
represent an abandonment of the judicial duty to declare law and a
foreclosure of the jury's duty to apply law.


*A3. Violation of Plaintiff's Adjudicative Right to Due Process and a
Law-Governed Jury Trial*A civil plaintiff who invokes the judicial system
is constitutionally entitled to adjudication: a decision of their claim on
its legal merits under correct legal standards. Under the Fourteenth
Amendment and California Constitution article I, section 7, a cause of
action is a protected property interest that the State cannot extinguish
through procedurally unfair processes. Due process does not guarantee a
favorable verdict, but it guarantees a fair opportunity to have the jury
weigh evidence against the statutory standards of care that define the
claim. When a deliberating jury identifies missing statutory duties and
requests instruction, a judicial order banning consideration of those
duties deprives the plaintiff of the legal foundation of their claim.

Furthermore, California Constitution article I, section 16 guarantees
Plaintiff the right to a jury trial where factual disputes are resolved
under court-declared law. Banning consideration of governing statutes after
the jury requests them leaves jurors in the box but strips the trial of its
legal content. The trial court created an instructional gap by omitting the
statutory duties from the initial charge, and then treated that very gap as
a justification for refusing to instruct when the jury asked for guidance.
Due process does not permit the court to create a legal vacuum and then
treat that vacuum as a permanent barrier to declaring the law. This
structural defect altered the framework within which the trial proceeded,
resulting in a miscarriage of justice under California Constitution article
VI, section 13.


*A4. The Statutory Duty Stack: CCP Section 614, Evidence Code Section 310,
and Vehicle Code Duties*California's statutory framework establishes an
mandatory duty stack that governs deliberating jury inquiries. Under Code
of Civil Procedure section 614, when deliberating jurors express confusion
or request information on a point of law, the trial court has an
affirmative, non-delegable duty to provide clear and legally accurate
guidance. As established in People v. Beardslee and Bartosh v. Banning, the
court must attempt to clear up instructional confusion expressed by the
jury regarding applicable legal principles. Jury Question #1 explicitly
named Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a) and requested instruction,

Evidence Code section 310, subdivision (a), reinforces that stack by

prohibiting consideration and refusing instruction before holding the
on-record colloquy.

Fifth, the parties held an on-record discussion where Plaintiff expressed
concern about jurors accessing external code sections not included in the
charge. Sixth, the trial court unilaterally declared that Plaintiff had
waived the instruction and acquiesced in the ruling. Seventh, Plaintiff
actively contested those labels, invoked California Rules of Court, rule
8.137, sought record reconstruction, and renewed objections and mistrial
motions. This chronological sequence defeats invited error as a matter of
law. A party cannot invite or induce a judicial ruling that the trial court
had already formulated, delivered, and implemented prior to the alleged
invitation.


*B3. Distinguishing Invited Error from Defensive Reactions to an
Instructional Lacuna*Under settled California law, the doctrine of invited
error applies only where a party's affirmative conduct induces the specific
ruling challenged on appeal. As held in Norgart v. Upjohn Co. and People v.
Cooper, the record must demonstrate a clear, deliberate tactical choice
concerning the challenged ruling itself. Furthermore, Huffman v. Interstate
Brands Corp. establishes that mere acquiescence or failure to object is
insufficient to invoke invited error where counsel did not request the
erroneous ruling and no deliberate tactical choice appears on the record.

Here, Plaintiff made no deliberate tactical choice to exclude Vehicle Code
sections 22107 and 21804(a) from the legal framework. Plaintiff's comments
during Reporter's Transcript Volume 11 were defensive reactions to an
existing instructional gap and an effort to address potential juror
misconduct. Defensive handling of a pre-existing instructional lacuna does
not constitute affirmative inducement under Huffman or Norgart. Object
mismatch reinforces this distinction: agreement to stop unsupervised juror
research is logically distinct from consenting to a judicial ban on
court-declared instructions.


*B4. Rebutting Judicial Waiver Labels and Establishing Ongoing
Preservation*Respondent
relies heavily on the trial court's unilateral statements at Reporter's
Transcript Volume 11, page 7, lines 16 to 22 ("You've waived that request,
sir") and page 11, lines 14 to 19 (asserting "acquiescence"). However,
judicial labels do not substitute for the appellate inquiry into whether a
party intentionally relinquished a known right. Under People v. Saunders
and City of Ukiah v. Fones, a valid waiver requires clear, unequivocal, and
intentional relinquishment of the specific right at issue. The trial
court's subjective characterization of Plaintiff's position is merely
evidence of the court's view, not proof of intentional relinquishment by
Plaintiff.

The record demonstrates continuous preservation rather than abandonment.
Plaintiff explicitly contested the court's waiver characterization,
requested to place objections on the record regarding Jury Questions #1 and
#2, invoked California Rules of Court, rule 8.137, and formally renewed
motions for mistrial. Under Code of Civil Procedure section 647, formal
exceptions are unnecessary once an objection has been brought to the
court's attention. As confirmed in People v. Brooks and City of Long Beach
v. Farmers & Merchants Bank, once a court has ruled and cut off argument,
continuous repetition is not required to preserve an issue for appeal.


*B5. The Off-Record Colloquy and CRC Rule 8.137 Record Reconstruction*Critical
discussions regarding Jury Questions #1 and #2 occurred off the record
without a court reporter present. When the trial court later asserted on
the record that Plaintiff had acquiesced, Plaintiff immediately objected
that material discussions occurred off the record and invoked California
Rules of Court, rule 8.137 to reconstruct the proceedings. Rule 8.137
provides a specific procedural mechanism to settle the record when
unreported proceedings are material to appellate review.

Under People v. Young and People v. Gzikowski, an appellate court must
exercise extreme caution when a respondent relies on an incomplete or
silent record. Respondent cannot affirmatively use a silent or incomplete
transcript to prove intentional waiver when the unreported proceedings
themselves were disputed and subject to a timely reconstruction request.
The trial court overruled Plaintiff's reconstruction request and terminated
argument. Respondent cannot transform that procedural truncation into
affirmative proof of a knowing waiver.


*B6. The "Even-If" Analysis: Object Mismatch Defeats Waiver Even Under
Hostile Paraphrases*Even if Respondent's paraphrase of Reporter's
Transcript Volume 11 is fully credited arguendo, Respondent's waiver
defense still fails under a mandatory "even-if" legal analysis. Assuming
arguendo that Plaintiff agreed that Jury Question #1 should be answered by
stating the statutes could not be considered because they were not in the
instructions, that agreement addressed managing uninstructed material
during deliberations. It did not constitute consent to a judicial rule that
those statutes could never be given as legal instructions.

This object mismatch defeats estoppel under four distinct principles.
First, Code of Civil Procedure section 614 imposes a non-delegable duty to
instruct on points of law when requested by deliberating jurors, a duty
that cannot be waived into silence by agreement regarding uninstructed
material. Second, the object of Plaintiff's agreement (preventing juror
self-help) mismatches the object of the challenged ruling (forbidding
judicial instruction). Third, Plaintiff's pleadings and proposed
instructions demonstrate a continuous theory seeking statutory protection,
making Respondent's "statutes out forever" interpretation incoherent.
Fourth, under Norgart and Cooper, agreement with a procedural response to
uninstructed text does not supply the deliberate tactical inducement
required for invited error.


*B7. Re-Establishing the Primary Appellate Question and Handoff to
Merits*Respondent's
waiver arguments represent an attempt to divert this Court's attention away
from the trial court's failure to fulfill its mandatory duty under Code of
Civil Procedure section 614. The primary question on appeal is not whether
Plaintiff articulated objections with technical perfection during rapid,
unrecorded sidebar discussions. The dispositive question is whether, after
a deliberating jury explicitly named Vehicle Code sections 22107 and
21804(a) and asked for legal instruction, the trial court fulfilled its
obligation to declare the law.

Plaintiff preserved the issue by objecting to the court's characterization,
seeking record reconstruction under rule 8.137, and renewing mistrial
motions until the court cut off argument. Having dismantled Respondent's
invited-error gate, the Court must return to the central substantive error:
whether the trial court erred in banning consideration of sections 22107
and 21804(a) and refusing instruction after Jury Question #1.

*IV. Argument III: Dismantling Respondent's Tertiary Affirmative Defenses
(ROB Nests)*


*IV-A. Rebutting the Pretrial Form Trap (CACI 707 vs. CACI 705)*Respondent
contends that Plaintiff waived appellate review of any turning instruction
because Plaintiff's pretrial proposed instruction list cited CACI 707
(Speed Limit) rather than CACI 705 (Turning). This argument represents a
hyper-technical form trap that elevates an administrative CACI number typo
over explicit textual substance. Respondent's brief acknowledges that
beside the CACI 707 entry, Plaintiff's proposed instruction list contained
the explicit textual description: "Duty of Driver to Pedestrians: Explicit
duty of drivers to yield to pedestrians within marked crosswalks." That
textual description provided clear, unambiguous notice of Plaintiff's legal
theory.

Under Civil Code section 3528, the law respects substance rather than form.
Pretrial form-number disputes governed by Agarwal, Finn, Metcalf, and
Gregory do not control once a deliberating jury submits a specific inquiry
under Code of Civil Procedure section 614. Even assuming arguendo that
Plaintiff typed CACI 707 instead of 705 on a pretrial list, the
deliberating jury subsequently named Vehicle Code section 22107 directly
and requested instruction. Jury Question #1 triggered a fresh, independent
judicial duty under section 614 that completely bypassed pretrial
form-number typos.


*IV-B. Rebutting the Omitted Negligence Per Se Request Defense*Respondent
asserts that Plaintiff cannot claim error regarding Vehicle Code section
22107 because Plaintiff never requested a formal negligence per se
instruction. This defense argument attempts to rewrite Plaintiff's appeal
into a missing presumption argument under CACI 418. Negligence per se is a
specific evidentiary mechanism that establishes a rebuttable presumption of
negligence upon proof of a statutory violation. Plaintiff's appeal does not
depend on whether a formal CACI 418 presumption instruction was requested.

Applying the weighing bar reveals the flaw in Respondent's argument: the
absence of a formal negligence per se request does not authorize a trial
court to forbid a deliberating jury from considering governing statutory
duties. Vehicle Code section 22107 defines the statutory standard of care
for turning maneuvers. The trial court was obligated under Evidence Code
section 310 and Code of Civil Procedure section 614 to instruct on that
statutory standard when requested by the jury, regardless of whether
Plaintiff sought a statutory presumption instruction.


*IV-C. Rebutting the Category-Error Denial and Circular Rationale*Respondent
denies that the trial court barred Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a)
on the ground that statutes were "not evidence," attempting to distance the
court from its own words at Reporter's Transcript Volume 11, page 8, lines
5 to 9. Respondent's denial fails because the trial court's rationale is
recorded on the transcript page: the court explicitly declared that the
statutes were not part of the evidence and that jurors were not permitted
to consider them as evidence. That rationale represents a direct,
unvarnished category error that treats legislative commands as unadmitted
exhibits.

Even if Respondent's alternative interpretation is accepted, that the court
barred the statutes because they were "not part of the instructions," the
ruling remains fatal. Answering a jury's request for statutory instructions
by stating that the statutes cannot be considered because they are not in
the instructions is completely circular. That circularity converts the
court's initial omission into a permanent barrier against supplying the law
under section 614. Defense Exhibit 500 confirms that the statutes were
properly before the jury's eyes, and sustaining midtrial evidentiary
objections to a witness reading code sections did not authorize banning
those statutes when the jury asked for them as law.


*IV-D1. Rebutting the Alleged Equivalence of General Negligence (CACI
401)*Respondent
claims that omitting CACI 705 was harmless because the jury received CACI
401 (General Negligence), which contains an equivalent reasonable-care
standard. This argument relies on a false equivalence between abstract
ordinary care and specific statutory turning duties. CACI 401 provides a
general definition of negligence, whereas Vehicle Code section 22107
establishes a mandatory statutory duty: a driver shall not turn a vehicle
from a direct course unless and until such movement can be made with
reasonable safety.

Sesler v. Ghumman directly refutes Respondent's equivalence theory. Sesler
establishes that general reasonable-care instructions do not cure a trial
court's refusal to provide specific statutory turning and right-of-way law
when requested by a deliberating jury. While Sesler factually involved a
multi-lane left turn, its legal principle applies with equal force to right
turns: when a deliberating jury asks a specific question about turning
duties, re-reading general instructions or relying on abstract care
standards constitutes reversible error.


*IV-D2. Rebutting the Claimed Harmlessness Based on Disputed Trial
Evidence*Respondent
argues that any instructional error was harmless under Soule v. General
Motors Corp. because defense testimony established low speed, no felt
impact, no vehicle damage, and wrong-way scooter operation by Plaintiff.
Respondent's argument misapplies the Soule prejudice standard. Soule does
not require an appellant to prove that a favorable verdict was guaranteed;
it requires showing a reasonable probability that the error affected the
verdict.

Under Soule factor (4), indications from the jury itself that it was misled
provide the most compelling evidence of prejudice. The deliberating jury's
explicit request for Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a) demonstrates
that the jury considered those statutory duties material to its liability
determination. Conflicting trial evidence under Soule factor (1) confirms
prejudice rather than erasing it. When liability evidence is sharply
contested, banning the specific statutory standards of care distorts the
weighing process, making a different outcome reasonably probable had
correct instructions been given.


*V. Conclusion and Relief Requested*First, during deliberations in this
motor-vehicle-versus-scooter action, the jury submitted Jury Question #1
explicitly asking whether it should consider Vehicle Code sections 22107
and 21804(a) and whether it could be instructed on those provisions.
Second, the trial court issued a dual prohibition forbidding the jury from
considering those statutes, refusing to instruct on them, and justifying
the ban by asserting that enacted statutes were non-evidence at Reporter's
Transcript Volume 11, page 7, lines 24 to 27 and page 8, lines 5 to 9.
Third, as established in Argument I, that ruling constituted a
constitutional and statutory misallocation of adjudicative functions under
Code of Civil Procedure section 614, Evidence Code section 310, California
Constitution article I, sections 7 and 16, and the Fourteenth Amendment.
Fourth, as established in Argument II, Respondent's invited-error and
waiver arguments fail because Code of Civil Procedure section 614 duties
cannot be waived into silence, agreement regarding uninstructed material
mismatches a permanent ban on court-declared law, Defense Exhibit 500
refutes the claim that the statutes were foreign to the trial, and ongoing
preservation is established on the record. Fifth, under California
Constitution article VI, section 13 and Soule v. General Motors Corp.
factor (4), the jury's explicit request for the withheld statutes
establishes that a different result was reasonably probable, requiring
reversal of the defense judgment.

Respondent's Opening Brief is a failed attempt to rewrite trial history by
characterizing this appeal as a waived turning instruction concocted after
judgment. The true record demonstrates the opposite: Respondent has
attempted to transform a mid-deliberation categorical prohibition on
statutory law into a CACI-label and colloquy waiver story. Respondent's
leaf arguments rewrite a statutory jury inquiry as a form typo, rewrite
defensive reactions to an instructional gap as invited error, rewrite a
structural ban on law as ordinary packaging, and rewrite the jury's
explicit statutory question as harmless abstraction.

Jury Question #1 and the trial court's dual answer place the fundamental
instructional error in the record with absolute clarity. On invited error,
non-delegable judicial duties and mismatched consent objects leave the
court's dual prohibition fully reviewable on appeal. The judgment of the
trial court should be reversed because the deliberating jury explicitly
asked for governing law, and the trial court forbade that law.
