# The Architecture of a Three-Front Case: How a Plaintiff Turned Evidence, Contradiction, and Legal Duty into Pressure the Defense Cannot Escape

## 1. The Story in One Paragraph

A driver struck a pedestrian in a San Francisco crosswalk. The driver's insurance company — CSAA — sent a letter a few weeks later saying it had "concluded" its investigation after a "careful review," and denied the claim. But CSAA had not pulled the 911 audio, had not pulled the officers' body-worn camera footage, and had not pulled the computer-aided-dispatch record that was referenced by number on the very police report it already held. Years later, in a civil trial against the driver, the same audio, the same body-camera footage, and the same records surfaced — and defense counsel admitted, on the record, that prior counsel had "provided" those materials all along. At the same time, the trial court refused to tell the deliberating jury the traffic statutes that defined the driver's duty, even though the jury asked for those statutes by number, and even though the original complaint (admitted as a defense exhibit) specifically pleaded them. The jury came back 9–3 on liability — the bare minimum under California law. From that one factual record, three separate legal matters now run in parallel: an appeal, an equitable fraud action, and a fraud/unfair-competition case against the insurer. They look like three different fights. They are actually one fight expressed in three different courtrooms, using one shared set of facts as the spine. The plaintiff did not "get lucky" three times. He built a structure where the defense's best argument in any one forum destroys its best argument in the others.

## 2. The Fundamentals: What a Civil Case Actually Is

To understand why this situation is as uncomfortable for the defense as it is, it helps to remember what a civil lawsuit is for. A civil lawsuit is not a hunt for "the truth" in the abstract. It is a structured process in which one side makes specific, legally recognized accusations (called *causes of action*), and the other side must either admit them, deny them, or offer a legal reason the accusations do not matter. Each cause of action has *elements* — a short list of things the plaintiff must prove to win on that theory. If the plaintiff proves every element, the plaintiff wins on that count, and the law imposes a consequence (money, an order to stop doing something, an order undoing a prior judgment).

That structure is the key to everything that follows. Because elements are fixed in advance by statute and case law, both sides can see the board before the game starts. Both sides know which facts matter. Both sides know which facts, if proven, close off which defenses. A well-built case is one where the plaintiff has *pre-positioned* the facts so that each element has more than one path to "proven," and each defense has more than one way to fail.

That is what is happening here. The plaintiff is not asking a jury to feel a certain way. He is showing the court a set of documents, transcripts, and recordings, and pointing out — element by element — that each piece of the record independently satisfies a legal requirement. The jury's job is not to trust the plaintiff's narrative. The jury's job is to read the record. The record does the work.

## 3. The Factual Spine: Five Pieces of Evidence That Matter Everywhere

Everything else in this document sits on top of five facts. If you remember only these five, the rest of the reasoning follows naturally.

**First — the 911 audio.** Shortly after the collision, the driver, who is the person CSAA insured, called 911. In that call, he said, in his own voice, that he had "just hit him … barely hitting anything." In evidence law, that is what is called a *party admission*. A party admission is a statement made by someone who is a party to the case, against their own interest, and it comes into evidence almost automatically under California Evidence Code § 1220. Once the driver confirmed at trial, under oath, "Yes. That is my voice," the audio is no longer challengeable on authentication grounds. It is simply admissible evidence of what the driver believed and said at the moment of the collision.

**Second — the body-worn camera footage and the CAD (computer-aided-dispatch) reference.** When officers respond to a crash in San Francisco, they generate three categories of record: the police report itself, the body-worn camera video, and a CAD entry (logged by an identifying number). The police report that CSAA held referenced the CAD number right on its face. That reference is a pointer — a signpost saying, "There is more in this system; pull it to complete your picture." The body-worn camera footage is the visual analog of the audio: it shows the scene, the positions of the vehicles and the pedestrian, and what was said in the first minutes.

**Third — the Vehicle Code turning-duty statutes.** California Vehicle Code §§ 22107 and 21804(a) define what a driver must do before turning or crossing a lane of traffic: signal, yield, make sure the movement can be made safely. VC § 360 provides the definition of "highway" that those statutes rely on. These statutes are the *rules of the road* that decide, as a matter of legal duty, whether a driver was behaving lawfully at the moment of a collision. They are not optional. They are, in the language of the appeal, "law arising in the case."

**Fourth — the Day-7 admission, April 16, 2025.** During the underlying trial, defense counsel stated, on the record, in a certified court-reporter transcript, that the 911 calls, the body-worn camera footage, and the unredacted documents "were provided by Dolan. He has had them for whatever, sure." Dolan Law Firm was prior counsel. That single sentence is doing three jobs at once: it says the defense had those materials; it says the defense has had them for a long time; and it says those materials were transferred through the defense's own file chain. None of that can be un-said. The transcript is certified. It is the permanent record of what was said in open court.

**Fifth — CSAA's funding and direction of the defense.** The insurance company paid for the defense of its insured driver in the underlying personal-injury case. That is how liability insurance works — the insurer retains and funds counsel to defend its insured, and in doing so it directs and supervises the defense. The billing records, retention letters, and claim file all document this relationship. CSAA cannot deny that it paid for the defense, because the records of its own payments exist.

Those five facts are the **spine**. Every legal theory in every one of the three cases traces back to one or more of them.

## 4. Three Forums, One Spine: Why There Are Three Cases at All

A single set of wrongful acts can produce more than one kind of legal injury. When that happens, the law provides more than one remedy, and the injured person is not required to pick one. The plaintiff here is using three separate legal vehicles because each one targets a different wrong and seeks a different remedy. Each is its own courtroom, its own judge, its own schedule. But each is anchored in the same record.

**The appeal (A173827)** is before the First District Court of Appeal. It is about the trial court's handling of the jury — specifically, whether the trial judge was required to tell the deliberating jury what the Vehicle Code turning-duty statutes actually said, and whether the refusal to do so was prejudicial under California Code of Civil Procedure § 614 and California Evidence Code § 451(a). The appeal does not produce money directly. What it produces is the possibility of reversal and a new trial under a clean record — which is itself a massive event, because at retrial the 911 audio comes in as an admitted party admission, and the 9–3 liability posture collapses.

**The 801 case (CGC-25-631801)** is an equity action in San Francisco Superior Court. Its spine is what the law calls an "independent action in equity to vacate a judgment for extrinsic fraud" — in plain language, a separate lawsuit asking a court to undo an earlier judgment because the earlier judgment was procured by fraud on the court, not just by the usual back-and-forth of litigation. Its defendants include the driver, CSAA, the law firms, and individual attorneys. The remedies it seeks are equitable (vacating the underlying judgment, fraud findings), not primarily monetary. But because certain statutes such as California Business & Professions Code § 6128(a) — the attorney-deceit statute — allow Civ. Code § 3294 punitive damages and Prentice fee-shifting, and because California Civil Code § 2338 makes a principal (here, the insurer) liable for the wrongful acts of its agents (here, its retained counsel), there is real money on the table as well.

**The 802 case (CGC-25-631802)** is the money case. It is against CSAA and CSAA alone. It pleads three causes of action: intentional fraud by omission, fraud by concealment or half-truth, and an unfair-competition claim under California Business & Professions Code § 17200. Its factual pivot is the February 25, 2021 denial letter — the letter that told the claimant the investigation was "concluded" after "careful review," which CSAA issued before it had obtained the 911, the body-worn camera, or the CAD record. That is where the phrase *completed investigation versus no knowledge* comes in: you cannot honestly say you concluded a careful review without pulling the materials the police report pointed you to. That contradiction is the 802's entire reason for existing.

Three cases. Three forums. Three remedy profiles. One spine.

## 5. The Core Legal Theory: "Completed Investigation" Versus "No Knowledge" as a Self-Defeating Pair

This is the hinge of the plaintiff's position, and it is the hinge the rest of this narrative turns on. Stated simply, the defense has said two things in the course of this litigation that cannot both be true at the same time, and the plaintiff has arranged the record so that each one is *proven from the defense's own mouth*.

The first statement, made by CSAA in its February 25, 2021 denial letter: the investigation is **concluded** after **careful review**. That is not a throwaway phrase. Under California's Fair Claims Settlement Practices Regulations (Title 10, California Code of Regulations §§ 2695.4 and 2695.7), an insurer must conduct a "thorough, fair, objective investigation." When an insurer says it has "concluded" its investigation, it is representing that it has done that work. When it represents that in writing to a claimant, that representation is what the claimant is entitled to rely on in deciding what to do next. And when the representation is false — when the investigation was *not* thorough because obvious next steps (pull the 911, pull the body-worn camera, pull the CAD) were not taken — the insurer has not just been careless. It has made a misrepresentation about the very fact the claimant was asking about.

The second statement, made by defense counsel at trial in April 2025, is: we didn't know; this is news to me; it wasn't produced. That "no knowledge" framing appeared in the pre-trial motions in limine in January 2025 and again in arguments at the February 18, 2025 hearing. It was the defense's shield against questions about what the defense had and when.

Those two statements, taken together, say this: *we completed the investigation* (in 2021) and *we did not know about the very materials that a completed investigation would have produced* (in 2025). That is a logical impossibility. Either the 2021 investigation was incomplete, which means the denial letter was a misrepresentation. Or the 2021 investigation was complete, which means the "no knowledge" claim in 2025 was a misrepresentation. The defense cannot occupy both positions at once.

This is where the *lever* appears. The plaintiff does not need to guess which story the defense will ultimately pick. The plaintiff has *engineered* the record so that whichever door the defense walks through, the plaintiff wins on an element somewhere. In 802, if the defense says the investigation was complete, the plaintiff's fraud and Title-10-regulations claims are proven because the denial letter sits next to a record showing the materials were never pulled. In 801, if the defense says it did not know about the materials (or argues they were not in the defense's hands), the Day-7 certified admission destroys that position. And in the underlying appellate posture, any defense argument that the materials were "not in the case" or "not in evidence" runs directly into the fact that defense counsel argued from the complaint itself — admitted as Defense Exhibit 500 — which pleaded the very statutes the trial court refused to give the jury.

This is not trickery. It is the natural consequence of having made contradictory representations to two different audiences (a claimant in 2021, a trial court in 2025) about the same underlying set of facts. The plaintiff has simply made sure that both representations are on paper, in the court's own records, where they cannot be walked back.

## 6. The Weave: Why the Three Cases Reinforce Each Other Rather Than Compete

Most defendants facing multiple lawsuits can use the multiplicity as a tactical asset. They can say different things in different rooms, because no one is in every room at once. That strategy depends on the rooms being insulated from each other.

The plaintiff here has eliminated that insulation, and he has done it using three legal doctrines that every law student learns in the first year.

**Judicial notice.** California Evidence Code § 451(a) requires a court, on request, to take judicial notice of California statutes. That means any court, in any of the three matters, must recognize California Vehicle Code §§ 22107, 21804(a), and 360 as "the law." A defendant cannot argue in one case that a statute applies and in another that it does not. The statutes exist in the same form everywhere.

**Judicial estoppel.** California recognizes a doctrine that prevents a party from taking one position in one legal proceeding and then taking the opposite position in another proceeding once the first position has served its tactical purpose. The idea is ancient: courts are not tools for opportunism, and a party who wants the benefit of saying X in one courtroom cannot later say not-X in a different courtroom when X becomes inconvenient.

**Collateral estoppel (issue preclusion).** When a court actually decides a factual or legal issue, and the losing party had a full and fair chance to litigate it, the decision is binding on that party in later cases involving the same issue. If the 801 court finds, as part of its equitable-vacatur analysis, that CSAA ratified its retained counsel's misrepresentations, that finding can be brought into 802 through judicial notice and used as part of the proof of CSAA's institutional state of mind.

Those three doctrines, together, turn the three cases into a *weave*. Each case deposits findings and admissions that reappear in the others. The plaintiff does not need to relitigate the same facts three times. He litigates them once, cleanly, using the forum that is best suited to each, and the results propagate.

The weave operates at four specific pressure points.

**Point one — the 911 audio.** In the appeal, the audio is the heart of the prejudice argument: a party admission of fault was concealed from a jury that split 9–3. In the 801, the audio is the meritorious-case element of the extrinsic-fraud claim: the concealed evidence was not trivial; it was a self-incriminating statement by the defendant in his own voice. In the 802, the audio is the "liability reasonably clear" trigger under California Insurance Code § 790.03(h)(5): a reasonable adjuster hearing a 911 call in which the insured says he "just hit him" would not deny the claim after a single month of review. The same audio does three different pieces of legal work in three different cases. The defense cannot say it is material in one case and immaterial in another. Whatever the defense says about the audio in any one forum becomes evidence in the other two.

**Point two — the body-worn camera and the CAD reference.** This is the document-intensive overlap. The camera footage was not one file in one place. It existed in the Dolan Law Firm's production index, in successor counsel's transmittal log, in current counsel's intake records, and in the San Francisco Police Department's CAD system. That is four separate paper trails, each maintained by a different custodian, each generated in the ordinary course of business. If the defense tries to deny receipt or possession of the footage, those four trails have to all line up with the denial. They do not. The Day-7 admission confirmed that Dolan "provided" the materials, which closes the chain: the materials existed, were collected, were passed along, and eventually reached the defense in the underlying trial. In the 802 context, the footage matters in a different way: CSAA's failure to request it in 2021, despite holding the police report that named the CAD number, is the exact pattern that Title 10's regulations prohibit.

**Point three — the Vehicle Code turning-duty statutes.** This is the appellate spine, and it bleeds into the other two cases in a subtler way. On appeal, the refusal to instruct the jury on Vehicle Code §§ 22107 and 21804(a) is the reversible error itself. In the 801 case, those statutes are the *yardstick* by which the plaintiff measures whether the concealed evidence was "meritorious" — because under the governing California Supreme Court case on extrinsic fraud (the *Kulchar* line), a plaintiff has to show that the concealed evidence would have produced a different outcome *under applicable law*. The applicable law here is the Vehicle Code turning-duty framework. In the 802 case, those same statutes are part of what an insurance adjuster is expected to know when evaluating a pedestrian-versus-vehicle claim. The statutes are everywhere, and they say the same thing everywhere. The defense cannot disown them in one room without making them harder to disown in the others.

**Point four — the Day-7 "provided by Dolan" admission.** This is the single most important sentence in the entire case architecture. It is four words long in its operative portion — "provided by Dolan" — and it carries four distinct structural consequences. It proves, on a certified transcript, that the defense had the materials. It contradicts the "news to me" framing used earlier in the litigation. It establishes that the materials existed and were accessible from the earliest stages, which matters for the 802 claim that CSAA could have pulled them in 2021. And it forecloses an "innocent mistake" defense, because under California law innocence-of-fraud depends on a defendant not having a meaningful opportunity to know the truth. The defense had the opportunity. The defense had the materials. The record says so.

## 7. The Lever: How Contradiction Becomes Pressure

Having laid out the spine and the weave, we can now describe what the plaintiff actually does with them. This is the mechanism of the case, and it is worth understanding on its own terms.

Think of pressure in a legal case the way an engineer thinks about pressure in a structure. If you want a structure to fail in a predictable way, you identify the load-bearing members and you make sure that any force applied to the structure gets transmitted to one of those members. In a litigation context, the "load-bearing members" are the elements of each cause of action. The "force" is the evidence the plaintiff marshals. A well-built case is one where every piece of evidence is pointed at an element, and where every element has more than one piece of evidence pointed at it, so that even if the defense removes one piece, the element stays proven.

This plaintiff has gone one step further. He has arranged the record so that the defense's *own statements* are among the pieces of evidence pointed at the elements. That is what the "completed investigation versus no knowledge" contradiction accomplishes. The 2021 denial letter is a document CSAA generated itself. The "news to me" / "not produced" framing in the 2025 motions in limine is a statement defense counsel made themselves. The Day-7 transcript is a statement defense counsel made in open court, captured by a certified court reporter. None of these are plaintiff-generated documents. They are the defense's own words, collected over four years, now assembled in a way that shows they cannot all be simultaneously true.

That is the *lever*. Once the defense's own words are inconsistent with each other, the plaintiff does not need to prove the defense is wrong. The plaintiff only needs to prove the defense is inconsistent, and ask the court to choose which of the defense's statements to believe. Either way the court decides, an element is proven.

The pressure shows up in a very specific pattern in each case.

**In the appeal**, the pressure is on the question of prejudice. The defense needs the court to believe that the trial court's refusal to give the Vehicle Code instructions was harmless — that the jury reached a correct outcome regardless. But the jury came back 9–3, which is the bare minimum for a civil verdict in California. A 9–3 verdict means a single juror shift would have flipped the outcome. Under the governing case (*Soule v. General Motors*), when a verdict is that close and the instructional error touches the central duty-of-care question, prejudice is effectively presumed. The defense's only escape is to argue the Vehicle Code statutes were not the governing law or were "implicitly covered" by the general negligence instructions. Both escapes are closed: the jury literally asked for Vehicle Code § 22107 by number, which means the statutes were not implicitly covered; and Defense Exhibit 500, which is the operative complaint, specifically pleaded the statutes, which means they were in the case.

**In the 801 case**, the pressure is on the "false representation" and "prior possession" elements. The plaintiff has two certified transcript sources: the February 18, 2025 hearing (where "news to me" was said) and the April 16, 2025 Day-7 colloquy (where "provided by Dolan" was said). These cannot both be accurate. The defense must either say the first statement was wrong (which proves the false-representation element) or say the second statement was wrong (which requires impeaching the court reporter, a nearly impossible move). Whichever the defense chooses, the plaintiff's element is proven. That is not a trick. It is what happens when a party makes contradictory statements on the record and the record is kept by neutral officers of the court.

**In the 802 case**, the pressure is on the adequacy of the 2021 investigation. CSAA's internal claims manual — which will be produced in discovery — sets out the company's own written standard for how to handle a claim with a police report in hand. Industry custom and Title 10 of the California Code of Regulations both require a thorough, fair, objective investigation. The 2021 file will show what CSAA did, and what CSAA did not do, before sending the denial letter. If CSAA did not pull the 911, did not pull the body-worn camera, and did not pull the CAD record despite the police report naming it, the denial letter's "concluded" language becomes the exact kind of half-truth that California's fraud statutes (California Civil Code §§ 1709 and 1710) and the unfair-competition statute (California Business & Professions Code § 17200) are designed to address. CSAA cannot fix this by producing additional 2021-era investigation notes, because if those notes existed they would have been part of the claim file from the beginning; and if they do not exist, the absence itself is the fraud.

In each forum, the defense is trying to defend a different element, but the defense's best argument in each forum is contradicted by the defense's own prior statements in one of the other forums. That is the full structural problem. There is no "right answer" the defense can give that does not hand the plaintiff a tool somewhere.

## 8. Flanking Pressure: Why the Defense Cannot Fight One Fire Without Feeding Another

A single lawsuit is a two-dimensional fight: the plaintiff pushes forward, the defense pushes back, and each side tries to control the record that the fact-finder eventually reads. Adding a second lawsuit makes the geometry three-dimensional: now each side has to keep track of two records at once, and a choice made in one courtroom shows up as a fact in the other.

Three lawsuits, against overlapping defendants, on a shared factual spine, make the problem four-dimensional. That is the situation CSAA, the attorney defendants, and the driver are in. Each move the defense makes has cross-case consequences, and those consequences are asymmetric: they generally help the plaintiff.

Consider a specific example. Suppose CSAA, in the 802 case, wants to argue that it did not direct or control the defense of the underlying personal-injury case. That would mean the retained attorneys acted on their own in preparing the motions in limine and in making the statements the 801 case is built on. That is a plausible position in isolation, and it might help CSAA reduce its ratification exposure in 801. But if CSAA actually takes that position, two problems appear. First, California Civil Code § 2338 makes a principal liable for the wrongful acts of an agent acting within the scope of the agency, and the retention letters and billing records already establish agency; the "independent counsel" position is legally weak. Second, if CSAA convinces a court that it did not control the defense, it has just told the court that its retained counsel acted without insurer oversight — which is precisely the kind of loose supervision that *supports* an institutional-bad-faith claim under the 802 pleading. The position CSAA would take to minimize 801 exposure actively increases 802 exposure.

The reverse is also true. Suppose CSAA wants to argue, in 802, that it took claim-handling seriously, supervised its counsel, and engaged in reasonable investigation in 2021. That position may help CSAA reduce 802 punitive exposure, but it hands the plaintiff a ratification finding in 801, because if CSAA was a careful, engaged supervisor of its counsel in 2021 and thereafter, it cannot plausibly say it did not know what its counsel was doing in 2025.

The same crossfire applies to the attorney defendants individually. California Business & Professions Code § 6128(a) — the attorney-deceit statute — makes it a misdemeanor and a civil tort for a lawyer to deceive a court or a party. The violation authorizes Civ. Code § 3294 punitive damages and Prentice fees. The predicate acts in the 801 case are the certified-transcript statements: "news to me," "not produced," "provided by Dolan." These are not disputed recollections; they are on the record. The attorneys' defense to § 6128(a) would have to be that the statements, read in context, were not deceptive. But the "not produced" statement in 2025 and the "provided by Dolan" statement in 2025 are both *in the 2025 record*. Any attempt to reconcile them for § 6128(a) purposes creates a problem for CSAA's ratification defense, because it forces the attorneys to explain what they knew, when they knew it, and who at CSAA they communicated with.

This is what "pressing on all fronts and flanks" means in practical terms. The defense has a limited number of moves. Each move is pinned — in the chess sense — by a fact in another case. The defense is not being outmaneuvered in any single game; it is being outmaneuvered because the games are connected, and the defense cannot pick a position in one game without surrendering a position in another.

## 9. The Mechanisms of the Causes of Action: Why the Law Itself Supports the Pressure

The plaintiff's leverage is not purely factual. The specific causes of action he has chosen each come with built-in mechanisms that amplify the pressure produced by the spine and the weave. Understanding those mechanisms helps explain why the defense's position is as narrow as it is.

**Extrinsic fraud and the *Kulchar* framework (801).** An independent action in equity to vacate a prior judgment for extrinsic fraud has four elements: a false representation, a concealment or chain-of-custody problem that kept the truth from the court, a state of mind sufficient to make the fraud wrongful rather than accidental, and a showing that the prior judgment would plausibly have been different if the fraud had not occurred. The defense has to defeat all four elements to win. The plaintiff only has to prove one element per evidentiary node to keep the case alive on summary judgment. With the Day-7 admission, the February 18, 2025 "news to me" transcript, the certified retention letters, the claim file, and the 9–3 verdict, the plaintiff has multiple independent proofs for each element. In a case with that many redundant proofs, summary judgment for the defense is structurally unavailable.

**Attorney deceit under Business & Professions Code § 6128(a) (801).** This statute is unusual: it is both a statutory tort and a misdemeanor, and its violation authorizes Civ. Code § 3294 punitive damages plus Prentice fees. The triggering act is deceit "with intent to deceive the court or any party." The predicate acts in this case are on the record. Defending against § 6128(a) in this context is extraordinarily difficult because the attorneys cannot plausibly argue they were unaware of what was said in court under their names. Beyond the money, the statute creates a career-risk dimension — State Bar referral, disciplinary investigation, malpractice-carrier tender — that gives each individual attorney an incentive independent of CSAA's incentives. Over time, that incentive divergence tends to produce cooperating witnesses, which further compounds the plaintiff's leverage.

**Insurance Code § 790.03(h) and Title 10 regulations (802).** Insurance law in California imposes duties on insurers that do not exist in ordinary contract or tort law. One of those duties — under Insurance Code § 790.03(h)(5) — is to attempt in good faith to effectuate prompt, fair, and equitable settlements when liability has become reasonably clear. Another — under Title 10 of the California Code of Regulations, §§ 2695.4 and 2695.7 — is to conduct a thorough, fair, objective investigation. When an insurer says an investigation is "concluded" but has not pulled the documents a reasonable investigation would have produced, the regulatory structure makes the denial actionable. This is not a vague "bad faith" claim. It is a violation of a specific statutory and regulatory standard, and the standard is not something CSAA can disavow — it is the same standard applied to every California-licensed insurer.

**California Business & Professions Code § 17200 (802).** The Unfair Competition Law is broad on purpose. It has three prongs: unlawful, fraudulent, and unfair. An act that violates any California statute or regulation satisfies the "unlawful" prong, even if the plaintiff does not prove traditional fraud. That means even if the plaintiff's Civil Code § 1709 fraud claim were somehow weakened, the UCL claim survives on the regulatory violations alone, and the UCL authorizes restitution and injunctive relief. The injunction is the piece that elevates the case from a bilateral money dispute to a structural one, because a statewide injunction against a claim-handling practice has enormous institutional cost: regulatory examinations, mandatory process reforms, periodic compliance reporting. Insurers typically fear UCL injunctions more than they fear money judgments, because the money is capped by what the insurer can pay, while the injunction reshapes the business.

**California Civil Code § 2338 and ratification (801 and 802).** When an agent commits a wrong while acting within the scope of the agency, the principal is liable. Ratification extends this rule to situations where the principal becomes aware of the agent's wrong and either approves it expressly or fails to disavow it. CSAA retained the law firm. CSAA paid the law firm. CSAA received reports from the law firm. Once the plaintiff shows (through discovery of claim notes, billing entries, and internal communications) that CSAA was aware of the representations its retained counsel was making and did not disavow them, ratification is established. That is what carries the 801's attorney-deceit findings into 802 as evidence of CSAA's institutional state of mind.

Every one of those mechanisms is written into California statutory and case law. The plaintiff did not invent them. He simply chose causes of action whose element structures happen to match the shape of the evidence he has.

## 10. Why the 9–3 Verdict Matters So Much

A brief but important observation. In California civil litigation, a general-jurisdiction civil verdict requires agreement from nine of the twelve jurors. Nine out of twelve is the floor. Not ten, not eleven — nine. A 9–3 verdict is, by definition, the narrowest legally possible finding. One juror's change of mind would have produced a different result.

That matters in this case for three reasons. In the appeal, the 9–3 verdict is the factual underpinning of the prejudice analysis. Under the *Soule* framework, a close verdict combined with an instructional error on a central element is very likely to produce reversal. In the 801 case, the 9–3 verdict is evidence that the concealed materials would have mattered — a 12–0 verdict would be harder to reopen because the concealment could be characterized as immaterial; a 9–3 verdict undermines the "immaterial" characterization. In the 802 case, the 9–3 verdict shows that even without the 911 audio or the body-worn camera, three jurors already disagreed with the defense; with those materials available to an adjuster in 2021, "liability reasonably clear" under Insurance Code § 790.03(h)(5) becomes a straightforward conclusion.

The 9–3 verdict is, in effect, the jury's own statement that the case was close. The defense cannot credibly argue in any of the three forums that the evidence overwhelmingly supported its side when the jury itself said otherwise by the smallest legally cognizable margin.

## 11. The Appeal as a Detonator: Zero Dollars, Full Pressure

One of the most important structural features of the three-case architecture is that the appeal carries no direct money remedy. If the First District reverses, the plaintiff does not receive a check; he receives a remand for a new trial. That is why it is tempting, from the defense side, to under-invest in the appeal — there is no payout to stop.

That instinct is a trap. The appeal is not a money vehicle. It is a *detonator*. A reversal triggers three chain effects. First, it produces a retrial under a clean evidentiary record, with the 911 audio authenticated, with the body-worn camera admitted, and with the Vehicle Code turning-duty instructions given. At that retrial, the 9–3 posture does not hold, because the party admission comes in and the governing duty of care is clearly stated. Second, it produces a published appellate opinion (or a substantive unpublished one) that can be judicially noticed in the 801 and 802 cases. Third, it reactivates CSAA's indemnity obligation for the retrial, because the underlying policy obligation does not end when an initial verdict is rendered; it follows the case through its full appellate and remand course.

The zero-dollar ceiling of the appeal is therefore misleading. The appeal is the lowest-friction pressure source available to the plaintiff, because the defense cannot buy it off directly — there is nothing monetary to settle. The plaintiff pays only filing fees and attorney time, and in return receives the possibility of a reversal that reshapes the record of every other case. That is a remarkable cost-benefit profile, and it is why the appellate posture has been rated as highly prejudicial-to-defense in the risk-surface files.

## 12. What a Defense Would Need to Look Like to Work

It is worth asking the question directly: what does a successful defense of this situation look like? The honest answer, given the record on file, is that there are very few successful-defense paths, and all of them require conceding something that makes another case harder to win.

A defense that concedes the 911 audio is material — because the audio is a party admission that comes in automatically — has to argue that the concealment of the audio was not what drove the 9–3 verdict. That is an uphill argument because 9–3 is the narrowest possible verdict, and because the appellate court's prejudice analysis under *Soule* does not require the plaintiff to prove the audio would have flipped the verdict; it only requires showing the audio could plausibly have flipped one juror.

A defense that concedes CSAA did not have the 911 audio or the body-worn camera in 2021 has to explain why a reasonable insurer, looking at a police report that referenced a CAD number, would not pull the CAD or the associated body-worn camera footage. That defense runs into Title 10 of the California Code of Regulations, which effectively defines "thorough investigation" to include following obvious references in the documents already in hand. Industry custom is consistent. Expert testimony on claim-handling practice would not support a 2021 investigation that ignored the CAD reference.

A defense that concedes the Day-7 "provided by Dolan" admission is accurate but argues it does not prove prior possession by the defense has to explain what "provided by Dolan" could otherwise mean. The plain reading of those words, in context, is that the materials were transferred from prior counsel to current counsel. There is no alternative reading that preserves the defense's "news to me" position.

A defense that argues the attorneys' January 2025 motions in limine and February 2025 hearing statements were not false has to reconcile them with the April 2025 transcript. That reconciliation requires either impeaching the earlier statements (which proves the 801 case's false-representation element) or impeaching the later transcript (which requires attacking the court reporter's certification).

In each case, the defense's path to survival requires making a move that the plaintiff has already pre-positioned against. That is the signature of a well-engineered case structure.

## 13. Why Settlement Is the Only Rational Off-Ramp, and Why It Keeps Getting Harder

The final piece of the narrative is a practical one. In a case where the defense's options are structurally constrained, settlement becomes not just a possibility but the only rational path. And yet the settlement problem for CSAA has a particular shape: the longer it waits, the more expensive settlement becomes.

This happens for several reasons. Discovery in the 801 and 802 cases generates additional record. Each claim-file page produced, each deposition taken, each email reviewed, adds to the public footprint. An interlocutory ruling in 801 — even a denial of summary judgment on the fraud counts — creates a finding that CSAA cannot undo. An adverse ruling on the anti-SLAPP motion produces mandatory fee-shifting under California Code of Civil Procedure § 425.16(c), which adds real money to the settlement floor. An appellate decision, whether favorable or adverse to the driver, generates additional law that constrains the defense's options in the superior-court cases.

Each of those events is a *ratchet*. They move the settlement floor up, and none of them move it down. That is why the worst-case scenario described in the companion document is not a static number — it is a number that grows with time. The defense's best moment to settle is always yesterday. The plaintiff's structural leverage increases every week.

This is not a tactic invented by the plaintiff. It is the natural consequence of having three live cases on a shared factual spine, with a judicial system that produces findings and admissions as the cases move forward. The plaintiff simply recognized the dynamic early and structured his filings to take advantage of it.

## 14. The Plaintiff's Core Achievement, in One Sentence

If all of the above has to be compressed to a single idea, it is this: the plaintiff has arranged the factual record, the legal theories, and the procedural forums so that the defense's *own statements* have become the most important evidence in the case, and those statements cannot all be simultaneously true. The plaintiff's structural advantage does not come from hiding the ball, inflating damages, or exploiting sympathy. It comes from having read the record carefully, identified the contradictions the defense has already created, and chosen causes of action whose elements line up with those contradictions. The pressure the defense feels is not pressure the plaintiff is applying. It is pressure the defense's own past representations are applying, which the plaintiff has merely made visible and legally actionable.

That is the fundamental answer to the question "how is this case working?" It is working because the defense told two different stories to two different audiences about the same underlying facts, and the plaintiff assembled the record that shows both stories in one place. Once that record exists — and it exists now, in certified transcripts, in regulatory filings, in pleadings, in the operative complaints and the denial letter — the case is not about which side is more persuasive. It is about which of the defense's own statements the court chooses to believe. Either choice completes an element in one or more of the three cases. That is why the plaintiff can press on all fronts, and why the defense is flanked not by the plaintiff's arguments but by its own.

## 15. A Note on Tone and Purpose

This narrative is intentionally restrained. Nothing here is predictive of a specific outcome in any of the three matters. Courts make their own decisions, juries make their own decisions, and institutional parties like CSAA have options that a case analysis cannot fully model. What the narrative does describe is the *structure* of the situation and the reasons the structure produces pressure. Structure is not destiny. But in a litigation posture this tight, structure tends to be a very strong predictor of where the cases go.

The companion document to this narrative — `CROSS-CASE-LOGIC-TREE-AND-CSAA-WORST-CASE.md` — provides the tables, the logic trees, and the quantified risk-surface numbers for readers who want the underlying detail. This narrative is the conceptual layer above those tables. Read together, they describe one case from two angles: the analyst's angle (elements, trees, numbers) and the storyteller's angle (theory, structure, leverage). Both lead to the same conclusion. The plaintiff built the trap; the defense walked into it; the trap is not sprung by cleverness, it is sprung by the ordinary mechanisms of California civil law operating on a record the defense itself assembled.
