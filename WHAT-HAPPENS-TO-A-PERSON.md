# What happens to a person

> **This page is commentary.** It is published as speech under the First Amendment and article I, section 2 of the California Constitution. It is not a court filing. It is not service of any paper on any party. It does not restart any Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 period.

> **No names, no dates, no case numbers.** This page describes a category of experience rather than any individual. Nothing here is an assertion of fact about any person or company. Readers who want to know whether events of this kind are documented anywhere can start at the [homepage](README.md) and read the filings themselves.

There is a version of this that happens to somebody every day, and almost nobody writes it down. It is worth writing down, because the paperwork that follows is impossible to understand unless you know what it grew out of.

---

## The hospital

It starts in a body.

A person crosses a street and wakes up somewhere else. The room is loud in a way hospitals are loud, and there are people above them saying numbers. They do not yet know what is broken. They do not know which of the broken things will heal and which ones they will keep. Nobody can tell them yet, and the not knowing is its own injury.

Somewhere across town a phone rings. A stranger says the person's name as a question, and everyone in that family remembers the next ten seconds for the rest of their lives.

What follows for the family is a kind of time that runs differently than ordinary time. Someone drives to the hospital not knowing whether they are arriving for a conversation or for a goodbye, and spends the whole drive negotiating with something they do not otherwise believe in. Someone else stays home with the children and lies to them about why. A parent who thought the caretaking years were finished discovers they are starting again, later in life, with less strength for it. A partner learns to pronounce the name of an injury they had never heard of the week before, and learns it well enough to correct people.

Nobody in that waiting room is thinking about an insurance company. That comes later, and it comes as a surprise.

---

## The letter

Three weeks later, roughly, a letter arrives.

The person is still in acute recovery. Still under specialist follow-up. Still, some days, unable to sit upright long enough to read a page carefully. Somebody in the family opens the envelope and reads it out loud, or waits until later, or does not know whether to bring it into the room at all.

The letter says the investigation is concluded. It says the insured is not liable.

Sit with what that sentence does to someone in that condition.

A stranger, in an office, has already finished thinking about the worst day of your life. They finished before you were medically cleared. They finished before anyone asked you a single question. The word "concluded" is not a neutral word. It tells you that a process occurred, that it was thorough enough to end, that people looked at the thing that happened to you and reached a considered result. You are being handed the answer to a question you did not know was being asked, by people who never called.

And there is a particular shame in it that is hard to describe to anyone who has not received one of these letters. It is the feeling of having been weighed and found to be exaggerating. Of being, in some file you will never see, the kind of person who makes things up. You were unconscious and now you are being told, in writing, on letterhead, that nothing much happened.

Then comes the part that changes the temperature of the whole story.

Much later, the person learns what that investigation actually consisted of.

Not that investigators gathered the evidence and weighed it and honestly disagreed. That they never obtained the recording of the driver's own call to the emergency dispatcher. Never obtained the body-worn camera footage from the officers who responded. Never pulled the dispatch records. Materials that sit in public systems and are available to anyone who fills out the form.

The file was empty, and the letter said it was full.

That is the sentence. It does not need an adjective attached to it. Hospital bed, three weeks, "concluded," empty file. Anyone who reads those four things in that order understands immediately what happened, and no amount of argument afterward improves on it.

## The years

An insurance denial is not an event. It is a duration. A case like this can take four years to reach a courtroom, and those years are the part of the story nobody puts in a press release.

For the person, it is medical appointments that never quite stop. A body that no longer does what it used to do, in small daily ways that are invisible to everyone else and constant to them. Work that becomes impossible, or worse, becomes merely harder, so that nobody quite believes how hard. Savings that go, in an order everybody in this situation learns: first the cushion, then the retirement, then the things you swore you would never sell. There is the specific humiliation of asking family for money at an age when you had expected to be the one lending it.

Identity is the quiet loss. You used to be somebody who did things. Now you are somebody that a thing happened to. It becomes the first fact about you in every room.

For the family, the arithmetic is different and rarely counted.

A partner becomes a nurse, a driver, a bookkeeper, an appointment scheduler, and an advocate, and somewhere inside that stack of jobs loses the person they were actually married to. They do not complain about it, which makes it worse, because unspoken exhaustion turns into distance and the distance has no obvious cause to point at.

Children grow up in a house organized around an injury. They learn to be quiet in the afternoons. They learn that a parent is fragile, which is a thing children are not supposed to learn that early, and they learn that adults can be helpless against institutions, which is a thing that stays with them.

Everyone learns not to ask how it is going, because the answer is the same and the answer is bad. Friends drift, and not out of cruelty. Sustained catastrophe is exhausting to stand next to, and people have their own lives. Holidays get quieter each year. At some point the family's inside jokes start being about the case, and then, after a while, they stop being funny.

And there is a loneliness in it that is specific and worth naming plainly. On the other side of this is not a person. It is an institution with actuarial patience. It does not get tired. It does not lie awake. It does not have a bad month and lose its nerve. Delay costs it almost nothing and costs the person nearly everything, and both sides know this from the first day. That asymmetry is not an accident of the system. For a certain kind of claims operation it is the product.

## The trial

Eventually the day arrives that the person has been waiting four years for.

And they learn the thing nobody warns you about, which is that most of what decides a trial is decided before the jury ever walks into the room. Motions filed in advance determine what the jury will be permitted to hear. Whole categories of evidence are argued over and disposed of in a mostly empty courtroom, in language the person does not speak, while they sit in a chair and watch the account of their own collision get narrower.

By the time twelve strangers are seated and told to keep an open mind, they are being asked to decide a version of events that has already been edited, and they are never told that it was edited, or by whom, or on what representation.

Then the verdict comes back nine to three.

Not a rout. The bare minimum a civil verdict requires. Three people in that room looked at the limited thing they were allowed to see and believed the injured person anyway. One more and it goes the other way.

That number does not leave. It sits with a person for the rest of their life, because it is proof that the outcome was never inevitable. It was arranged.

---

## The recognition

Afterward, in the flat exhausted weeks when there is nothing left to prepare for, the person reads the transcripts.

This is the hinge of the whole story.

Because in those pages they find the attorneys who obtained the pretrial rulings telling the court that the crucial evidence was of unknown origin, that they did not know where it came from, that it had never been produced to them. And elsewhere in the very same record, in the same proceeding, an acknowledgment that it had been handed over years earlier.

Anyone who has ever been lied to by an institution knows the physical sequence of that discovery. First cold, starting somewhere behind the sternum. Then nausea. Then a terrible clarity that is almost a relief, because at least now the shape of the thing is visible.

The realization is not "I lost." People lose. Losing is survivable, and honest losing is part of the deal you make when you walk into a courtroom.

The realization is: the thing I was told was a fair process was not one.

And here is the point that the rest of it depends on.

Nobody wakes up one morning and decides to spend their remaining years and their remaining money inside courthouses. Litigation is not an appetite. It is what is left over when a company certifies an investigation it did not perform, and the record shows it in the company's own documents, and no other institution will look at it.

The lawsuits are not the story. They are what the story turned into after every quieter option had been closed, one at a time, by the party that closed them. A person who is told the truth early sues nobody.

## Doing it alone

By this stage the money is gone, so the person represents themselves.

It is worth describing what that actually involves, because from the outside it looks like a choice and from the inside it is not one.

It is learning civil procedure at night, while healing, from books and public rules, in a body that hurts. It is deadlines that do not care that you were in an appointment all day or that you were too sick to sit up. It is filings rejected for margins and font size and caption formatting, each rejection costing days. It is opposing correspondence that suggests, sometimes gently and sometimes not, that you are not competent to be in this building and that the responsible thing would be to go away.

It is doing all of that against people who do this for a living, who are paid by the hour to do it, and who have colleagues.

And then, at the end, comes the last inversion.

The fact that the person keeps using the courts becomes the argument for not letting them use the courts. The volume of paper generated in response to motions filed against them is offered as proof that they file too much. The exercise of the right is offered as proof of the abuse of the right.

---

## Why people are disgusted by this

The pattern has a name the public already knows: deny, delay, defend.

People recognize it instantly, and not because they have read about this kind of case. They recognize it because they have their own smaller version. A claim denied by an adjuster who never called back. A form letter about a hospitalization that clearly nobody read. A denial that arrived faster than any real review could possibly have taken.

So the reason a record like this travels is not that any single story is uniquely terrible. Plenty of people have been hurt worse. It is that almost everybody already suspects the process works this way, and here is documentation of it, in writing, on the company's own letterhead.

And the specific thing that offends people is worth isolating, because it is not the denial.

A company is allowed to disagree with you. It is allowed to investigate and conclude you are wrong, and to say so, and to make you prove it. That is what the system is for.

What people cannot forgive is being told that a process happened that did not happen. The certification is the offense. It treats the injured person as somebody who will never check, and the only reason anyone ever learns otherwise is that occasionally, against considerable odds, somebody checks.

---

## Where it ends for the company

It would be easy to end this with a prediction, and the prediction would be worth nothing.

So instead, the structural facts, which do not depend on anyone's optimism.

A file like this does not improve with time. The contradiction lives in transcripts and on letterhead, which means it can never be walked back, only defended. Every additional motion adds another signature to a position that becomes more expensive to hold, and each signature is a public document.

Attempts to shut the record down become part of the record. A fee award, a security requirement, an order restricting access: each is a filed paper, dated, available to anyone who looks, and each one is easier to explain to a court than to explain to a stranger reading it later.

Regulators read denial letters for a living. They are not persuaded by adjectives and they are not impressed by volume. They know what a claim file is supposed to contain.

And then there is the arithmetic, which never changes and never gets better. Obtaining the recording, the footage, and the dispatch records at the outset would have cost a few hours of staff time and a few public-records requests. The institution has now spent many multiples of the entire underlying claim avoiding the question of whether those hours were ever spent.

Companies of this size do not lose these cases dramatically. They lose them slowly, in public, one document at a time, to people who kept the receipts.
