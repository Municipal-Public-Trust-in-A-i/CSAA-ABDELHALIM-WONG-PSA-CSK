## A Defense Strategy Built on an Expanded Admission

The defense strategy in *Rosario v. Abdelhalim* illustrates a fundamental danger in appellate advocacy: technically identifiable transcript citations can be assembled into a narrative that materially changes the significance of the recorded exchange. The problem is not that the respondent argued forcefully for waiver. The problem is that its briefs repeatedly presented Rosario's statements in a different chronological sequence, omitted an immediately adjoining qualification, and treated objections concerning distinct judicial acts as evidence of consent to a separate refusal to instruct the jury.

That strategy proved consequential. The Court of Appeal affirmed on consent-based forfeiture and invited-error grounds without determining whether the court's supplemental instructional response was substantively erroneous or prejudicial. The defense's account of agreement therefore became the gateway through which the instructional claim was denied merits review.

The record presents a striking contrast. During deliberations, the jury expressly asked whether Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a) applied to the defendant's negligence and whether it could be instructed on those provisions. The court responded that the jury could not consider them and that the court would not instruct on them. Rosario later challenged the proceedings. The respondent characterized those objections as proof that Rosario had wanted the instructions withheld.

The resulting legal question is straightforward: **When does an acknowledgment of an existing restriction on jury consideration become consent to the court's separate refusal to provide additional legal instructions?**

The defense answered that question by expanding the scope of Rosario's statements rather than establishing assent to the precise refusal clause.

## The First Error: Treating CACI Numbering as Dispositive of a Jury's Request for Law

The respondent's appellate brief repeatedly emphasized that Rosario had not specifically requested CACI No. 705, the instruction addressing reasonable care when turning. It also correctly identified CACI No. 707 as a speed-limit instruction rather than the turning instruction.

That distinction matters when evaluating the original proposed instructions. It does not dispose of a separate supplemental-instruction claim arising after the jury begins deliberating.

**Code of Civil Procedure section 614 imposes an independent procedural duty when a deliberating jury requests information concerning a point of law arising in the case.** The duty concerns adequate legal guidance, not whether the parties previously supplied a particular CACI instruction number.

The jury here identified the relevant statutes by number and expressly requested instruction if they applied. The defendant had testified that he was making a right turn at approximately five miles per hour. (8 RT 120:17-26.) That testimony furnished a factual basis for examining Vehicle Code section 22107's turning requirements. Section 21804(a), governing entry onto a highway from property or an alley, required its own separate applicability analysis.

The trial judge retained authority to determine which statutory provisions applied and whether the existing instructions adequately addressed them. The jury's question did not require the court to deliver every requested statute. It did require consideration of the applicable legal principles and the guidance necessary to answer the question.

The defense's initial-instruction argument did not resolve that separate duty.

*Flores v. Liu* (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 278, 289, recognizes the trial court's responsibility to provide necessary supplemental guidance. *Sesler v. Ghumman* (1990) 219 Cal.App.3d 218, 226-228, demonstrates why a jury question exposing an omitted legal principle can be consequential even after the original charge has been given.

A sound appellate defense would have addressed the legal applicability of the statutes, the adequacy of the instructions already given, and the effect of the response. Substituting a dispute over CACI numbering for that analysis left the central section 614 issue unresolved.

## The Second Error: Reversing the Chronology Twice

The most concrete defect in the respondent's presentation appears in its treatment of the April 23, 2025 colloquy.

The certified transcript follows this sequence:

**First**, Rosario acknowledged the proposition that jurors could not consider material outside the existing instructions. (11 RT 4:8-10.)

**Second**, he immediately qualified his acknowledgment by complaining that the judge had added material and describing its effect. (11 RT 4:11-14.)

**Third**, the judge asked whether Rosario objected to the response to Jury Request No. 1. (11 RT 4:15-17.)

**Fourth**, Rosario answered and identified his objection concerning what the jury had been accessing or requesting. (11 RT 4:18-25.)

The respondent's appellate brief, at page 8, presented the third and fourth events before the first. It then introduced the earlier acknowledgment as occurring "Further on."

The Supreme Court Answer repeated the same arrangement at page 6.

Both documents also cited 11 RT 4:8-14 while reproducing only the acknowledgment at lines 8-10, omitting the qualification at lines 11-14.

**That combination is consequential.** The arrangement makes the earlier acknowledgment appear to follow the judge's focused question, as though Rosario subsequently confirmed agreement. The actual transcript places the acknowledgment before the question and immediately accompanies it with a complaint about the judge's addition.

Appellate advocates routinely organize evidence by subject rather than chronology. But a presentation that reverses the sequence, describes an earlier passage as coming later, and excludes the adjoining qualification can materially influence the inference drawn from the record.

Here, that inference was consent to the completed refusal to instruct.

The repetition across two filings establishes that the same presentation was carried from the intermediate appeal into Supreme Court proceedings. It does not, by itself, establish knowing deception. It does establish a repeated, objectively verifiable discrepancy in the factual presentation on which the defense relied.

## The Third Error: Assigning Objections to the Wrong Judicial Act

The respondent's waiver theory also depends on collapsing distinct subjects into a single purported position.

Rosario's statements addressed several matters: suspected unauthorized juror research, the restriction on considering provisions outside the instructions, the court's refusal to supply instructions under Jury Request Form No. 1, and the additional vehicle and highway definitions supplied under Form No. 2.

Those are not interchangeable subjects.

He objected to the trial judge's failure to instruct the jury before deliberations, which, in his understanding, left jurors needing to seek legal guidance concerning the evidence. His objection to that resulting predicament is not an objection to supplying the instructions, and it is not consent to refusing them.

The defense treated Rosario's statements about jurors accessing or requesting Vehicle Code provisions as evidence that he opposed the court's providing those provisions through proper instructions.

The filed papers assign those statements to the court's failure to instruct and to the Form No. 2 transmission, not to consent to Refusal 1.

A litigant can maintain that jurors must obtain governing law from the court rather than investigate it independently. The object of that position remains the court's failure to instruct, not a request that the instructions be withheld.

The distinction becomes particularly important because the completed Form No. 1 response included two separate commands: a restriction on consideration and an express refusal to instruct. The respondent's presentation treated agreement with the former as approval of the latter.

The Form No. 2 exchange raised another distinct issue. Rosario objected to the court's additional highway-and-street explanation and its transmission during deliberations. The statutory sentence identifying a street as a highway was drawn from Vehicle Code section 360. Thus, the genuine dispute concerned the addition's scope, disclosure, timing, and effect, rather than whether those words existed in California law.

An appellate argument that attributes these Form No. 2 objections to Rosario's agreement with the refusal under Form No. 1 changes their subjects. That is the object-assignment problem at the center of the petition.

## The Fourth Error: Denying a Reason the Judge Expressly Gave

A particularly serious discrepancy appears in the respondent's appellate brief at pages 11-12.

The brief maintained that the court's refusal was not based on the statutory provisions' absence from evidence and characterized Rosario's contrary account as unsupported.

The trial transcript directly records the judge's explanation:

> “neither Vehicle Codes Section 22107 nor 21804 subdivision A were part of the evidence.” (11 RT 7:5-7.)

The judge immediately stated that jurors were not permitted to consider those provisions as evidence. (11 RT 7:8-9.)

That is not an inference invented by the appellant. It is part of the court's own explanation of the response.

The judge also asserted waiver, so the evidentiary rationale was not his only stated justification. But the defense's categorical denial that this reasoning supported the response is difficult to reconcile with the recorded language.

The legal distinction matters. California public statutory law is subject to judicial notice under Evidence Code section 451(a). A statute does not become available as governing law only after a party introduces it as evidence. Its applicability depends on the issues and relevant trial facts.

The defense's introduced complaint contained references to the statutory provisions, and the driver testified about his right-turn movement. Those circumstances did not automatically establish a statutory violation, but they required the court to assess the legal applicability of the requested provisions rather than exclude them merely because the statutory text was not evidence.

The respondent's own appellate brief recognized that the admitted complaint was a likely source of the jurors' question. That acknowledgment made its categorical treatment of the resulting inquiry especially difficult to defend.

## The Fifth Error: Treating Later Objections as Proof of Earlier Invited Error

The defense invoked invited error to argue that Rosario could not challenge the refusal because his own conduct had produced it.

That doctrine requires attention to causation.

Under *Norgart v. Upjohn Co.* (1999) 21 Cal.4th 383, 403, invited error concerns conduct inducing the challenged ruling. A statement made after an instructional response has already been transmitted cannot itself cause the earlier transmission.

A later statement can provide evidence of earlier consent. But the advocate must establish that connection through the statement's actual subject and surrounding circumstances.

The respondent's theory blurred these two propositions. It used the reported post-delivery mistrial exchange to characterize Rosario's earlier position and then relied on that characterization to establish invited error.

That approach risks converting a litigant's reaction to an adverse ruling into the supposed cause of the ruling itself.

*Mary M. v. City of Los Angeles* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 202, 212-213, supplies an important distinction between inducing error and proceeding under an adverse ruling after objection. *People v. Bohana* (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 360, 372-373, separately illustrates the significance of actual consent to a jury response and earlier opposition on which the court relied.

The critical question remains whether Rosario approved or induced the particular refusal, not whether he used imperfect language while subsequently objecting.

## The Sixth Error: Minimizing the Judge's Express Rulings on the Objections

The respondent's waiver theory also confronts the judge's closing acknowledgment.

The judge expressly stated that Rosario had submitted arguments clarifying objections to the responses to both Jury Request Forms Nos. 1 and 2. The court confirmed that it had considered and overruled those objections. (11 RT 12:27-13:7.)

Earlier, the judge likewise declared that the objections were noted and overruled. (11 RT 11:28-12:4.)

These rulings establish that Rosario placed opposition to the completed responses before the court and that the court rejected it.

They do not automatically cancel the judge's earlier statement that Rosario had waived objection during the unreported consultation. An earlier agreement can remain relevant despite a later objection.

**But a serious waiver analysis must reconcile both portions of the record.** It must identify what the litigant previously accepted, what he subsequently opposed, and whether those statements concerned the same completed response.

The opinion's consent-based disposition did not examine that complete sequence. Instead, it accepted the broader agreement premise and declined to reach harmlessness.

The result was a procedural ruling that prevented substantive appellate consideration of the jury's express request and the court's refusal.

## The Final Irony: Accusing the Appellant of Rewriting History

The respondent concluded its appellate brief by accusing Rosario of trying to rewrite history. Its Supreme Court Answer repeated the accusation.

The irony is unmistakable.

The respondent was accusing its opponent of altering the historical account while its own briefs placed earlier transcript statements after later ones, removed an immediately adjoining qualification, and relied on the resulting presentation to argue unequivocal consent.

This is more than an unfortunate rhetorical choice. It illustrates why accusations of misrepresenting a record are most persuasive when the accusing party's own quotations withstand chronological and contextual verification.

A correct pin citation is not sufficient when the narrative surrounding it materially changes the meaning of the cited exchange.

## What This Case Teaches Appellate Strategists

The lesson is not that every selective quotation constitutes misconduct. The evidence establishes repeated quotation and chronology problems, not a final adjudication of counsel's intent.

The lesson is that **procedural defenses become vulnerable when they depend on assigning a statement a broader meaning than its actual subject and context support.**

The defense had legitimate arguments available. It could challenge the applicability of section 21804(a), the completeness of the original instruction requests, the significance of the judge's earlier account of agreement, and whether the alleged instructional error probably affected the verdict under *Soule v. General Motors Corp.* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 548, 580-581.

Those arguments required legal and factual analysis.

Instead, its consent-based argument repeatedly relied on a contested reading of the colloquy and a presentation that reversed the actual speaking sequence.

The Court of Appeal's opinion expressly recognized the independent duty to provide necessary supplemental instructions but resolved the claim through attributed agreement. The petition for review consequently presents a focused question about how appellate courts determine actual consent when the quoted record contains distinct subjects, immediate qualifications, disputed prior consultation, and subsequent acknowledged objections.

The appropriate institutional response is a careful examination of that record and the governing preservation standards. The court must distinguish an initial failure to propose a particular CACI instruction from the later statutory duty to answer a deliberating jury's request, and must separately assess actual consent, invited error, instructional correctness, and prejudice.

**The fundamental strategic failure was trying to make the waiver narrative do the work of the underlying law. When a defense needs reordered quotations, omitted qualifications, and expanded objects of assent to make its procedural bar appear conclusive, the integrity of that bar becomes the central appellate issue.**

That is the real significance of *Rosario v. Abdelhalim* for appellate advocacy: the most damaging weakness in a procedural defense may not be an incorrect citation, but an accurately cited statement made to prove something the complete record does not necessarily establish.
