I.    INTRODUCTION
Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) improperly seeks damages that are not recoverable
as a matter of law, including: attorney’s fees, punitive damages, and a series of allegedly compensatory
damages including: “forced third party litigation” damages, lost earnings, loss of earning capacity, “lost
productive time,” various medical-related damages, transportation, “household assistance,” emotional
distress damages, “future damages,” injunctive relief, prejudgment interest and other miscellaneous and
legally unsupportable items of alleged damage. All these items of alleged damages arise from Plaintiff’s
underlying third-party litigation against a CSAA insured, where Plaintiff lost by defense verdict; hence,
these alleged damages are unsupported by any statutory, contractual, or factual basis. Further, the conduct
alleged in the FAC is absolutely privileged as occurring in the course of litigation. Under Code of Civil
Procedure § 436, the Court is authorized to strike any irrelevant, improper, or nonconforming matter from
a pleading. Because the deficiencies in Plaintiff’s damages claims appear on the face of the FAC and
cannot be cured by amendment, the challenged portions should be stricken in their entirety.

                                             III.    LEGAL STANDARD
               “Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion
     to strike the whole or any part thereof….” (Code Civ. Proc. § 435(B)(1).) Furthermore, “[t]he court may,
     upon a motion made pursuant to section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems
     proper:
               (a) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.
(b) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this
               state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 436)
Where pleadings are defective, “the defect may be raised by a demurrer or motion to strike or by
motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Coyne v. Krempels, (1950) 36 Cal.2d 257, 263.)
Here, each of Plaintiff’s alleged damages falls squarely within this rule. Plaintiff cannot recover
attorney’s fees as a self-represented litigant; cannot meet the heightened pleading and evidentiary
requirements for punitive damages; and cannot base any other claimed damages on conduct that is
absolutely privileged under California’s litigation privilege, and when he lacks standing.
     IV.    THE FAC’S CLAIMED DAMAGES ARE IRRELEVANT, FALSE, IMPROPER AND DO
NOT CONFORM WITH THE LAWS OF THIS STATE
Plaintiff’s FAC improperly seeks multiple categories of damages that are not recoverable as a

demands for attorney’s fees, punitive damages, and all compensatory damages each fail independently
under well-established California law.         The FAC relies on conclusory allegations, improper legal
argument, and conduct that is either legally privileged or wholly insufficient to meet the applicable

A. THERE IS NO BASIS FOR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIM FOR ATTORNEY’S FEES
“Except as attorney’s fees are specifically provided for by statute, the measure and mode of
compensation of attorneys and counselors at law is left to the agreement, express or implied, of the
parties;…” (Trope v. Katz (1995) 11 Cal.4th 274, 278–279; Code Civ. Proc., § 1021.) Under California
law, a party litigating in propria persona is not entitled to recover attorney’s fees. (Trope, supra, 11 Cal.4th
at 292.)
Here, Plaintiff seeks attorney’s fees for his own time, as a pro per litigant, and for expenses
allegedly incurred in litigating his prior action—and this action. (FAC, p. 3, lines 12–15; p. 9, lines 16–
25.) However, the FAC identifies no statutory, contractual, or equitable basis for such recovery. Even if
Plaintiff had an attorney, which he does not, he represented himself in both the underlying litigation and
this action and therefore cannot recover such fees as a matter of law. (Ibid.)
Accordingly, Plaintiff’s demand for attorney’s fees is improper and should be stricken pursuant to
Code of Civil Procedure section 436 as irrelevant and not drawn in conformity with California law.
B. THERE IS NO BASIS FOR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIM FOR PUNITIVE DAMAGES
“A claim for punitive damages requires ‘evidence which establishes by ‘clear and convincing
evidence’ that the defendant has been ‘guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.’” (Food Pro Internat., Inc.
v. Farmers Ins. Exchange (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 976, 994, quoting Stewart v. Truck Ins. Exchange
(1993) 17 Cal.App.4th 468, 481-482.) This requirement is enshrined in Civil Code § 3294(a) which states
in relevant part that “[i]n an action for the breach of an obligation. . . , where it is proven by clear and
convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, the plaintiff. . .
may recover damages for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant.” To meet this
evidentiary standard, Plaintiff must present evidence that is “so clear as to leave no substantial doubt’;
‘sufficiently strong to command the unhesitating assent of every reasonable mind.” (In re Angelia P,
(1981) 28 Cal.3d 908, 919.)
Along with its failure to state a “breach of an obligation”, as required by Civ. Code § 3294(a), the
FAC presents no factual support for any fraud. Instead, the FAC seems to rely on quotations from litigation
related communications in which CSAA stated that it had concluded its investigation into Plaintiff’s
original claim. (See FAC ¶ 2, 4, 5, 8, 16, 17, 18, 20.) Plaintiff alleges that this denial letter, and subsequent

litigation conduct, constitutes evidence of fraud. Not only is the information alleged by Plaintiff proper,
litigation related conduct and thus subject to absolute privilege, it does not present evidence of any kind,
let alone “sufficiently strong to command the unhesitating assent of every reasonable mind.” (In re
Angelia, supra, at 919.) Instead, to support his assertion Plaintiff presents arguments which.
The FAC presents as evidence only an argument that, “CSAA could not have reached a conclusion
that its insured was ‘not liable’ in the face of its own insured’s admission of contact. The existence of this
admission [allegedly in a 911 call] corroborates either that CSAA never obtained the recording (rendering

CSAA reached a different conclusion from the evidence, and the jury agreed with CSAA. Plaintiff’s FAC
fails, therefore, to present evidence which is so “clear as to leave no substantial doubt” (In re Angelia,

reached in its investigation, that CSAA’s insured was not, in any way, liable to Plaintiff.
1.      Plaintiff’s Claim for Punitive Damages is Subject to Strike Because There Has
Been No Oppression.
Oppression is conduct “that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard
of their rights.” (Today’s IV v. Los Angeles Metropolitan Transportation Authority (2022) 83 Cal. App.
5th 1137.)
To support his conclusion that CSAA’s denial of liability was improper, Plaintiff’s allegations
center around CSAA’s defense of it’s insured (See FAC at ¶ 22D, 22F.) Plaintiff was allegedly “forced …
into third-party litigation in CGC-21-594102 to vindicate his claim” via routine litigation. (See FAC ¶ 25.)
Not only was Plaintiff’s claim not “vindicated”, but a properly empaneled jury rendered a “9-3 defense
verdict in the Underlying Action.” (See FAC ¶ 25A.) In sum, Plaintiff alleges that CSAA’s good faith
litigation defense of it’s insured from Plaintiff’s unsuccessful litigation has somehow deprived him of key
rights. CSAA abiding its contractual duty to fund a successful defense of its cannot constitute the requisite
oppression for the pleading of punitive damages.

2.      Plaintiff’s Claim for Punitive Damages is Subject to Strike Because the FAC
Cannot Allege Malice.
“’Malice’ means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or
despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the
rights or safety of others.” (Civ. Code, § 3294 (c)(1).) “Despicable conduct” has been defined as conduct
that is “so vile, base, or contemptible that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent
people.” (McNeal v. Whittaker, Clark & Daniels, Inc. (2022) 80 Cal.App.5th 841, 866.)
At most, the FAC alleges that Defendant engaged in wrongful conduct by i) denying a claim; and
ii) funding a successful defense of that claim. (FAC ¶¶ 2, 3, 4, 16, 17, 21A.) Such allegations, even if
accepted as true, cannot be described as being “… so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or
loathsome that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people.” (American
Airlines, Inc. v. Sheppard, Mullin, Richter & Hampton (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 1017, 1050.) Plaintiff’s
FAC cannot possibly support even an inference of malice. CSAA performed an investigation, determined
that its insured was not liable, and informed Plaintiff of their decision. The same facts were presented to
a jury, and the jury agreed with CSAA’s denial position, rendering a “9-3 defense verdict in the Underlying
Action.” (FAC ¶ 25A.) This is routine practice in the insurance industry, and the objective facts as
presented by Plaintiff do not show conduct which was “intended by the defendant to cause injury to the
plaintiff or despicable conduct…carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the
rights or safety of others.” (Civ. Code § 3294 (c)(1).)
Therefore, CSAA’s denial of Plaintiff’s initial demand does not constitute malice, as required by
California law to support his demand for punitive damages.
3.      Plaintiff’s Claim for Punitive Damages is Subject to Strike Because The FAC
Cannot Plead Fraud.
“In California, fraud must be pled specifically; general and conclusory allegations do not suffice.”
(Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) When the defendant is a corporate entity, the
pleading standard is even higher, the Plaintiff must plead, with particularity, facts to “to show how, when,
where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.” (Ibid.)
The FAC fails to allege facts sufficient to state a claim for fraud. In the absence of a properly

Code Civ. Proc. § 436.
C. THERE IS NO BASIS FOR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS FOR VARIOUS COSTS,
               COMPENSATORY DAMAGES, INJUNCTIVE RELIEF, OR FUTURE DAMAGES
The entire factual basis contained in the FAC targets privileged conduct and communications
under Civ. Code § 47(b). Further, Plaintiff lacks standing to sue CSAA for the claims herein, including
the claim for all his alleged compensatory damages.
                    1.      The Absolute Litigation Privilege Bars the FAC’s Claim for Compensatory
                            Damages
            Civ. Code § 47(b)provides in relevant part:
A privileged publication or broadcast is one made…(b) In any (1) legislative proceeding,
(2) judicial proceeding, (3) in any other official proceeding authorized by law, or (4) in the
            initiation or course of any other proceeding authorized by law and reviewable pursuant to
Chapter 2…
“The only exception to the application of section 47(2) to tort suits has been for malicious
prosecution actions.” (Ribas v. Clark (1985) 38 Cal. 3d 355, 364.) The privilege also “poses a clear bar to
[an] action insofar as it is based on . . . the tort of intentional infliction of emotional distress.” (Ribas v.
Clark (1985) 38 Cal.3d 355, 364.)
Here, all the conduct alleged in Plaintiff’s FAC arises from statements, filings, or other
communicative acts undertaken in connection with prior judicial proceedings. As such, those allegations
fall squarely within the scope of the litigation privilege and cannot support a claim for emotional distress
damages as a matter of law.
Plaintiff has not alleged in the FAC any cause of action for malicious prosecution, nor could he do
so under these circumstances and facts alleged. On the contrary, Plaintiff is the party who initiated and
pursued the underlying litigation. Accordingly, the sole exception to the litigation privilege (malicious
prosecution) is inapplicable here. Accordingly, all portions of Plaintiff’s FAC related to emotional distress
damages should be stricken pursuant to Code Civ. Proc. § 436.

2.      Plaintiff Lacks Standing Against CSAA to Assert a Claim for Compensatory
Damages
Plaintiff lacks standing against CSAA in its capacity as insurance carrier for a third-party
insured—to whom Plaintiff lost at trial.
“As a general rule, a third party may directly sue an insurer only when there has been an assignment
of rights by, or a final judgment against, the insured.” (Shaolian v. Safeco Ins. Co. (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th
268.) Further, California courts have consistently held that an attorney cannot be sued by an adverse party
for that attorneys’ actions in representing their client, except for an action for malicious prosecution. (See,
e.g., Norton v. Hines (1975) 49 Cal.App.3d 917; Omega Video v. Superior Court, (1983) 146 Cal. App.
3d 470.)
Here, Plaintiff obtained neither an assignment from, nor judgment against, the Insured, Subhi
Abdelhalim. Plaintiff is a third party to Defendant, and had no direct relationship with it, and seeks only
to sue CSAA for its role in affording a defense to its Insured, through trial. Beyond these threshold bars,
there are no specific allegations that CSAA made any specific misleading representations to the Court,
whatsoever. Indeed, CSAA—in its role as insurance carrier to the original defendant—did not actually
litigate the trial. Indeed, Plaintiff’s FAC improperly imputes the acts of retained insurance defense
counsel, against Defendant. Plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that he can hold an insurance
company responsible for the litigation conduct of its third-party insurance defense counsel, while
defending a third-party insured. Hence, CSAA cannot possible be liable for Plaintiff’s underlying
emotional distress from a lawsuit Plaintiff lost.
3.      Plaintiff is Collaterally Estopped from Seeking Recovery on the Same
Damages Sought in his Underlying Jury Trial—Which he Lost.
Plaintiff is estopped from simply attempting to relitigate all the same damages that he failed to
obtain at his underlying jury trial.
“A collateral attack is an attempt to avoid the effect of a judgment or order made in some other
proceeding.” F.E.V. v. City of Anaheim (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 462, 471 (quoting Rico v. Nasser Bros.
Realty Co. (1943) 58 Cal.App.2d 878, 882.) “An attack in a second action on an earlier judgment is
collateral.” (Ibid.) (citing Wouldridge v. Burns (1968) 265 Cal.App.2d 82, 84.) “A judgment of a court of
           MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES IN SUPPORT OF MOTION TO STRIKE PLEADING
---
general jurisdiction can only be set aside on collateral attack if the judgment is void on the face of the
record.” (Wouldridge, supra, 265 Cal.App.2d at 84.) Finally, “collateral estoppel precludes relitigation of
issues argued and decided in prior proceedings.” (Singh v. Lipworth (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 813, 826.)
Finally, “[t]here are no maxims of the law more firmly established, or of more value in the
administration of justice, than [those] … designed to prevent repeated litigation between the same parties
in regard to the same subject of controversy.” U.S. v. Throckmorton (1878) 98 U.S. 61, 65 (emphasis
added.)
Here, the FAC’s entire effort is to relitigate all the same issues presented in his underlying case—
and seek all the same damages sought in the original case. All the key damages allegations against
Defendant target the same damages attributable to Plaintiff’s injuries in the underlying case, which
culminated in a defense verdict and valid judgment. Hence all the purported damages presented in this
FAC were already “argued and decided in prior proceedings.” (Singh, supra, 227 Cal.App.4th at 826).
Hence all Plaintiff’s damages should be stricken from the FAC.
4.      Plaintiff Was Not the Prevailing Party at the Underlying Jury Trial and
Cannot Therefore Seek Costs.
To obtain compensatory damages or prevailing party costs, Plaintiff must have prevailed at trial.
He did not.
Under Code Civ. Proc. § 1032, the term “‘Prevailing party’” includes the party with a net monetary
recovery, … the “prevailing party” shall be as determined by the court, and under those circumstances,
the court, in its discretion, may allow costs or not and, if allowed, may apportion costs between the parties.
Furthermore, “a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or
proceeding.”
Hence, here, all the items of Plaintiff’s alleged damages that are simply his costs in litigating the
underlying jury trial cannot be recovered and should also be stricken.
