# Neu-Visions Sports, Inc. v. Soren/McAdam/Bartells

**Citation:** 86 Cal.App.4th 303 (Cal. Ct. App. 2000)

**Court:** California Court of Appeal, Fourth District, Division Two

**Date:** December 22, 2000

**Panel:** Justice Hollenhorst (Opinion), with Justices Ramirez and Richli concurring

**Parallel Citations:** 103 Cal.Rptr.2d 159

---

## Parties

**Plaintiffs/Appellants:** Neu-Visions Sports, Inc. and Edge Skating Center, Inc.

**Defendants/Respondents:** Soren/McAdam/Bartells (accounting firm) and Jeffrey Sykes (principal)

---

## Facts

Plaintiffs sued an accounting firm and its principal for negligent misrepresentation in connection with a proposed ice and roller hockey arena project at a former Norton Air Force Base commissary building.

The plaintiffs alleged two misrepresentations by defendant Sykes:

1. **The "Value Representation":** That the former commissary building was worth $5 million.

2. **The "Title Representation":** That title to the property was not a problem for financing because another defendant (Beck) would obtain clear title before funding.

Defendants filed a motion for summary judgment based on deposition testimony from the plaintiffs' principals (Robert, Raymond, and Brian Neufeld).

---

## Procedural Posture

The trial court granted defendants' motion for summary judgment. Plaintiffs appealed.

---

## Issue

Whether the alleged misrepresentations were actionable statements of fact or non-actionable statements of opinion.

---

## Holding

**Affirmed.** The court held that the alleged misrepresentations were not actionable because they constituted expressions of opinion rather than statements of fact.

---

## Key Legal Principles (pp. 309-310)

### Distinguishing Fact from Opinion

The court established the following test for determining whether a statement is actionable:

> A statement is actionable when it "**expressly or impliedly asserts a specific verifiable fact**" or when "**the recipient would reasonably believe that it is intended as an assertion of fact.**"

A representation is **non-actionable "opinion"** only when it is understood by both parties as:
- A subjective belief
- A value judgment
- Puffery

### The Test Applied

The court found that the prediction about property valuation was "the expression of an opinion as to a future fact" and therefore non-actionable. An opinion of property value is "quintessentially a matter of opinion, not a statement of fact."

### Exceptions: When Opinions May Be Actionable

Despite the general rule that opinions are not actionable, California law recognizes three exceptions where opinions *can* support a negligent misrepresentation claim:

1. **Superior Knowledge Exception:** Where a party holds itself out as specially qualified and the other party reasonably relies on the speaker's superior knowledge.

2. **Fiduciary Exception:** Where the opinion comes from a fiduciary or other trusted person.

3. **Deliberate Affirmation Exception:** Where a party states its opinion as an existing fact or as implying facts that justify belief in the opinion's truth (i.e., "a deliberate affirmation of matters stated" rather than "a casual expression of belief").

### Question of Fact

The court noted: "Whether a statement is nonactionable opinion or actionable misrepresentation of fact is a question of fact for the jury."

---

## Elements of Negligent Misrepresentation

The court recited the elements a plaintiff must prove:

1. **Misrepresentation of a Material Fact:** The defendant must make an actual, positive assertion (not implied) about a past or existing fact.

2. **No Reasonable Ground for Belief:** The defendant must have no reasonable basis for believing the representation is true.

3. **Intent to Induce Reliance:** The defendant must intend that the plaintiff rely on the representation.

4. **Justifiable Reliance:** The plaintiff must justifiably rely on the misrepresentation.

5. **Causation and Damages:** The plaintiff's reliance must be a substantial factor causing harm.

---

## Significance

This case is frequently cited for the principle that a statement is actionable when it:
- Asserts or implies a fact that can be proven true or false
- Is reasonably understood by the recipient as an assertion of fact

Conversely, a statement is non-actionable when it:
- Is understood by both parties as a subjective belief or value judgment
- Concerns future events or predictions
- Constitutes mere puffery

The case has been cited in 66 subsequent cases and remains important precedent for distinguishing between actionable factual misrepresentations and non-actionable opinion statements, particularly regarding:
- Property valuations
- Predictions about future facts
- Statements by professionals holding themselves out as experts

---

## Application Example

When an insurer states that its "investigation is concluded," this is a factual assertion about a past event (the completion of an investigation) that can be tested: either the insurer performed the investigative steps necessary to reach a conclusion, or it did not. Under Neu-Visions Sports, such a statement "expressly or impliedly asserts a specific verifiable fact" and is therefore actionable if false.

This contrasts with a statement like "we believe the insured was not at fault," which may be characterized as an opinion or value judgment depending on context.
