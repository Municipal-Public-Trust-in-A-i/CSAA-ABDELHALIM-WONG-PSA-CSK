# When the Denial Letter Came Back as Three Lawsuits

### A reporter's guide to the Rosario v. CSAA litigation: the trial that ended one fight and started three, the trap the insurer's defense team built around itself, and what happens when a discovery clock runs out without answers

---

## Editor's note on scope

This narrative is written for a general newsroom and trade-press audience. It deliberately avoids motion-specific shorthand and calendar dates, because the story does not turn on any single filing. It turns on a structure: a four-year insurance dispute that produced a courtroom record so internally contradictory that the same facts now power three separate proceedings against the same insurer, the same lawyers, and (in one of them) the same trial-court ruling. The plaintiff is self-represented and indigent. The defendants are a multi-billion-dollar carrier (CSAA Insurance Exchange, the underwriting arm of AAA) and the law firms it retained.

---

## 1. Where this story begins: the verdict that did not end the case

The starting point for the three current proceedings is not a complaint. It is the close of an earlier personal-injury trial in San Francisco Superior Court arising from a pedestrian-versus-turning-vehicle collision serious enough to place the plaintiff in the **intensive care unit at San Francisco General Hospital** — the public trauma center that receives the city's most severe pedestrian injuries. While he was still in recovery and not yet medically cleared, the insurer for the driver mailed him a letter declaring its investigation "concluded" and its insured not liable. That letter — sent to a man who had not yet finished being treated for the collision it purported to have finished investigating — is the single document around which the rest of this story is built. The trial that eventually followed four years later returned a defense verdict on the bare-minimum civil margin — nine-to-three. By itself, that would normally be the end of the story.

What made it the *beginning* of a much larger story was what came out on the trial record in the final days of that proceeding:

- **The defendant driver authenticated, under oath, a 911 recording of his own voice** — a recording in which he is heard telling the dispatcher words to the effect that he had "barely" struck the pedestrian. The recording was placed before the jury inside the defense's own exhibit binder, and the defendant confirmed the voice was his.
- **The plaintiff's former trial attorney testified, also under oath, that the same 911 recording and related materials had been formally produced to the defense during discovery years earlier**, and used during depositions.
- **The defense file containing those materials had been transferred between two defense law firms by a publicly filed substitution-of-counsel**, with a transaction number that any clerk can pull. The successor firm held the file for roughly twenty months before trial.
- Yet during pretrial proceedings, **the same successor defense attorneys had told the trial judge, in open court and in written motions, that the recording was "news to me," of "unknown provenance," and "not produced in discovery."** The trial judge accepted those representations without ordering a file inventory, an evidentiary hearing, or written confirmation, and used them as a basis to defer the evidence and deny continuances.
- During deliberations, **the jury sent the judge a written question asking whether it could consider two specific Vehicle Code sections** governing the duty of a turning driver toward a pedestrian in a crosswalk. The judge told the jury, categorically, that statutes were "not part of the evidence" and could "not" be considered. The same Vehicle Code sections were, in fact, physically present in the materials the defense had submitted to the jury.

In other words, by the time the verdict was read, the trial record contained — in the defense's own transcripts, exhibits, and filings — every component of two separate causes of action that had nothing to do with whether the driver was negligent: an *extrinsic fraud on the court* claim against the defense lawyers, and a structural *instructional error* claim against the trial judge's handling of the jury's question. Both were preserved verbatim in the official record.

The plaintiff did not have to manufacture this evidence. The defense and the court created it.

## 2. The three cases, in plain English

Out of that record, the plaintiff opened three distinct fronts. They are best understood not as three lawsuits about the same accident, but as three different decision-makers being asked three different questions about three different kinds of wrongdoing.

### Case One — the appeal of the underlying trial

This proceeding is on file in the First District Court of Appeal. It does not re-litigate whether the driver was negligent. It asks an appellate panel a single, narrow question of law: when a deliberating jury asks whether it may consider the governing statutory duty of care, may a trial judge tell the jury, categorically, that statutes are "not evidence" and may not be considered? Under California's Code of Civil Procedure and Evidence Code, the answer is no — statutes are subject to mandatory judicial notice and are *law,* not evidence. The 9-3 verdict supplies the prejudice analysis: a one-juror swing changes the outcome. This case stands or falls on instructional error alone, independent of any fraud theory.

### Case Two — the equity action for fraud on the court

Filed in the same San Francisco Superior Court that hosted the original trial, but in a different department, this is an *independent action in equity* to set aside the underlying judgment on the ground of extrinsic fraud. Its caption is the broad one: it names the carrier, the named driver, the successor defense law firm, the predecessor defense law firm, and the individual attorneys who appeared at pretrial and trial. The theory is straightforward: officers of the court told the trial judge that evidence in their own file was "unknown" to them; the trial judge relied on those statements; the rulings that followed shaped the verdict. That sequence — knowledge, false representation, judicial reliance — is the textbook map of extrinsic fraud.

### Case Three — the institutional fraud and unfair-business-practices action

Also filed in San Francisco Superior Court but in a third department, this case has a narrower caption — the carrier and unnamed corporate doe defendants. Its anchor is a single document: the carrier's denial letter, mailed roughly three weeks after the collision, declaring the company's investigation "concluded" and the insured not liable. The timing is not a technicality. The plaintiff had just been discharged from the **intensive care unit at San Francisco General Hospital** — the county's Level I trauma center, which admits pedestrian-versus-vehicle casualties only when the treating team believes the patient is at risk of dying. The letter arrived while he was still in acute recovery, still under specialist follow-up, still physically unable to assemble a response. At that same moment, the carrier had not obtained the 911 recording of its own insured's call to the dispatcher, the responding officers' body-camera footage, or the computer-aided dispatch records — all available through routine public-records requests, all later obtained by the plaintiff's prior counsel within months. The case alleges that "concluded" was a representational event, not a description: a deliberate timing choice designed to close the file before the patient left the hospital, and the visible top of a deny-delay-defend pipeline that California's Unfair Competition Law and common-law fraud doctrine reach. It carries a *Prentice*-doctrine theory that recovers the plaintiff's entire four-year underlying-litigation cost as consequential damages, and a punitive-damages prayer keyed to corporate ratification.

The three cases run in parallel. They share a single factual record. A win on the appeal does not require a win in the equity action. A win in the equity action does not require a win in the institutional case. But a *defense* in any one of them tends to concede an element in the other two, and that is the structural feature that makes the whole architecture difficult to escape.

## 3. The plaintiff's strategic model — what the architecture is actually doing

The plaintiff's strategy is best described as **structural rather than rhetorical**. He is not asking any court to take his word for anything. He is positioning the same documentary record — transcripts, filings, transaction IDs, sworn statements by an officer of the court, and his opponents' own exhibits — in front of three different decision-makers at the same time, in such a way that each defense move in one forum becomes evidence in another.

Several features distinguish that model from ordinary post-trial litigation:

**A closed evidentiary system.** Every load-bearing factual proposition is sourced to a public record entry: a reporter's transcript page, a clerk-stamped substitution of counsel, a filed motion in limine, a denial letter on carrier letterhead, a juror question in the court file. There is essentially nothing in the architecture that depends on the plaintiff's own credibility as a witness, which is a deliberate choice given his pro-se status.

**Forced binary contradictions.** The model is built so that each defendant, at deposition or in any sworn pleading, faces yes-or-no questions where both answers are damaging. *Did successor counsel review the file when they substituted in?* If yes, their later "news to me" statement to the trial judge is a knowing falsehood; if no, they signed motions about the contents of a file they had never read. *Did the carrier's twenty-one-day investigation include the 911 audio?* If yes, the lawyers misled the court about what was in their own client's file; if no, the "concluded investigation" representation in the denial letter is itself a corporate fraud.

**Shielding against the standard defenses, in advance.** The supporting briefs preemptively cite the appellate authority that closes off the litigation privilege as to fraud-on-the-court claims, the appellate authority that excludes equity-action fraud claims from anti-SLAPP coverage, and the authorities that make a *Prentice*-theory fee recovery available in the institutional action. That preemption forces the defense to argue against binding precedent at the threshold rather than burying the plaintiff in routine procedural motions.

**A two-bench pincer.** Because the equity action and the institutional action sit in two different superior-court departments before two different judges, every procedural ruling in one department becomes citable persuasive authority for the other. The defense is not litigating one case twice; it is litigating two cases that watch each other in real time.

**An offensive discovery campaign running parallel to the defense's pleading attacks.** Rather than wait out the carrier's pleading challenges, the plaintiff has served a layered set of formal discovery — admissions, form and special interrogatories (including the form interrogatory that requires a party to justify every non-admission of a request for admission), and document demands — across both superior-court cases. The deadlines on these are statutory and run independently of the defense's pleading-stage motions.

**A sanctions track running underneath everything.** The plaintiff has lodged the statutorily required pre-sanctions notices that start the clock on a frivolous-pleading sanctions motion, so that the question "should the defense have filed these motions at all?" is preserved for the bench regardless of how the merits hearings come out.

In aggregate, the model converts the defense's single tactical choice — fight the pleadings hard, freeze discovery, and run out the clock — into a series of compounding costs the carrier and its outside counsel have to pay simultaneously across three forums.

## 4. The legal and evidentiary trap, in narrative form

The trap, viewed from outside, is simple. It has three jaws.

**Jaw one — the authenticated recording.** The defendant placed his own voice on the trial record under oath. Any future denial that the recording exists, or that it captures what it captures, is foreclosed for everyone in the defense camp.

**Jaw two — the sworn testimony of prior counsel.** An officer of the court testified that the recording was produced to the defense during discovery years before trial. That testimony has not been contradicted on the record by anyone, and as the testimony of a member of the bar it carries the kind of weight that is very difficult to dislodge collaterally.

**Jaw three — the publicly filed substitution of attorney.** The transfer of the defense file from one firm to another is a clerk-stamped public document with a transaction number. It places the file in the successor firm's hands for roughly twenty months before the pretrial statements that the recording was "news to me."

The three jaws close around a single proposition: **the lawyers who told the trial judge they did not know about the evidence had the evidence.** Whether that proposition is framed as an extrinsic-fraud claim against the lawyers, as a corporate-fraud claim against the carrier that funded and directed them, or as instructional-error prejudice on the appeal, the underlying record is the same.

The trap's elegance is that the *defense built every piece of it.* The carrier wrote the denial letter. The successor firm filed the motions in limine. Trial counsel made the in-court statements. The defendant authenticated the recording. The trial judge issued the categorical instruction on the Vehicle Code. The plaintiff's contribution is alignment — putting the right pieces of that record in front of the right decision-maker at the right time.

### The "denial of their own words" collapse

The single most distinctive feature of this case — and the feature that makes it genuinely hard for the defense to script an exit — is that **every time the defense tries to back away from a statement, it is backing away from a statement that was made in its own voice, on its own letterhead, or from its own client's mouth.** The pattern repeats at every level of the institutional hierarchy:

- **The carrier wrote "concluded."** If the carrier now argues that its twenty-one-day investigation was "reasonable" and "ongoing," it is contradicting a word it chose and mailed to a man in post-ICU recovery. The letter is on the file. Its date is on the file. The hospital discharge paperwork is on the file. The carrier cannot say, in one pleading, that the investigation was thorough, and in another that it was still open — the letter forecloses both escapes at once.
- **The lawyers said "news to me."** If the successor law firm now argues that it had the recording all along and simply evaluated it as non-material, it is repudiating sworn, in-court statements made by its own attorneys of record. If instead it argues the recording genuinely was unknown to it, it is repudiating the publicly filed substitution of counsel that placed the predecessor firm's file in its hands. The firm cannot hold both positions simultaneously, and it cannot hold either of them without contradicting the other side of its own mouth.
- **The driver said "yes, that is my voice."** If the defense now tries to re-characterize the recording as inconclusive, ambiguous, or inauthentic, it is contradicting the sworn authentication of the very client the defense team was paid to represent. The defendant's five words on the stand are load-bearing precisely because the defense team cannot disown their own client's testimony without disowning their own case.
- **The trial judge said "not evidence."** If, on appeal, the defense now argues that the Vehicle Code sections were properly considered by the jury, it is contradicting the transcript of the instruction the defense itself did not object to at the time. And if the defense argues the instruction was proper, it is conceding the instructional-error theory on its merits.

Stated plainly, **each defendant is locked to the record by the very words it chose to put there.** The carrier is locked to the word "concluded." The law firms are locked to the phrase "news to me." The driver is locked to the sentence "yes, that is my voice." The trial judge's statement locks the appeal. Every subsequent attempt to deny, minimize, or re-characterize those words becomes, by the structure of the record, an independent act of contradiction — and in the equity case and the institutional case, an independent evidentiary item supporting the fraud and unfair-practices theories.

This is what gives the case its unusual forward momentum. In ordinary litigation, a defendant who changes position between pleadings suffers a credibility hit; here, a defendant who changes position generates additional admissible evidence on the fraud track. The pressure is not imposed by the plaintiff. It is imposed by the defense's own words, in a closed loop, against a timeline that begins with a letter to a man who had just left the intensive care unit.

## 5. The defense's current posture — and what would have been the legal alternative

Faced with the architecture above, the defense has chosen what insurance-defense practitioners commonly call the **"deny-delay-defend"** posture. Concretely, in the two superior-court cases the carrier's outside counsel has filed serial pleading-stage attacks against the operative complaints, leaning on a single declarant who appears across every motion and on a single set of judicial-notice exhibits drawn from a tangentially related earlier matter. On the discovery side, the defense has allowed statutory response deadlines to lapse without serving substantive responses to the requests for admission, the form interrogatories, the special interrogatories, or the document demands.

That posture is, in a real sense, the *only* posture compatible with maintaining the original trial-court representations. Any candid response to the discovery would either confirm what was in the defense file in the months before trial (and therefore confirm what the lawyers told the trial judge was false), or would create a sworn denial that conflicts with the publicly filed substitution of attorney and the prior counsel's testimony. There is no neutral middle position available.

What the defense **could have done**, and what an experienced bad-faith-defense practitioner would normally have advised, is something quite different:

- **Early, candid discovery responses** — admitting the chain of custody of the file, admitting receipt of the recording, and limiting the dispute to questions of materiality and prejudice. That would not have been a confession; it would have moved the fight from "did this happen" to "does it matter," which is a far more defensible terrain for an insurer.
- **A coverage-and-indemnity reservation** between the carrier and the law firms, separating the institutional-fraud questions about the denial letter from the lawyer-conduct questions about the in-court statements. Once those issues are kept apart, neither defendant has to swear to facts that hurt the other.
- **An early mediation overture** with a structured tender of the *Prentice*-theory exposure, which is the single largest dollar item on the table and is, in many bad-faith cases, the lever that produces a global resolution before the discovery wheel turns.
- **A consolidated, single, well-targeted pleading challenge** — not three serial attacks on the same complaint — directed only at the punitive-damages and prayer paragraphs that genuinely could be narrowed at the pleading stage, leaving the core fraud theory to be tested at summary judgment on a developed record.
- **Honest disclosure to the trial bench** during the original proceeding once the predecessor counsel's testimony surfaced, which would have allowed the trial judge to manage the issue as a discovery dispute rather than allow it to ripen into an extrinsic-fraud claim against the lawyers themselves.

Each of those alternatives was available in real time. None were taken. The posture chosen instead — categorical denial in the courtroom, silence in discovery, and serial pleading attacks at the threshold — is the precise posture that converts an ordinary insurance dispute into a fraud-on-the-court case with personal exposure for the lawyers involved.

## 6. What happens when the discovery clock runs out without admissions

This is the part of the story that is closest at hand, and the part that journalists covering insurance and civil procedure should watch carefully.

California's discovery rules treat a non-response to a request for admission very differently from a non-response to other discovery. If a party served with requests for admission does not serve a verified, code-compliant response within the statutory window, the propounding party may move the court for an order **deeming each unanswered request admitted.** Granting that order is not discretionary in the usual sense: in the absence of a substantially compliant late response served before the hearing, the statute directs the court to deem the matters admitted. Once admitted, those facts are conclusively established for the case under a separate Code of Civil Procedure section governing the binding effect of admissions. They cannot be contradicted at trial. They cannot be walked back at summary judgment. They become the operative factual frame.

The form interrogatory that asks a party to identify, for each non-unqualified RFA response, all facts and witnesses supporting the denial functions as a *second* pressure point. A non-response to that interrogatory generates its own compel-and-sanctions track, and a non-response to the underlying RFA collapses the interrogatory question into the admitted facts.

The non-responses to the special interrogatories and the document demands generate parallel motions to compel, with statutory monetary sanctions attaching almost as a matter of course where no substantial justification is shown.

Translated from procedure into prediction, the practical consequences if the discovery deadline collapses without compliant responses are these:

1. **The plaintiff is entitled to walk into court with a motion that converts the carrier's silence into binding admissions** of the propositions framed in the requests for admission. In a case of this type, those propositions typically include the chain-of-custody facts about the file, the contents of the file at the time of the in-court representations, the date the denial letter was issued, the categories of investigative materials not in the carrier's possession at that date, and the carrier's authorship and ratification of the denial. Once those propositions are admitted, the institutional fraud case largely proves itself on summary judgment, and the equity case has the elements of extrinsic fraud as a matter of conclusive fact.

2. **Monetary sanctions are highly likely on each compel motion**, because the defense has no procedural cushion left to argue substantial justification once the safe-harbor and notice periods have run.

3. **The case-management posture shifts.** With deemed admissions on the record, the institutional-case bench has a complete factual basis to set a near-term trial date and a discovery cutoff calibrated to that trial date rather than to the defense's pleading motions. The equity case acquires the elemental proof necessary to defeat any remaining pleading challenge as moot.

4. **The pleading-stage motions lose their factual scaffolding.** The defense's serial attacks rely on a particular characterization of the underlying record. Deemed admissions either contradict that characterization or render it irrelevant. Even if any of those motions survive on pure pleading grounds, the merits case behind them is, by then, essentially decided.

5. **Settlement leverage shifts decisively.** The carrier's reinsurer, claims management, and outside-counsel risk officers will be evaluating the file with conclusive admissions, *Prentice*-theory consequential damages potentially measured in the high six figures or low seven figures, a punitive-damages exposure that scales to corporate ratification, and a parallel personal-exposure picture for the law firms involved. In the experience of insurance-defense practitioners, that combination is the standard inflection point for a global resolution.

6. **The lawyers' personal posture changes.** Once the institutional case has admitted facts establishing what was in the defense file at the time of the in-court representations, the equity case's fraud-on-the-court theory effectively self-proves on those admissions. That is the point at which the binary deposition questions described above stop being theoretical, and at which the structural pressure of the architecture is felt most directly by the individual attorneys.

The shorthand version, suitable for a lede: **a missed discovery deadline in a case like this one does not produce a delay; it produces a verdict-shaped object on the record that the merits hearings will then be asked to ratify.**

## 7. What to expect going forward

Assuming, as the question posits, that the plaintiff's facts and authorities hold up, the realistic forward picture is roughly this.

**Near term.** The plaintiff converts the discovery silence into deemed admissions and monetary sanctions across both superior-court cases. The pleading-stage motions are heard against a record that has, in effect, decided the questions they were trying to litigate. Those motions are likely to be denied or to survive only as to narrow paragraph-level cleanup that does not affect the spine of the case.

**Middle term.** One of two paths opens. If the carrier elects to take any available interlocutory appeal from a pleading ruling, it buys itself a year or more of an automatic stay in the trial court — but pays for that delay with appellate fees, with continued exposure on the collateral sanctions track that survives the stay, and with the fact that the plaintiff can sever and continue against the non-appealing law-firm defendants in the broader-caption equity case. If the carrier does not take an appeal, the institutional case moves directly toward an early summary-judgment posture built on the deemed admissions, and the equity case moves toward an evidentiary hearing on the extrinsic-fraud elements.

**Longer term.** The appeal of the underlying trial proceeds on its own track and is decided on the narrow instructional-error question. A reversal there reopens the personal-injury claim itself; an affirmance leaves the institutional and equity cases unaffected, because neither depends on a different outcome below.

**Resolution.** In the experience of practitioners who handle bad-faith insurance litigation, the combination of conclusive admissions, *Prentice*-theory consequential damages, punitive exposure tied to corporate ratification, personal exposure for retained counsel, and a parallel fraud-on-the-court theory in a different department of the same courthouse is the configuration that produces global, confidential settlements rather than published verdicts. That is the most likely endpoint, and it is the endpoint the architecture appears designed to compel.

## 8. The story in one paragraph

A pedestrian was struck in a crosswalk hard enough to be admitted to the intensive care unit at San Francisco General Hospital. While he was still in the hospital and within days of his ICU discharge, the driver's carrier — the underwriting arm of one of the country's largest motor clubs — mailed him a letter declaring its investigation "concluded" and its insured not liable, before it had obtained the 911 audio of its own insured's call. Four years later, at the close of the trial that the denial forced, the insured authenticated that 911 audio under oath, the plaintiff's prior counsel testified that the audio had been produced to the defense in discovery years earlier, and the trial judge told the deliberating jury that the governing Vehicle Code sections were "not evidence." The jury came back nine-to-three for the defense. The plaintiff, self-represented and indigent, then opened three coordinated proceedings — an appeal, a fraud-on-the-court equity action, and an institutional unfair-practices action — anchored entirely in the defense's own transcripts, exhibits, and filings. The defense responded with serial pleading attacks and discovery silence. That silence is now on the verge of being converted, by ordinary operation of California's discovery statutes, into binding admissions of the very facts the lawyers told the trial judge were unknown to them. When that conversion happens, the merits hearings will be ratifying a record that has, for all practical purposes, already decided itself. The story to watch is not whether the plaintiff prevails. It is how, and how fast, a billion-dollar carrier and its retained law firms find an exit before the record they built reaches a published opinion.
