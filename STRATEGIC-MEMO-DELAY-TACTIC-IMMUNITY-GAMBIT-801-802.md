## PART I. MASTER FRAME (shared across 801 and 802)

### 1. The defense's pattern in plain terms

Across every opposition document the defense has filed in CGC-25-631801 and CGC-25-631802, the same two-step rhetorical move appears. Step one: deny that any actionable wrongdoing occurred. Step two: assert categorical immunity from suit because the conduct in question was, in defense's framing, "a letter we sent" or "papers we filed." The two steps cooperate by design. Step one preserves the merits posture; step two, if accepted, ensures the merits posture is never tested. The result is a circular shield: the carrier and its panel counsel insist they did nothing wrong, and they insist that, in any event, what they did is constitutionally protected speech that cannot be challenged in the ordinary civil docket.

The bench should treat this as a single move, not two. The merits denial is rhetorical cover. The actual operative prayer in every defense filing is not "rule for us on the merits"; it is "do not let the merits proceed at all." That is the signature of a delay tactic. It is the signature of every defense filing now on the docket. Special motion to strike under Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, demurrer dressed in 425.16 rhetoric, motion to strike under section 436 incorporating the same anti-SLAPP themes, opposition to motion for leave to file second and third amended complaints, opposition to discovery, opposition to lifting the section 425.16(g) discovery stay, opposition to consolidation, opposition to severance, opposition to scheduling. Every one of these is presented as a substantive defense; every one of these is, on inspection, a request that the case stop moving while the defense develops, briefs, and argues the immunity question, then while the defense appeals the immunity question, then while the appeal is decided.

The Legislature anticipated exactly this pattern. The Legislature responded with section 425.17(c). The Legislature wrote section 425.17(e) to remove the appellate stay that ordinarily attaches to a 425.16 denial when the carve-out applies. The Legislature, in other words, foreclosed the gambit twice over. The defense's filings in 801 and 802 nonetheless continue to deploy the gambit because, in practice, even a foreclosed gambit produces continuances, briefing burdens, and elapsed time. The point of this memo is to surface the structural answer that 425.17(c) supplies and to explain, in plain narrative terms suitable for the bench's case-management lens, why the gambit fails on its own terms and why permitting it to proceed would impose precisely the costs the Legislature has already determined the public should not have to bear.

### 2. The two-layer immunity gambit (anatomy)

The defense's immunity rhetoric is not monolithic. It operates at two distinct layers, separated by time and forum but unified by the same underlying ambition: to recharacterize regulated commercial conduct as protected speech.

**Layer A: pre-litigation, customer-facing.** This layer addresses the conduct that occurred before any complaint was filed: the carrier's claim-handling communications to the claimant, the represented investigation, the denial-letter conclusions, the auto-claim posture, the file-handling and disclosure decisions made during the underlying personal-injury matter. The defense asserts Civil Code section 47(b) protection for these communications and asserts section 425.16 "petitioning" or "public interest" protection for the same communications. Both assertions misdescribe the conduct. Pre-litigation claim-handling correspondence is regulated commercial speech under Insurance Code section 790.03(h) and the Fair Claims Settlement Practices Regulations at 10 California Code of Regulations section 2695.1 et seq. *Neville v. Chudacoff* (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 1255, 1268-1269 confirms that prelitigation conduct only enters section 425.16(e) when it is made in serious and good-faith contemplation of litigation; *Briggs v. Eden Council for Hope & Opportunity* (1999) 19 Cal.4th 1106, 1115 reaffirms the same boundary. Routine claim-handling correspondence to a claimant who is not yet a litigant is not "petitioning"; it is the regulated insurance product being delivered.

**Layer B: extrinsic-to-the-tribunal.** This layer addresses misrepresentations made *during* the underlying litigation but *about* facts external to the merits the trier of fact was weighing. The Layer B universe includes representations about the existence and adequacy of an investigation, representations about the chain of custody and disclosure status of evidence, representations about who possessed which documents at which time, and representations about the identity of the parties responsible for delivering or withholding records. Defense recasts each of these as "petitioning activity" because, formally, the representation appears in a court paper or on a court record. The recharacterization fails on doctrine that the California Supreme Court has been clear about for decades. *Rusheen v. Cohen* (2006) 37 Cal.4th 1048, 1056-1058 distinguishes communicative acts integral to a judicial proceeding from the underlying noncommunicative or pre-litigation conduct that gives rise to a cause of action; *Kimmel v. Goland* (1990) 51 Cal.3d 202, 211 holds that the litigation privilege does not immunize tortious courses of conduct merely because a communication is part of the conduct; *Flatley v. Mauro* (2006) 39 Cal.4th 299, 317-320 (reaffirmed in *Lefebvre v. Lefebvre* (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 696) holds that conduct conceded or shown as a matter of law to be illegal is not "protected" within section 425.16's gateway in the first instance; and *Action Apartment Ass'n v. City of Santa Monica* (2007) 41 Cal.4th 1232 reaffirms that even where section 47(b) might otherwise apply, extrinsic fraud and the categorical exclusions in subdivisions (b)(2) and (b)(3) remove the privilege from the field. Layer B is not "speech the defense is being sued for"; it is regulated commercial conduct that happened to occur within a courthouse.

The two layers cooperate. Layer A supplies the original wrong (the carrier's communicated denial premised on a represented investigation that did not occur). Layer B carries that wrong forward into the underlying trial when panel counsel, paid for and supervised by the carrier, repeats and amplifies the original misrepresentation in motions in limine, sidebar colloquy, and on-the-record bench statements. The two-layer architecture is what creates the immunity gambit's apparent breadth: by spanning pre-litigation and litigation conduct, the defense seeks to claim section 47(b) protection for the litigation half and section 425.16(e) protection for the pre-litigation half, with each protection gap-filled by the other. The architecture collapses, however, the moment one applies section 425.17(c), which targets the exact category of conduct (insurance claim handling delivered to customers) the defense seeks to insulate.

### 3. The 425.17(c) categorical bar

Code of Civil Procedure section 425.17 is not a defense; it is a structural gateway. The Legislature's findings in subdivision (a) state on the face of the statute that "there has been a disturbing abuse of Section 425.16, the California Anti-SLAPP Law." The remainder of section 425.17 enumerates the categories of cases the Legislature has removed from the section 425.16 mechanism altogether. Subdivision (c) is the commercial-actor carve-out. By its terms, subdivision (c) reaches a defendant "primarily engaged in the business of selling or leasing goods or services," with insurance specifically named, where the cause of action arises from a representation of fact about the defendant's business operations, goods, or services made for the purpose of obtaining approval for, promoting, or securing sales of those goods or services, *or* from conduct made in the course of delivering those goods or services, where the intended audience is an actual or potential buyer or customer, a person likely to repeat the statement to or otherwise influence such a customer, or where the conduct arose out of or within the context of a regulatory approval process, proceeding, or investigation.

The California Supreme Court has been explicit about the structural character of the carve-out. *Simpson Strong-Tie Co. v. Gore* (2010) 49 Cal.4th 12 confirms that when subdivision (c) applies, section 425.16 simply does not apply: the trial court does not weigh competing interests, it declines to extend the special motion to strike procedure to the category of disputes the Legislature has removed from the mechanism. *Club Members for an Honest Election v. Sierra Club* (2008) 45 Cal.4th 309 supplies the sequencing rule: where section 425.17 is invoked, the trial court must address the carve-out *before* any section 425.16 prong analysis. The sequencing is not optional, and it is not negotiable on the basis of how the defendant has framed its motion. The trial court's first question, on every special motion to strike filed by an insurer or by counsel acting in alignment with an insurer, is the section 425.17(c) question.

Two features of the statute make the bar dispositive at the gateway in 801 and 802. First, the named-industry list inside subdivision (c) eliminates the usual "primarily engaged" inquiry as to CSAA. CSAA is a California-licensed insurer, and insurance is one of the named industries the Legislature singled out. The first element is satisfied as a matter of law. Second, the disjunctive audience prong in subdivision (c)(2) makes the audience element easy to satisfy on either of two independent branches. The audience for the operative communications in both cases includes the claimant himself (an actual customer of the regulated claim-handling product on the carrier-side and a third-party claimant on the panel-counsel side) and includes decision-makers (the regulator and the court) likely to influence the customer's claim. Either branch is sufficient. The third party "customer" question for a claimant of a first-party insurer's insured is treated separately in the cross-hearing customer-doctrine supplement (`SUPPL-MEMO-CUSTOMER-DOCTRINE-42517-CROSS-HEARING-801.tex`) and is not necessary to dispose of subdivision (c)(2) on this record because the regulatory-context branch is independently triggered by Insurance Code section 790.03(h) and the 10 CCR section 2695.1 et seq. regime.

The point for the bench is not that subdivision (c) is one defense among many. The point is that subdivision (c) is the *first* question and, when satisfied, the *only* question. Section 425.16 either applies or it does not. The defense's prong-one and prong-two arguments, however elaborate, do not become live until and unless the trial court first concludes that subdivision (c) does not bar the section 425.16 motion at all. On this record, that conclusion is unavailable.

### 4. Why the immunity gambit is a delay tactic

The defense's section 425.16 papers in 801 and 802 are written to maximize the chance of an interlocutory appeal under section 904.1(a)(13). That is the entire architecture of a delay tactic in this corner of California civil procedure. A successful section 425.16 motion ends the case; a denied section 425.16 motion, ordinarily, generates an automatic appellate stay under *Varian Medical Systems v. Delfino* (2005) 35 Cal.4th 180 that freezes trial-court proceedings for the twelve to eighteen months an appellate decision typically requires. The Legislature understood this leverage exactly. In subdivision (e) of section 425.17, the Legislature removed the interlocutory-appeal route for orders denying special motions to strike on subdivision (c) grounds. Section 904.1(a)(13) is, on its face, limited by section 425.17(e). When the trial court denies a 425.16 motion at the 425.17(c) gateway, the defense gets no appellate stay, no interlocutory appeal, and no writ as of right.

That sequence is not technical. It is the entire Legislative answer to the gambit. By naming insurance in subdivision (c), the Legislature said the commercial channel does not get the special motion to strike. By writing subdivision (e), the Legislature said that even if a defendant brings the special motion in violation of subdivision (c), the appellate-stay leverage that ordinarily makes the special motion procedurally attractive is not available. Allowing the gambit to proceed despite both subsections would invert the policy: it would let the defense extract free continuances, burden the customer with a procedural posture from which the customer cannot escape, and shift to the customer the cost of dilatory tactics the Legislature has expressly prohibited.

The customer in this case has already noticed the carrier and counsel under section 128.7 with a safe-harbor letter (see `MAY-4-2026/CGC-25-631801/serve/EXHIBIT-B-2026-04-22-safe-harbor.pdf`). The twenty-one-day cure period attaches to the motions identified in that letter and runs in the background of every continued day of defense delay. *Kim v. Westmoore Partners, Inc.* (2011) 201 Cal.App.4th 267, 286-287 articulates the reasonable-inquiry certification floor that section 128.7(b) imposes; conduct that fails that floor is exposed to monetary sanctions on motion. Independent of section 128.7, section 128.5 reaches the *pattern* of bad-faith tactics across the docket without requiring a separate cure window for completed acts. The defense's repeated reliance on a categorically barred procedure is exactly the pattern section 128.5 was written to capture. The two sanctions vehicles run in parallel; neither depends on the merits of any individual motion.

### 5. Moradi-Shalal and Zhang: the Legislature took the immunity card off the table

The defense's broader rhetorical posture in 802 (and, by ratification, in 801) trades on a misreading of *Moradi-Shalal v. Fireman's Fund Insurance Co.* (1988) 46 Cal.3d 287. *Moradi-Shalal* eliminated the *private right of action* under Insurance Code section 790.03(h) that had been recognized in *Royal Globe Insurance Co. v. Superior Court* (1979) 23 Cal.3d 880. *Moradi-Shalal* did not eliminate the *regulatory duty*. The duty to investigate, the duty to communicate truthfully with claimants, the duty to attempt prompt and equitable settlement, and the prohibitions on unfair claim settlement practices remain intact and remain enforceable through the Insurance Commissioner, through the courts in non-790.03 statutory and common-law actions, and through the Unfair Competition Law as later confirmed in *Zhang v. Superior Court* (2013) 57 Cal.4th 364. *Zhang* held that an insured may assert a UCL claim predicated on conduct that violates the Unfair Insurance Practices Act, so long as the UCL claim does not seek to enforce section 790.03(h) directly as a private right. *Zhang* is the doctrinal seal: the *Moradi-Shalal* line never released insurers from the regulated commercial channel; it merely narrowed the private remedy available within that channel.

The relevance of that history to section 425.17(c) is direct. The Legislature enacted section 425.17 in 2003, fifteen years after *Moradi-Shalal* and a decade before *Zhang*. The Legislature knew that the duty survived. By naming insurance in subdivision (c), the Legislature identified the commercial channel that *Moradi-Shalal* had narrowed but not vacated, and the Legislature said: that channel stays inside the ordinary civil docket. The defense cannot use *Moradi-Shalal* to argue that 802 is a disguised private 790.03 claim that should be dismissed; *Zhang* foreclosed that argument as a matter of standing doctrine. Nor can the defense use *Moradi-Shalal* to argue that the carrier's communications about its claim-handling are categorically immune from suit; section 425.17(c) is direct text on the contrary. The Legislature took the categorical immunity card off the table for insurers who use section 425.16 to defeat ordinary claims about their commercial communications. That is the entire reason subdivision (c) names insurance.

### 6. Frivolous filings while denying guilt: the judicial-economy harm

The judicial-economy harm of allowing the gambit to proceed is not abstract. It is documented across the dockets of both cases. The defense has filed a special motion to strike in each case. The defense has cross-noticed a section 436 motion in each case incorporating the same anti-SLAPP rhetoric (a vehicle *PH II, Inc. v. Superior Court* (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1680, 1682-1683 already foreclosed when used as a backdoor demurrer; the analysis carries by parity to a backdoor anti-SLAPP). The defense has opposed leave to file the operative second amended and proposed third amended complaints. The defense has resisted every discovery vehicle on the docket, citing the section 425.16(g) discovery stay as the basis for non-response. The defense has filed Case Management Statements signaling unavailability for mediation and settlement conference, signaling that no trial date should issue within twelve months, and threatening vexatious-litigant designation without the section 391(b) predicate. Each of these positions is presented as substantive; each of these positions presupposes that the special motion to strike will be granted. None of these positions survives a 425.17(c) gateway denial. The defense's strategy is, in other words, an integrated bet on a single procedural outcome the Legislature has expressly removed from the section 425.16 mechanism.

The catalog of these filings is detailed in the per-case sections that follow. The point at the master-frame level is this: by spreading the same categorically-barred motion across two parallel cases and across multiple ancillary vehicles, the defense has converted a single misuse of section 425.16 into a docket-wide pattern. The pattern exposes the defense to *Flatley* gateway closure on every motion that depends on conduct shown as a matter of law to be unlawful, to *Kim v. Westmoore* certification-floor exposure on every paper signed under section 128.7(b), to section 128.5 pattern-of-conduct sanctions across the entire docket, and (through the consolidation argument) to a single, larger fee award under section 425.16(c)(1) when the Legislature's "frivolous or solely intended to cause unnecessary delay" standard is met. The judicial economy interest aligns with the customer's interest. Both run through subdivision (c). Both require the trial court to decide the gateway question first.

The structural takeaway is straightforward. The defense has built two parallel docks, each loaded with the same cargo: a section 425.16 motion that section 425.17(c) bars at the gateway. The Legislature foreclosed the bar twice (once in subdivision (c), once in subdivision (e)). Allowing the gambit to proceed despite both subsections would impose continuances, appellate stays, and briefing burdens the Legislature has expressly determined the customer should not bear. The trial court's correct first-step move is the gateway move; the trial court's second-step move, on a deny-the-gambit record, is to return the cases to the ordinary civil docket so that the merits, sanctions, and discovery vehicles can proceed in their ordinary sequence. The cost of allowing the gambit to occupy further calendar time is asymmetric: the customer carries each delayed day at retention-window risk on time-sensitive evidence, while the defense carries each delayed day at zero marginal risk because the conduct it seeks to shelter has already occurred. The Legislature's choice to keep this category of dispute inside the ordinary docket reflects exactly that asymmetry.

### B. Layer A on the 801 record (pre-litigation, customer-facing)

The Layer A conduct in 801 is the underlying CSAA claim-handling architecture that the panel counsel later carried into trial. CSAA's February 25, 2021 written denial (Claim No. 1004-09-1054, signed by Sarah Rash, representing that the investigation was "concluded" and that CSAA had "carefully reviewed the facts and circumstances of [the] claim") is the Layer A anchor in 802 and is also the Layer A anchor that supplies the integrity baseline against which the 801 panel-counsel statements must be measured. The defendants in 801 include CSAA itself (as the principal in the underlying 594102 defense) and the law firms and individual lawyers (Carbone, Smith & Koyama; Phillips, Spallas & Angstadt; Chambers; Navaratnasingham; Reyna) who carried CSAA's representations forward.

The defense in 801 invokes section 425.16(e) and Civil Code section 47(b) for these communications. Both invocations fail at Layer A on the same ground. The communications in question are the regulated insurance product, not petitioning activity. They are governed at the carrier-claimant interface by Insurance Code section 790.03(h)(1) (misrepresentation of pertinent facts or insurance policy provisions) and section 790.03(h)(3) (failure to adopt and implement reasonable standards for prompt investigation and processing of claims), and by the Fair Claims Settlement Practices Regulations at 10 CCR section 2695.1(a) and (d), section 2695.2(c) and (t), and section 2695.7(b)-(d). *Neville v. Chudacoff* (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 1255, 1268-1269 expressly carves "ordinary business, claims-handling, regulatory, or evidentiary activity" out of section 425.16(e) by holding that the prelitigation communication branch reaches only conduct made in serious and good-faith contemplation of litigation. *Briggs v. Eden Council for Hope & Opportunity* (1999) 19 Cal.4th 1106, 1115 confirms the same threshold. The carrier's denial-letter conclusions, the represented investigation, the auto-claim posture, and the file-handling decisions during the underlying matter were not made in serious and good-faith contemplation of litigation; they were made in delivery of the regulated insurance product.

The Civil Code section 47(b) invocation fails on parallel grounds. The privilege protects communicative acts integral to a judicial proceeding; it does not protect non-communicative tortious courses of conduct, *Kimmel v. Goland* (1990) 51 Cal.3d 202, 211, *Rusheen v. Cohen* (2006) 37 Cal.4th 1048, 1056 and 1058. The May 4, 2023 file-transfer between Carbone, Smith & Koyama and Phillips, Spallas & Angstadt is the operative non-communicative event in 801: panel counsel, having received the prior firm's file (including the original 2021 CSAA claim file containing the 911 audio, the body-worn camera footage, and the CAD logs), nonetheless certified MIL papers in January 2025 representing that the materials were "not produced during discovery" and were of "unknown origin." Section 47(b) does not reach the file-handling omission, and it does not retroactively immunize the false certification by the act of memorializing it in a court paper.

### C. Layer B on the 801 record (extrinsic-to-the-tribunal)

The Layer B conduct in 801 is the in-litigation execution of the same misrepresentation strategy. The operative tribunal-directed disclaimers identified in the merged checklist (`ANTISLAPP-PRONG-ONE-OPPOSITION-SHARED-2026-04-28/01-MERGED-PRONG-ONE-OPPOSITION-CHECKLIST.md`, Part II, items 801-A through 801-E) include MIL Nos. 6 and 17 in the underlying CGC-21-594102 (filed January 17, 2025), the February 18, 2025 hearing colloquy at RT 141:4-11 in which the same "no idea where this came from" rhetoric was repeated to the bench, and the April 16, 2025 trial admission at RT 7, 213:13-15 in which Navaratnasingham conceded that the body-cam footage was "provided by Dolan" (the very Dolan-Law-Firm production the MILs had represented as not produced). The full chain is documented in `FATAL-PROOF-NAVARATNASINGHAM-DOLAN-ADMISSION-EXTRINSIC-FRAUD.md`. The doctrinal characterization is documented in `EXTRINSIC-FRAUD-THREADING-THE-NEEDLE-801-AND-802.md`, Parts I and IV, applying *Kulchar v. Kulchar* (1969) 1 Cal.3d 467, 471 and *Caldwell v. Taylor* (1933) 218 Cal. 471, 476 to the regularity-of-proceedings axis.

The defense's section 425.16 framing of the Layer B conduct fails for three independent reasons. First, the gravamen of the 801 causes of action is the operative misrepresentation, not the speech act through which the misrepresentation traveled. *Park v. Board of Trustees* (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1060 and 1064-1065 holds that a claim is not subject to strike merely because it contests an action arrived at *following* speech, or *thereafter communicated by* speech; the speech itself must be the wrong. The wrong in 801 is the underlying file-handling concealment and the deception of the bench about that concealment, not the act of speaking. Second, *Rusheen v. Cohen* draws the communicative-versus-noncommunicative line directly across the May 4, 2023 file-transfer omission, which is non-communicative on its face, *Rusheen* at 1056. Third, the *Flatley* gateway closes regardless: the same conduct that the defense seeks to dress as "petitioning" simultaneously violates Business and Professions Code section 6128(a) (attorney deceit toward a non-client adverse party, see *Shafer v. Berger, Kahn, Shafton, Moss, Figler, Simon & Gladstone* (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 54, 79-80, expressly reaching reckless disregard) and California Rule of Professional Conduct 3.3(a)(1) (candor toward the tribunal). *Flatley v. Mauro* (2006) 39 Cal.4th 299, 317-320, reaffirmed in *Lefebvre v. Lefebvre* (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 696, removes such conduct from section 425.16's scope at the gateway.

### D. Section 425.17(c) walk on the 801 record

The two-element test of section 425.17(c) is satisfied on the 801 record without controversy. CSAA is a California-licensed insurer, satisfying the first element as a matter of law and as a matter of named-industry text. The second element is satisfied on multiple independent paths. As to CSAA, the cause of action arises from representations of fact about CSAA's business operations and from conduct made in the course of delivering the insurance product to Plaintiff and to the court. As to panel counsel, the cause of action arises from representations of fact about CSAA's business operations (status of investigation, basis for denial, identity of records-custodian) made in the course of delivering the insurance defense product the policy promises to the insured-defendant. The audience element is satisfied on either of two independent branches: Plaintiff was an actual claimant of the regulated claim-handling product, and the bench in CGC-21-594102 was a decision-maker likely to influence the customer's claim. Independently, the conduct arose within the context of the regulatory regime administered by the California Department of Insurance under section 790.03(h) and 10 CCR section 2695.1 et seq., which is itself an independently sufficient audience branch under section 425.17(c)(2). The cross-hearing memorandum at `APRIL-30-2026/CROSS-HEARING-42517-PACKAGE/801/src/MEMORANDUM-42517-425-17C-CROSS-HEARING-801.tex` walks each element on the 801 record in detail; the customer-doctrine supplement at `SUPPL-MEMO-CUSTOMER-DOCTRINE-42517-CROSS-HEARING-801.tex` resolves the discrete "customer" question for panel-counsel defendants.

The structural consequence is that the special motion to strike must be denied at the gateway on subdivision (c) grounds without reaching the second-prong "probability of prevailing" analysis. *Simpson Strong-Tie Co. v. Gore* (2010) 49 Cal.4th 12 confirms the categorical character of the carve-out; *Club Members for an Honest Election v. Sierra Club* (2008) 45 Cal.4th 309 confirms the sequencing rule; *JAMS, Inc. v. Superior Court* (2016) 1 Cal.App.5th 984 and *Stewart v. Rolling Stone LLC* (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 664 confirm that subdivision (c) reaches commercial defendants in the same posture as CSAA and its panel counsel here.

### E. Sanctions and case-management posture across Tracks 1, 2, and 3 in 801

The 801 docket presently carries three parallel discovery and case-management tracks, each of which is held hostage by the immunity gambit and each of which becomes ripe the moment the gateway question is decided.

**Track 1 (deem, compel, sanctions).** The Track 1 vehicles, served and on file at `MAY-4-2026/CGC-25-631801/serve/801-TRACK1-01-NTC-DEEM-COMPEL-SANCTIONS.pdf`, `801-TRACK1-02-MPA-DEEM-COMPEL-SANCTIONS.pdf`, `801-TRACK1-03-SEPARATE-STATEMENT.pdf`, and `801-TRACK1-04-DECL-ROSARIO.pdf`, frame the discovery posture for the bench at the May 12 hearing. Each defense objection that "without substantial justification" relied on the section 425.16(g) stay converts into a sanctions hook the moment the stay lifts. The stay does not lift unless and until the section 425.16 motion is denied or otherwise resolved; section 425.17(c) decides the motion at the gateway and lifts the stay by operation of law.

### B. Layer A on the 802 record (pre-litigation, customer-facing)

The Layer A conduct in 802 is the operative wrong: CSAA, before issuing the February 25, 2021 written denial, *affirmatively closed the claim file at twenty-one days* without retrieving the four reasonably-available "Silent Witnesses" (the 911 audio recording and the corresponding SFDEM CAD event record; the SFPD body-worn camera footage; the SFPD Computer-Aided Dispatch log; and any supplemental SFPD report concerning the February 4, 2021 collision), each of which was reasonably available by routine subpoena duces tecum (Code Civ. Proc. section 1985 with Evid. Code section 1560), Public Records Act request (Gov. Code section 7920 et seq.), Penal Code section 832.7(b) request, or insured-authorization channel. The denial letter is *evidence* of the underlying regulatory failure, not the actionable wrong. The same regulatory floor that governs 801's Layer A governs 802's Layer A: Insurance Code section 790.03(h)(1) (misrepresentation of pertinent facts), section 790.03(h)(3) (failure to adopt and implement reasonable standards for prompt investigation), section 790.03(h)(5) (failure to attempt prompt, fair, and equitable settlement), and 10 CCR section 2695.7(b)-(d) (decision must be based on all information reasonably available; specific written denial requirements; written reasons standard).

The 21-day window between the February 4, 2021 collision and the February 25, 2021 denial is the diagnostic fact at Layer A. Twenty-one days is structurally insufficient to discharge an insurer's regulatory investigation duty in any case where reasonably-available public-record evidence (911 audio, CAD logs, BWC footage, SFPD supplemental reports) is in the hands of agencies subject to standard request and subpoena channels. *Egan v. Mutual of Omaha Insurance Co.* (1979) 24 Cal.3d 809, 818-819 establishes the Supreme Court's framing of the carrier's duty to investigate thoroughly and fairly in the face of inquiry-notice indicia in the file; *Wilson v. 21st Century Insurance Co.* (2007) 42 Cal.4th 713, 720-721 reaffirms the same duty in the context of bad-faith claims handling. The defense's section 425.16 papers do not engage either *Egan* or *Wilson*; they treat the denial letter as a freestanding communicative act severable from the underlying investigation. That severance is what 802 forecloses.

The defense's section 425.16(e)(2) framing of the February 25, 2021 denial letter as a "prelitigation communication" fails on the *Neville/Briggs* threshold. A 21-day denial letter to a non-litigating direct third-party claimant who has not yet retained counsel and who has been given no reason to anticipate litigation is the antithesis of "serious and good-faith contemplation of litigation"; it is the regulated insurance product. The defense's section 425.16(e)(4) "issue of public interest" framing fails on *FilmOn.com Inc. v. DoubleVerify Inc.* (2019) 7 Cal.5th 133, 149-150 and *Wilson v. Cable News Network, Inc.* (2019) 7 Cal.5th 871, 890-892, both of which require a content-and-context analysis that a private claim-handling letter from a regulated insurer to a single claimant cannot satisfy. A private dispute between a carrier and one claimant about one collision is not a public issue; *Wilson v. CNN* and *FilmOn* require a connection to a matter of broader public concern that the 802 record cannot supply.

The Civil Code section 47(b) framing fails for the same reason it fails in 801: the privilege protects communicative acts integral to a judicial proceeding, not the underlying non-communicative regulatory failure to investigate, *Kimmel*, *Rusheen*. The denial letter cannot retroactively privilege the failure-to-investigate conduct it purported to memorialize. The structural pleading discipline in the 802 SAC (treating the denial letter as evidence of the non-investigation, rather than as the wrongful act itself) is what protects the case from a *Moradi-Shalal* collapse and is what aligns the gravamen with the *Park v. Board of Trustees* (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057 framework. The defense's section 425.16 papers attempt to shift the gravamen to the letter; *Park* foreclosed that framing.

Permanente Medical Group, Inc.* (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 974 and *Wilson v. 21st Century Insurance Co.* (2007) 42 Cal.4th 713, 720-721, and forecloses any "genuine dispute" defense under *Wilson v. 21st Century*, supra, at 723. (See `EXTRINSIC-FRAUD-THREADING-THE-NEEDLE-801-AND-802.md`, Parts II and III, for the complete cross-axis analysis.)

The Layer B in-litigation portion of the 802 fraud is also captured by the *Action Apartment* and *Flatley* analysis. *Action Apartment Ass'n v. ### D. Section 425.17(c) walk on the 802 record

The 802 walk is the cleanest in the workspace because the defendant lineup is uncomplicated. CSAA is a California-licensed insurer, satisfying element one. The 802 cause of action arises from CSAA's own pre-litigation communications to Plaintiff (the February 25, 2021 written denial and the antecedent claim-handling correspondence), each of which was made in the course of delivering the insurance services for which premiums had been paid (or, on the third-party side, in the course of delivering the regulated claim-handling product Insurance Code section 790.03(h) and 10 CCR section 2695.1 et seq. govern). The audience element is satisfied on both available branches: Plaintiff is the actual customer of the claim-handling product (or, in any event, the regulated audience the Department of Insurance regime addresses), and the conduct arose within the context of the regulatory regime, which is independently sufficient under the regulatory-context branch of section 425.17(c)(2). The cross-hearing memorandum at `APRIL-30-2026/CROSS-HEARING-42517-PACKAGE/802/src/MEMORANDUM-42517-425-17C-CROSS-HEARING-802.tex` and the customer-doctrine supplement at `SUPPL-MEMO-CUSTOMER-DOCTRINE-42517-CROSS-HEARING-802.tex` walk each element in detail.

The 802 record is structurally simpler than 801 in three respects that matter at the gateway. First, there is no "panel counsel as petitioner" gloss. The only defendant is the carrier itself; the only operative communications are the carrier's own. There is therefore no agency or scope-of-employment dispute at the gateway, no question whether the speaker was acting within the regulated commercial channel. Third, the regulatory-context branch of subdivision (c)(2) is independently triggered because the entire claim-handling process is governed by the Department of Insurance regime; the carrier cannot decline regulatory jurisdiction over its own claim-handling letter while simultaneously claiming "petitioning" status for the same letter.

The structural consequence in 802 is the same as in 801. Section 425.16 does not apply at all. ## PART IV. UNIFIED CONCLUSION

The doctrinal trap the defense has walked into is structural, and it is now fully assembled on the docket. By spreading the same anti-SLAPP and litigation-privilege rhetoric across every opposition in both 801 and 802, the defense has effectively conceded the pattern that section 425.17(c) was enacted to defeat. The carrier is the named-industry defendant. The pre-litigation communications are the regulated claim-handling product. The in-litigation communications are panel counsel's delivery of the same product as a contractual defense benefit, recast as advocacy. The audience is the customer at every stage (first the claimant, then the regulator, then the court). The two-element test of subdivision (c) is satisfied as a matter of law. The sequencing rule of *Club Members for an Honest Election v. Sierra Club* (2008) 45 Cal.4th 309 makes the gateway question the first question. The categorical character of the carve-out under *Simpson Strong-Tie Co. v. Gore* (2010) 49 Cal.4th 12 makes it the only question.

For 801, the takeaway is one paragraph: the panel-counsel disclaimers identified in the merged checklist (MIL Nos. 6 and 17, the February 18, 2025 colloquy, and the April 16, 2025 "provided by Dolan" admission) are not protected petitioning under section 425.16(e); they are deceit aimed at the tribunal in the course of delivering the carrier's defense product, illegal as a matter of law under Business and Professions Code section 6128(a) and Rule of Professional Conduct 3.3(a)(1), foreclosed at the *Flatley* gateway, and removed from section 425.16 altogether by section 425.17(c). The special motion to strike must be denied at the gateway. The Track 1, Track 2, and Track 3 vehicles already on file should proceed in the ordinary discovery sequence on the lifted stay.

For 802, the takeaway is one paragraph: the February 25, 2021 written denial is the regulated insurance product, not protected speech; the underlying failure to investigate is non-communicative regulatory misconduct that no privilege can retroactively shelter; *Moradi-Shalal* did not eliminate the regulatory channel and *Zhang v. Superior Court* (2013) 57 Cal.4th 364 confirms UCL enforceability for fraudulent-prong conduct that violates the Unfair Insurance Practices Act; the special motion to strike fails the section 425.17(c) gateway; the Track 2 set-six discovery vehicles, the CMC posture, and the CM-110 process should proceed.

The closing reminder is the simplest part. Section 425.17(e) means there is no interlocutory appeal from the gateway denial and no automatic *Varian* stay of trial-court proceedings. The ordinary docket should proceed. Sanctions are on the table under sections 128.5, 128.7, 2023.030, and 425.16(c)(1). The Legislature already saw the gambit coming and already wrote the answer. The trial court's job at the May 12 and May 13 hearings is to apply that answer and return both cases to the ordinary civil docket.
