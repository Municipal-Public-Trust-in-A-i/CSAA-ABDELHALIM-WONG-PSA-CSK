# FIVE PRINCIPAL CALIFORNIA SUPREME COURT AUTHORITIES AUTHORIZING AMENDMENT TO ADD A CAUSE OF ACTION

## Why these five, and not Court-of-Appeal authorities

The Court of Appeal cases commonly cited — *Mabie v. Hyatt* (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 581; *Howard v. County of San Diego* (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1422; *Atkinson v. Elk Corp.* (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 739; *Morgan v. Superior Court* (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 527; *Kyle v. Carmon* (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th 901; *Kolani v. Gluska* (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 402; *Nguyen-Lam v. Cao* (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 858 — apply the liberality rule but do not originate it. The California Supreme Court is the source of the rule, and five decisions of that Court do the substantive work of authorizing an amendment that adds a new cause of action. Holding the argument on Supreme Court law alone raises the floor of the citation.

## The Five

### 1. *Klopstock v. Superior Court* (1941) 17 Cal.2d 13, 19–20

**Court:** California Supreme Court (Gibson, C.J., writing for the Court, with Shenk, Curtis, Edmonds, and Carter, JJ., concurring).

**Verbatim holding (17 Cal.2d at pp. 19–20):**

> "[This] statutory provision giving the courts the power to permit amendments in furtherance of justice has received a very liberal interpretation by the courts of this state. … This position is clearly in accord with the modern theories of code pleading, which would permit amendment in the discretion of the court unless an attempt is made to present an entirely different set of facts by way of the amendment. Nor is this liberal policy of permitting amendments in furtherance of justice any different merely because a general demurrer for failure to state a cause of action has been sustained." (*Klopstock*, 17 Cal.2d at pp. 19–20.)
>
> "[I]t is obvious that the unqualified way in which the rule is sometimes stated — i.e., that a new or different cause of action cannot be introduced by amendment — cannot be accepted. For the most common kinds of amendments are those in which complaints are amended that do not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; and in these, and often in the case of new parties, **a new cause of action is in fact for the first time introduced**. All that can be required, therefore … is that 'a wholly different cause of action' shall not be introduced … [T]he test is not whether under technical rules of pleading a new cause of action is introduced, but rather, the test is whether an attempt is made to state facts which give rise to a wholly distinct and different legal obligation against the defendant." (*Klopstock*, 17 Cal.2d at p. 20.) [Emphasis added.]

**Application to this case.** *Klopstock* is the dispositive answer to any objection that the SAC "adds new causes of action." The Supreme Court in 1941 directly rejected the unqualified form of that objection. It held that the only limit is a "wholly different cause of action" — meaning one premised on a "wholly distinct and different legal obligation." The SAC/TAC theories (clarified non-communicative gravamen, *Prentice* consequential damages, delayed-discovery particularization, *Moradi-Shalal* / *Zhang* channel preservation) all enforce the same legal obligation pleaded in the FAC: CSAA's alleged fraud and unfair business practices arising from its February 25, 2021 denial of Plaintiff's uninsured-motorist claim and the claim-handling conduct that followed. Under *Klopstock*, they are permissible amendments.

### 2. *Blank v. Kirwan* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318

**Court:** California Supreme Court (Mosk, J., writing for the Court, en banc).

**Verbatim holding (39 Cal.3d at p. 318):**

> "When a demurrer is … sustained without leave to amend, we decide whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment: if it can be, the trial court has abused its discretion and we reverse; if not, there has been no abuse of discretion and we affirm. … The burden of proving such reasonable possibility is squarely on the plaintiff." (*Blank v. Kirwan*, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318.) [Verified against the locally cached slip opinion.]

**Application to this case.** *Blank v. Kirwan* is the Supreme Court's modern statement of the rule that leave to amend must be granted where there is a reasonable possibility of cure. It is cited in substantially every published California decision reviewing demurrer or leave rulings since 1985, because the Supreme Court has never revised the formulation. On this record, the burden *Blank v. Kirwan* places on Plaintiff — showing a reasonable possibility of cure — is easily met: Plaintiff has already lodged the proposed SAC, and it materially strengthens the pleading. The defense's Opposition does not assert that the SAC cannot state a cause of action; its objection is procedural. *Blank v. Kirwan* does not permit that objection to defeat amendment at this posture.

**Note on substitution:** This authority replaces *Desny v. Wilder* (1956) 46 Cal.2d 715, which had been included in an earlier draft of this citation card. *Desny* is the California Supreme Court's idea-submission / implied-in-fact contract landmark, not a general amendment authority. *Blank v. Kirwan* is the correct Supreme Court source for the "reasonable possibility of cure" rule. See `CITATION-VALIDATION-FIVE-SUPREME-COURT-AUTHORITIES.md`.

### 3. *Trafton v. Youngblood* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 17, 31

**Court:** California Supreme Court (Sullivan, J., writing for the Court, with Traynor, C.J., and McComb, Peters, Tobriner, Mosk, and Burke, JJ., concurring).

**Verbatim holding (69 Cal.2d at p. 31):**

> "It is of course settled that the allowance of amendments to conform to the proof rests largely in the discretion of the trial court and its determination will not be disturbed on appeal unless it clearly appears that such discretion has been abused. **Such amendments have been allowed with great liberality 'and no abuse of discretion is shown unless by permitting the amendment new and substantially different issues are introduced in the case or the rights of the adverse party prejudiced.'**" (*Trafton v. Youngblood*, 69 Cal.2d at p. 31, quoting *McNamara v. Steckman* (1927) 202 Cal. 569, 572.) [Emphasis added.]

**Holding verified via Justia/SCOCAL.** *Trafton* itself affirmed the trial court's denial of amendment, because the defendant sought at the very end of trial to inject a new quantum-meruit theory that had not been litigated. *Trafton* therefore cuts in different directions depending on the facts: it provides the Supreme Court's canonical statement of the "great liberality" standard, and it demonstrates that the standard has limits where the amendment introduces new-and-substantially-different issues on which the adverse party has had no opportunity to defend.

**Application to this case.** The *Trafton* test is met decisively for Plaintiff. The SAC does not introduce "new and substantially different issues" in the *Trafton* sense; it refines the same fraud/UCL cause of action against the same defendant arising from the same denial letter. Defendant has had every opportunity to defend — it has filed a demurrer, a special motion to strike, a motion to strike, and an opposition to this motion. *Trafton*'s cautionary second clause therefore does not apply, and its primary clause — "great liberality" — controls. Citation to *Trafton* in this posture is stronger than citation to any Court of Appeal application of the same rule, because it is the Supreme Court's own formulation.

### 4. *Mesler v. Bragg Management Co.* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 290, 296–297

**Court:** California Supreme Court (Grodin, J., writing for the Court, en banc).

**Verbatim holding (39 Cal.3d at pp. 296–297):**

> "Section 473 provides that 'in furtherance of justice' a court may allow a party to amend its pleadings. When a request to amend has been denied, an appellate court is confronted by two conflicting policies. On the one hand, the trial court's discretion should not be disturbed unless it has been clearly abused; on the other, **there is a strong policy in favor of liberal allowance of amendments**. This conflict 'is often resolved in favor of the privilege of amending, and reversals are common where the appellant makes a reasonable showing of prejudice from the ruling.' … Unfair surprise to the opposing party is also to be considered." (*Mesler*, 39 Cal.3d at pp. 296–297, citations omitted.) [Emphasis added.]
>
> "[T]he court **should have permitted plaintiff to plead the alter ego issue**. It apparently based its ruling on its reluctance to 'destroy the plaintiff's time of trial,' yet it was plaintiff who desired to amend regardless of any resulting postponement. … Finally, plaintiff was clearly prejudiced by this ruling, since his entire theory opposing summary judgment revolved around the relationship between defendant, Bragg Crane and Crescent Coke." (*Id.* at p. 297.)

**Application to this case.** *Mesler* is the strongest of the five for this posture because it squarely holds that (a) a trial court **abuses its discretion** by denying leave to amend to add a cause of action when no concrete prejudice is shown, and (b) the rule applies even **six weeks before trial**. This case is at a materially earlier posture — **no trial date is set at all** — and the proposed amendment is supported by specific pleadings and discovery. *A fortiori*, the Supreme Court's rule in *Mesler* requires leave here. The defense's "prejudice" argument in the April 23 Opposition — anti-SLAPP briefing cost and a compressed opposition schedule — does not qualify as cognizable prejudice under *Mesler*, which measured prejudice against the far more serious claim of an imminent trial date.

### 5. *Foreman & Clark Corp. v. Fallon* (1971) 3 Cal.3d 875, 884

**Court:** California Supreme Court (Sullivan, Acting C.J., writing for the Court, with Peters, Tobriner, Mosk, and Burke, JJ., and Files and Hufstedler, JJ. Pro Tem., concurring).

**Verbatim holding (3 Cal.3d at p. 884):**

> "[T]rial was had upon plaintiff's amended and supplemental complaint which did not contain a claim for specific performance. '**It is well established that an amendatory pleading supersedes the original one, which ceases to perform any function as a pleading.**' (*Meyer v. State Board of Equalization* (1954) 42 Cal.2d 376, 384 [267 P.2d 257].) 'Such amended pleading supplants all prior complaints. It alone will be considered by the reviewing court.'" (*Foreman & Clark*, 3 Cal.3d at p. 884, citations cleaned.) [Emphasis added.]

**Application to this case.** *Foreman & Clark* is the procedural engine that makes the first four authorities matter. Once this Court grants leave under *Klopstock* / *Blank v. Kirwan* / *Trafton* / *Mesler*, the FAC "ceases to perform any function as a pleading." The pending anti-SLAPP motion and demurrer — which are directed to the FAC — attack a pleading that no longer exists. They are moot by operation of law. The defense's remedy, as the Legislature itself provided in Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, subdivision (f), is a renewed special motion within sixty days against the operative pleading. That is the entire statutory scheme: amendment liberally granted (§§ 473(a), 576; *Klopstock* / *Blank* / *Trafton* / *Mesler*); amended pleading supersedes the original (*Foreman & Clark*); and defendant's renewed-motion right is preserved on statutory timing (§ 425.16(f)). There is no collision. There is only the routine operation of California procedure.

## The Five-Sentence Summary (for the bench)

> **First**, *Klopstock v. Superior Court* (1941) 17 Cal.2d 13, 19–20 holds that the rule against introducing a "new cause of action" by amendment cannot be "unqualified[ly]" applied, and that leave to amend is properly granted so long as the amendment does not state a "wholly distinct and different legal obligation against the defendant." **Second**, *Blank v. Kirwan* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318 holds that where "there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment," the Court reverses denial of leave. **Third**, *Trafton v. Youngblood* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 17, 31 holds that "[s]uch amendments have been allowed with great liberality" and are denied only where the amendment introduces "new and substantially different issues" on which the adverse party has had no opportunity to defend. **Fourth**, *Mesler v. Bragg Management Co.* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 290, 296–297 holds that "there is a strong policy in favor of liberal allowance of amendments" and that denying leave to add an alter-ego cause of action six weeks before trial was reversible abuse of discretion. **Fifth**, *Foreman & Clark Corp. v. Fallon* (1971) 3 Cal.3d 875, 884 holds that "an amendatory pleading supersedes the original one, which ceases to perform any function as a pleading," providing the procedural consequence that moots pending motions against superseded pleadings.

## Cross-reference — how these five foreclose each defense objection

| Defense objection | Supreme Court authority |
|---|---|
| "SAC adds new causes of action, so leave should be denied" | *Klopstock*, 17 Cal.2d at pp. 19–20 (rule "cannot be unqualified[ly]" applied; only "wholly different" obligation is barred) |
| "Plaintiff cannot cure the defects the defense identified in its anti-SLAPP and demurrer" | *Blank v. Kirwan*, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318 (reasonable-possibility-of-cure standard; leave must be granted when met) |
| "SAC fundamentally changes the case" | *Trafton*, 69 Cal.2d at p. 31 (great liberality; bar only for new-and-substantially-different issues the adverse party cannot defend) |
| "Amendment now is too late / defense has expended resources" | *Mesler*, 39 Cal.3d at pp. 296–297 (liberality applies even six weeks before trial; resource expenditure is not cognizable prejudice) |
| "Pending anti-SLAPP and demurrer should be adjudicated before any amendment" | *Foreman & Clark*, 3 Cal.3d at p. 884 (amended pleading supersedes original; pending motions against superseded pleadings are moot) |

## One-line citation block (copy/paste for filings)

Leave to amend to add a new cause of action is authorized by the California Supreme Court in *Klopstock v. Superior Court* (1941) 17 Cal.2d 13, 19–20 (new cause of action permitted absent a "wholly distinct and different legal obligation"); *Blank v. Kirwan* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318 (must be granted if reasonable possibility of cure); *Trafton v. Youngblood* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 17, 31 ("great liberality" standard); *Mesler v. Bragg Management Co.* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 290, 296–297 ("strong policy in favor of liberal allowance of amendments" at any stage); and *Foreman & Clark Corp. v. Fallon* (1971) 3 Cal.3d 875, 884 (amended pleading "supersedes the original one, which ceases to perform any function as a pleading"); and codified in Code of Civil Procedure sections 473, subdivision (a)(1), and 576.
