## 1. *Klopstock v. Superior Court* (1941) 17 Cal.2d 13 — ✅ VALID

**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Decided:** January 10, 1941
**Verified via:** Stanford SCOCAL; Justia; published tentative rulings; *Lambreton v. Industrial Acc. Com.* (1961) 46 Cal.2d 498 (citing *Klopstock* at 19–20 for the liberality rule).

**Verified holding (pages 19–20):** Section 473 is to be "liberally construed, to the end that cases may be tried on their merits." The Court held that amendments — including those stating a **new cause of action** — are permitted so long as the amendment rests on the same general set of facts and does not constitute a **wholly different cause of action**.

**Use in filings:** Safe to cite at **17 Cal.2d 13, 19–20** for (a) the liberality rule and (b) the same-general-facts permission for adding a new cause of action. This is the foundational Supreme Court authority and appears in every modern amendment-liberality tentative ruling surveyed.

## 2. *Desny v. Wilder* (1956) 46 Cal.2d 715 — ⚠️ WRONG PROPOSITION

**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Decided:** June 28, 1956
**Verified via:** Justia (full text); Stanford SCOCAL.

**What *Desny* actually is:** *Desny* is the California Supreme Court's landmark **idea-submission / implied-in-fact contract** case. It is the foundational authority on when an idea disclosure can support a contract claim against a film producer. It is cited to this day for that proposition and **nothing else of general application**.

**What it says about amendment:** *Desny* does contain a narrow amendment ruling — the Court rejected the defendants' argument that plaintiff's proposed amendment to allege submission of a synopsis (rather than the full 65-page story) constituted a new cause of action barred by the statute of limitations. That is a **relation-back** ruling in an entertainment-contract setting. It is **not** the Supreme Court's statement of a general rule that "leave to amend must be granted where facts state a cause of action on any theory."

**The problem:** I attributed to *Desny* a proposition (the "any theory" / "reasonable possibility of cure" rule) that actually comes from two *different* California Supreme Court lines:
- *Blank v. Kirwan* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318 is the correct Supreme Court authority for "if there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment, leave to amend must be granted" (on demurrer).
- *Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist.* (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 970–971 is the Supreme Court restatement of the same rule.

**Recommendation:** **Remove *Desny* from the five and replace it** with one of the following, both of which are solid California Supreme Court authorities on the point I was trying to anchor:

- **Option A (recommended): *Blank v. Kirwan* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.** Supreme Court; states the must-grant-if-curable rule in exactly the words used in every demurrer/leave opinion since. Already cached locally at `CASE-LAW-AND-STATUTES/opinions-pdf/Blank-v-Kirwan-39-Cal-3d-311.pdf`.
- **Option B: *Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist.* (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 970–971.** Supreme Court; same rule, newer statement. Also cached locally at `CASE-LAW-AND-STATUTES/opinions-pdf/Aubry-v-Tri-City-Hospital-Dist-2-Cal-4th-962.pdf`.

Either substitution restores the five-authority set to a fully-Supreme-Court-validated citation list.

## 3. *Trafton v. Youngblood* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 17, 31 — ⚠️ USE CAREFULLY

**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Decided:** July 11, 1968
**Verified via:** Justia (full text); Stanford SCOCAL; published trial-court rulings.

**What *Trafton* actually says (page 31):** "The allowance of amendments to conform to the proof rests largely in the discretion of the trial court and its determination will not be disturbed on appeal unless it clearly appears that such discretion has been abused. … Such amendments have been allowed with great liberality 'and no abuse of discretion is shown unless by permitting the amendment new and substantially different issues are introduced in the case or the rights of the adverse party prejudiced.'" The Court then added: "Amendments of pleadings to conform to the proofs should not be allowed when they raise new issues not included in the original pleadings and upon which the adverse party had no opportunity to defend."

**The posture:** *Trafton* itself **affirmed the trial court's denial** of leave to amend, because the defendant sought at the end of trial to introduce a new "reasonable value of services" theory that had not been litigated. So the proposition for which *Trafton* stands, in its own holding, is: *trial court's discretion to deny amendment that introduces new and unlitigated issues is not an abuse of discretion*.

**What that means for our brief:** *Trafton* can still be cited — it is in fact cited in every modern amendment ruling — but the **correct** citation is for the **liberality standard** (the "great liberality" quote), not for the proposition that "amendment changing the legal theory is proper absent prejudice." The latter framing is an overread. The safer framing is:

> "Amendments are 'allowed with great liberality' and may be denied only where 'new and substantially different issues are introduced in the case or the rights of the adverse party prejudiced.'" (*Trafton v. Youngblood* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 17, 31.)

Framed that way, *Trafton* actually **helps** the plaintiff here, because (a) the SAC adds causes of action arising from the same general facts (the February 25, 2021 denial letter and its sequelae), not "substantially different issues"; and (b) no trial has occurred, and no prejudice to the defense exists.

**Recommendation:** **Keep *Trafton* in the five**, but re-state the proposition it stands for. Use the "great liberality / no new-and-substantially-different-issues / no prejudice" formulation, not the "change of legal theory is proper" framing.

## 4. *Mesler v. Bragg Management Co.* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 290, 296–297 — ✅ VALID

**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Decided:** July 1985
**Verified via:** Justia (full text); vLex; published trial-court rulings.

**Verified holding (pages 296–297):** The California Supreme Court held that the trial court **abused its discretion** by denying plaintiff's leave to amend to add an **alter-ego** theory six weeks before trial. The Court emphasized the "strong policy in favor of liberal allowance of amendments" and rejected the argument that the proximity of trial justified denial, noting that the defendant had fair notice of the theory from discovery.

**Use in filings:** Safe to cite at **39 Cal.3d 290, 296–297** for (a) the "strong policy in favor of liberal allowance of amendments"; (b) the rule that liberality applies **at any stage**, including close to trial; and (c) the dispositive point that an amendment **adding a new cause of action or theory** (alter-ego there; *Prentice* / *Modnick* / fraud here) is proper absent concrete prejudice. This is the **strongest** of the five authorities for our posture.

## 5. *Foreman & Clark Corp. v. Fallon* (1971) 3 Cal.3d 875, 884 — ✅ VALID

**Court:** California Supreme Court
**Decided:** 1971
**Verified via:** Justia (full text); multiple published tentative rulings with pin cites at 884; *Evan Paul Auto Leasing* tentative ruling quoting 884 verbatim.

**Verified holding (page 884):** "It is well established that an amendatory pleading supersedes the original one, which ceases to perform any function as a pleading."

**Use in filings:** Safe to cite at **3 Cal.3d 875, 884** for the superseding-effect rule. This is the procedural engine of the mootness argument against the pending anti-SLAPP and demurrer: once leave is granted, the FAC "ceases to perform any function as a pleading," and the defense's pending motions target a pleading that no longer exists.
