## V. “De novo record” against evidence: what that phrase can mean here (and why it matters)

“De novo” is not one thing in California litigation; it is a **chameleon** that changes color with forum.

### A. Appellate “de novo” (pure questions of law)

On appeal, certain issues get **de novo** review. That helps plaintiff on **instructional error** and other legal questions your materials treat as independent of witness credibility. But **de novo legal review** does not automatically recreate a **complete factual record** if trial evidence was excluded or deferred for reasons later challenged as **procured by misrepresentation**. The appellate court works from **the clerk’s and reporter’s transcripts**, not from a hypothetical “full universe” unless the record is supplemented in procedurally proper ways.

**Implication:** Your materials explicitly split the remedial path: appeal addresses some instructional errors; the **equity action** addresses **extrinsic fraud** and **court reliance** on false factual predicates—because those issues may require **factfinding** beyond typical appellate assumptions.

### B. Equitable factfinding and “de novo” in spirit

An independent action in equity, as your `COURT.RELIANCE.md` frames it, seeks a **trial-level** inquiry into **whether** defense counsel’s tribunal-facing statements were false, **whether** the court relied, and **whether** the judgment is **infected** by that reliance. That is not “de novo” in the appellate sense, but it is **de novo** in the everyday sense: a **fresh judicial examination** of misconduct and causation that the appeal may not be structurally suited to resolve.

### C. Retrial after vacatur/reversal: the closest thing to a true “clean record”

If the judgment is vacated or reversed and the personal injury case returns, the “de novo record” idea becomes most literal: a **new jury** (or bench trial) could hear the case with **911 and BWC placed early**, with **police witnesses deposed**, with **judicial notice motions** resolved, and with **supplemental police materials** treated under proper foundations. Your theory is that the **first trial’s record** was not merely “close”; it was **structurally biased** by **process fraud** and by **evidentiary sequencing** that prevented a fair contest.

That reasoning depended entirely on the premise that the defense was an innocent bystander -- that the 911 evidence had appeared from nowhere, that the defense genuinely did not know its provenance, and that Plaintiff bore the entire burden of authenticating and presenting materials that the defense itself had possessed for years. Had the court known the truth -- that PSA had held the body-worn camera footage and the 911 audio in its own file since May 2023, that the materials were part of a unified discovery production from Dolan, and that defense counsel had specifically subpoenaed the same records from SFPD in February 2024 -- the continuance calculus would have been fundamentally different. Second, when Plaintiff referenced the 911 recording, Reyna stated he was "not sure what recording" Plaintiff was referring to (RT 7, ll. 2990-2994). He knew precisely what recording was at issue because he had requested it by name fourteen months earlier. His feigned confusion was a continuation of the same artifice that Navaratnasingham had established at the pretrial hearing.

Then came the confession. Minutes after Reyna's feigned confusion about the 911 recording, the court asked about the body-worn camera footage on the defense exhibit list. Navaratnasingham responded: "Sure. They were provided by Dolan. He has had them for whatever, sure." (RT 7, p. 214, ll. 13-15.) She then described having "five videos" and curating them into "about 8 or 9 minutes" for the jury. The statement is remarkable for its casual familiarity. She did not hesitate, did not qualify, did not express uncertainty. She named the specific law firm -- Dolan -- that had provided the materials years earlier. The dismissive phrasing -- "for whatever, sure" -- reveals that the Dolan origin of the body-worn camera footage was an established, recognized fact within the defense trial team. One does not casually name a specific law firm as the source of evidence if one is genuinely ignorant of that evidence's existence. The April 16 admission is the confession that proves the "news to me" statement was a lie, that the "not produced in discovery" declaration was false, and that the defense had curated and prepared the very materials it had spent months telling the court it did not possess or understand.

When Navaratnasingham admitted the body cams were "provided by Dolan," she was admitting the defense received materials from the same production that included the 911 audio. She cannot logically claim that the body cams came from Dolan while the 911 did not. They traveled together. They were produced together. They were in the same discovery response. Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.280 and *Deyo v. Kilbourne* (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 787, receipt of one document in a production establishes constructive receipt of all documents in that production.

The court never learned the truth in time for it to matter. The "provided by Dolan" admission was made on April 16, 2025. The verdict was returned on April 25, 2025. The judgment was entered the same day. The Notice of Entry of Judgment was filed April 29, 2025 -- signed by Alberto Reyna. At no point between the April 16 admission and the verdict did either Navaratnasingham or Reyna withdraw, correct, or amend any of their prior representations. Under California Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 3.3(a)(1), a lawyer must not "knowingly make a false statement of material fact or law to a tribunal or fail to correct a false statement of material fact or law previously made to the tribunal by the lawyer." The duty to correct is affirmative and continuing. Neither attorney discharged that duty. The prior false statements remained operative, uncorrected, embedded in the procedural framework of the trial, and the jury deliberated and returned a verdict on a record shaped by those false statements.

This is why the case is an equity case and not merely an appeal. The distinction matters because it defines the remedy and the standard. On direct appeal (A173827), the reviewing court examines the trial court's independent legal errors -- in this case, the deliberation-stage failure to provide Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a) when the jury asked for the governing statutory duty. That is an instructional error reviewable de novo. But the appeal cannot adjudicate whether defense counsel lied to the trial judge. The appellate court is limited to the record as it was presented. It cannot conduct a fact-finding inquiry into whether the "news to me" statement was a knowing falsehood. It cannot subpoena the defense file to determine whether the 911 audio was physically contained in it when PSA received it on May 4, 2023. It cannot take testimony from Jessup about the production. It cannot order discovery into CSAA's claims-handling files to determine whether CSAA knew its retained counsel was making false representations. Those are fact-bound inquiries that require a trial-level proceeding with evidence, witnesses, and the court's equitable jurisdiction.

The independent action in equity exists precisely for this category of wrong. Under *Kulchar v. Kulchar* (1969) 1 Cal.3d 467, 472, and Code of Civil Procedure section 187, a court has inherent equitable power to vacate a judgment procured by extrinsic fraud. The equitable action is not a second bite at the apple. It is the only bite for a category of fraud that, by its nature, cannot be detected and corrected within the proceeding it corrupts. The plaintiff cannot cross-examine the court's reliance on defense counsel's representations. The plaintiff cannot move to strike a ruling that was induced by a statement the court accepted as truthful. The plaintiff has no procedural mechanism to test the veracity of an officer of the court's representation to the judge in real time. The only mechanism is a separate action, brought after the fact, alleging and proving that the judgment was procured by fraud that deprived the aggrieved party of a fair adversary hearing.

The equity characterization also matters because it defines the prejudice standard. In an ordinary appeal, the reviewing court applies *People v. Watson* (1956) 46 Cal.2d 818, 836 (state law: "reasonably probable" a more favorable result) or *Chapman v. California* (1967) 386 U.S. 18, 24 (constitutional: "beyond a reasonable doubt" that error was harmless). In an equity action for extrinsic fraud, those frameworks do not apply because the fraud itself is the structural defect. When the exclusion of evidence was procured by an officer of the court's intentional deceit, the reviewing court does not calculate the mathematical prejudice of the excluded evidence. The process was corrupted. The question is not "would the jury have reached a different verdict?" The question is "was the plaintiff deprived of a fair opportunity to present his case?" Under *Smith v. Superior Court* (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042, when attorney misconduct is "sufficiently egregious," it constitutes a denial of due process that renders the trial "fundamentally unfair regardless of the strength of the evidence." The fraud here is sufficiently egregious: it involved written declarations under penalty of perjury containing false factual predicates, oral representations in open court directly contradicting sworn testimony given minutes earlier, a continuing failure to correct those representations over a period of months, and a casual admission that proved the prior statements were knowing lies -- all to suppress a party admission in which the defendant told a 911 operator that he struck the pedestrian.

The 9-3 verdict underscores the structural prejudice. Nine to three is the minimum civil majority under California law. A single juror changing sides would have produced an 8-4 split -- a mistrial, not a defense verdict. The suppressed evidence includes the defendant's own words to the 911 operator ("BOOM! I just hit him. HIT HIM. There was barely anything"), an eyewitness captured on Officer Thompson's body-worn camera stating "The man in the van hit the guy" and "That man in the van hit the guy who was laying on the ground" (RT 10, p. 39, ll. 21-23; RT 10, p. 59, ll. 3-4), and CAD records corroborating the emergency dispatch timeline. It is not speculative to say that a single juror would have been persuaded by the defendant's own admission that he struck the plaintiff. The defendant authenticated that recording under oath at trial: "Yes. That is my voice." (RT 8, l. 1979.) The jury heard the authentication but was prevented from hearing the recording in a timely, orderly manner because the defense had spent months telling the court the recording was of unknown provenance and had never been produced in discovery.

The institutional reliance problem also has a temporal dimension that deepens the equity claim. The false representations did not merely affect a single ruling. They created a framework that persisted across the entire trial. Once the court accepted on February 18 that the defense was genuinely ignorant of the 911 evidence, every subsequent ruling was filtered through that premise. The continuance denial on February 18 meant Plaintiff had to proceed to trial without resolving the foundation question. The MIL grants meant the evidence started trial in an excluded posture, requiring Plaintiff to spend trial time relitigating admissibility rather than presenting his case. The supplemental report exclusion on April 16 removed corroborative evidence. The 911 deferral on April 16 delayed the recording's presentation in a way that disrupted Plaintiff's trial narrative. Each ruling compounded the last. By the time the jury deliberated, the evidentiary landscape was the product of a cascade of rulings that traced back to a single false statement: "news to me."

This cascading quality is what distinguishes the fraud from an isolated evidentiary dispute. A single erroneous ruling can be reviewed for harmless error. A pattern of rulings, all flowing from a common false premise planted by an officer of the court, is structural corruption. It cannot be unwound ruling by ruling because the rulings are interdependent. The continuance denial affected the MIL posture, which affected the foundation burden, which affected the supplemental report exclusion, which affected the 911 deferral, which affected the jury's access to the evidence. Remove the initial false premise and the entire cascade collapses. That is why equity must intervene at the root -- the judgment itself -- rather than attempting to correct individual branches of the cascade on appeal.

The defense will argue that Plaintiff should have independently subpoenaed the records, that Plaintiff had the materials since August 2024 and failed to use them, and that any problem was Plaintiff's own fault. This argument is the defense's own creation. It is the very narrative that Navaratnasingham constructed when she told the court that Plaintiff "had had the materials" and the defense was "not blocking" admission. The law does not require a litigant to assume that opposing counsel is lying to the judge. A party is entitled to rely on the candor of officers of the court. (*Blanton v. Womancare, Inc.* (1985) 38 Cal.3d 396, 403.) When the court accepted the defense's representations and structured its rulings around them, the court created a procedural landscape that Plaintiff could not escape by independent action. Plaintiff did attempt to present the evidence. The defense's false representations blocked or delayed that presentation at every turn. The equitable wrong is not the plaintiff's litigation conduct but the corruption of the process itself.

Finally, the equity characterization encompasses the full scope of the wrong, including the role of CSAA Insurance Exchange as the principal that funded, directed, and benefited from the fraud. American National Fire Insurance Co.* (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 498, 527-528, an insurer that selects and retains counsel is vicariously liable for the conduct of that counsel in the defense of the action. Under Civil Code section 2338 and *Anderson v. Thacher* (1946) 76 Cal.App.2d 50, 72, a principal is liable for the fraudulent representations of its agent made within the scope of the agent's authority. CSAA cannot distance itself from a fraud it paid for and from which it directly benefited. The equity action reaches not only the attorneys who made the false representations but the principal that funded and benefited from the architecture of deceit. That comprehensive reach -- from the individual attorneys to the institutional insurer -- is available only in equity, and it is one of the reasons the independent action in equity is the correct and necessary vehicle for redressing this wrong.

## Why CSAA's Reliance on the Police Report Was Insufficient to Deny the Claim

The insufficiency breaks down into four interlocking categories: (1) the police report itself was incomplete; (2) CSAA had a legal duty to go beyond it; (3) "concluded" was an objectively false representation; and (4) the evidence the police report omitted would have changed the outcome.

---

### 1. The Police Report Was an Incomplete Record of the Incident

The police report was a summary document that **did not contain the most probative evidence** generated by the same law enforcement response. Specifically, the record establishes that the police report:

- **Did not include Abdelhalim's 911 admission.** The insured told the 911 operator: "BOOM! I just hit him. HIT HIM. There was barely anything." and "He cross from me, and he's barely hitting anything, but I'm calling for him." None of this appeared in the police report itself (RFA 20 in the 631802 discovery).

- **Did not include the body-worn camera footage.** Officer Thompson's BWC captured eyewitness Dustin Rosemond stating: "The man in the van hit the guy" and "That man in the van hit the guy who was laying on the ground." The police report did not reference this footage (RFA 21).

- **Did not include Plaintiff's own BWC statements** to officers that the driver struck him (RFA 22-23).

- **Did reference a CAD number** -- a direct link to the dispatch records, 911 audio, and other underlying materials. That CAD number was a roadmap to the suppressed evidence. CSAA saw it and did nothing with it (RFA 16).

A police report is a narrative summary written by an officer after the fact. It is not a substitute for the raw, contemporaneous evidence -- the 911 recording, the body-worn camera footage, and the CAD logs -- that the report's very existence signals are available.

---

### 2. California Law Required CSAA to Investigate Beyond the Police Report

CSAA had affirmative legal duties -- statutory, regulatory, and common-law -- that made reliance on the police report alone legally insufficient.

**a. The Implied Covenant and the *Egan* Standard.**
Under *Egan v. Mutual of Omaha Ins. Co.* (1979) 24 Cal.3d 809, an insurer may not deny a claim without first "thoroughly investigating the foundation for its denial." A thorough investigation of a vehicle-pedestrian collision where law enforcement responded requires obtaining the 911 audio, BWC, and CAD -- not just a one-page summary report. An investigation confined to the insured's own account and a bare police report is what *Egan* calls "perfunctory" and "outcome-driven."

**b. The *Mariscal* Neutral-Investigator Duty.**
Under *Mariscal v. Old Republic Life Ins. Co.* (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 1617, an insurer must act as a "neutral investigator" who affirmatively searches for evidence that **supports** coverage, not just evidence that defeats it. By relying only on the police report and the insured's self-serving account -- while ignoring the 911 recording in which the insured admitted fault -- CSAA was conducting a one-directional investigation designed to find reasons to deny.

**c. The *Bartel* Expansion of the Duty.**
*Bartel v. Chicago Title Insurance Co.* (2025) confirms that the duty to investigate is not limited to the narrow framing of the initial claim. When obvious leads exist -- and a CAD number on a police report is the most obvious lead imaginable -- the insurer must pursue them. CSAA's failure to follow the CAD number to the 911 audio and BWC violates *Bartel*.

**d. The Fair Claims Settlement Practices Regulations.**
CCR title 10, section 2695.7(d) requires that "every insurer shall conduct and diligently pursue a thorough, fair and objective investigation." The 911 audio, BWC, and CAD records are standard, routine products of any law enforcement response. They are "reasonably available" within the meaning of the regulation. Issuing a denial without requesting them violates the regulation on its face.

**e. Insurance Code section 790.03(h).**
Section 790.03(h)(3) requires insurers to "adopt and implement reasonable standards for the prompt investigation and processing of claims." Section 790.03(h)(5) prohibits failing to attempt good-faith settlement "in which liability has become reasonably clear." A reasonable standard for investigating a pedestrian collision includes pulling the 911 audio where the insured may have made admissions. CSAA's failure to do so is a statutory violation.

### 3. "Concluded" Was an Objectively False Statement

CSAA issued its denial letter on February 25, 2021 -- just 21 days after the collision -- stating its investigation was "concluded" and its insured was "not liable." But as of that date, CSAA had:

- **Not obtained** the 911 audio recording
- **Not obtained** body-worn camera footage
- **Not obtained** CAD records
- **Not obtained** any medical records from the Plaintiff
- **Not retained** an accident reconstruction or biomechanical expert
- **Not taken** a recorded statement from its own insured
- **Not contacted** the Plaintiff at all
- **Relied exclusively** on the insured's own account as the only liability-relevant information

Under California law, an investigation cannot truthfully be described as "concluded" when the insurer has not obtained foundational objective evidence that is known or reasonably knowable to exist. The word "concluded" is not mere opinion -- it is an implied assertion that objective investigative steps were taken. The Superior Knowledge doctrine applies: CSAA has exclusive control over its claims file, and the Plaintiff had no way to verify what CSAA actually did or did not do. When a party with superior knowledge makes a statement about a verifiable process to a party who lacks that knowledge, it is an actionable statement of fact, not opinion (Civil Code section 1710; *Randi W. v. Muroc Joint Unified School Dist.*).

The "based on available evidence" qualifier fails as a defense because "available" under CCR section 2695.7 means "reasonably obtainable," not "whatever is sitting on the adjuster's desk." CSAA cannot artificially restrict the evidence pool by refusing to look, and then cite that self-created ignorance as justification. This is the **willful blindness** doctrine: an insurer that puts on a blindfold cannot legally justify a denial by saying, "Based on what I can currently see, your claim has no merit."

### 4. The Evidence the Police Report Omitted Would Have Changed the Outcome

The evidence CSAA chose not to obtain was devastating to its own insured's position:

- **Party admission against interest**: Abdelhalim told the 911 operator he hit the pedestrian. This is a spontaneous, excited utterance, authenticated by the defendant himself at trial ("Yes. That is my voice.").

- **Eyewitness corroboration**: On the BWC, an independent eyewitness stated "The man in the van hit the guy" and "That man in the van hit the guy who was laying on the ground."

- **Inconsistency with insured's account**: Abdelhalim's 911 admission ("I barely hit him") directly contradicted the defense's trial position of "no contact."

If CSAA had obtained the 911 recording before issuing its denial, it would have heard its own insured admit fault. Under Insurance Code section 790.03(h)(5), liability was "reasonably clear" once that admission was available. CSAA's failure to obtain it before issuing a denial is not just negligent -- it is an investigation strategy designed to avoid discovering coverage.

This is confirmed by the timeline: Plaintiff's counsel (the Dolan Law Firm) obtained the same 911 audio through a routine California Public Records Act request in under three months. The evidence was not hidden, exotic, or difficult to find. It was standard public-agency material that any competent insurer investigating a serious auto-versus-pedestrian collision would have obtained as a matter of course.

### The Combined Effect

The police report was not the end of CSAA's investigation -- it was a starting point that CSAA refused to follow. The report contained a CAD number pointing directly to the 911 audio, BWC, and dispatch records. CSAA saw that number and did nothing. It then declared the investigation "concluded" and the insured "not liable" based solely on the insured's own self-serving account, without obtaining the objective evidence that would have proven the insured admitted fault.

Under *Egan*, *Mariscal*, *Bartel*, *Gruenberg*, Insurance Code section 790.03(h), and CCR section 2695.7, this is not a defensible investigation. It is a claim denial engineered to succeed by avoiding the truth -- and the "concluded" representation is the mechanism that weaponized that avoidance by deceiving the Plaintiff into believing a thorough inquiry had occurred.
