# A PEDESTRIAN, A CROSSWALK, AND A BILLION-DOLLAR DENIAL

## How an Injured Man Was Run Down, Then Run Over Again by the Insurance System

### Franciscus Dylan Rosario v. Subhi Abdelhalim, CSAA Insurance Exchange, and Related Defendants

**Public Statement -- Released for Circulation to News Media, Bar Associations, Attorney Networks, and the Public**

## I. WHAT THIS CASE IS ABOUT

On February 4, 2021, Franciscus Dylan Rosario was walking in a crosswalk in San Francisco when he was struck from behind by a vehicle driven by Subhi Abdelhalim, a commercial driver and owner-operator of Sonoma Liquor, a liquor store in the Tenderloin district. Mr. Rosario never saw the vehicle coming. He was a pedestrian in a crosswalk exercising his lawful right of way. He was hit from behind.

Moments after the collision, the driver called 911. On that recorded call, the driver made an admission: "I barely hit him with anything." Law enforcement responded. Officers wore body cameras. The incident was documented through police reports, body-worn camera footage, and computer-aided dispatch records. Every material fact of this collision was captured in real time by multiple independent sources.

What followed was not a straightforward resolution. What followed was a systematic campaign of denial, concealment, and institutional fraud that began on day one and continued through four years of litigation, a jury trial, and a verdict that was the product of corruption of the judicial process itself.

The driver hid behind his insurer. The insurer -- CSAA Insurance Exchange, the liability carrier affiliated with the American Automobile Association -- is not a small regional company. It is a multi-billion-dollar reciprocal insurance exchange that provides automobile coverage to millions of California drivers. It employs professional claims adjusters. It has investigators, attorneys, and institutional resources. It has the capacity to conduct thorough investigations. In this case, it chose not to.

Twenty-one days after the collision, CSAA sent a letter to Mr. Rosario stating that it had "concluded" its investigation and "concluded" that its insured was not liable. Twenty-one days. Three weeks. To a person receiving that letter, the word "concluded" means the investigation is finished. It suggests that the insurer obtained the relevant evidence, reviewed it, and made a determination. That is what "concluded" means in ordinary usage.

But at the time CSAA mailed that letter, it had not obtained the 911 call recording containing the driver's admission. It had not obtained the body-worn camera footage. It had not obtained the computer-aided dispatch records. These are the three categories of objective, contemporaneous evidence that are generated whenever law enforcement responds to a collision. They are the foundation of any genuine liability investigation. CSAA declared its investigation over before it had reviewed any of them. Mr. Rosario's then-counsel, Dolan Law Firm, obtained the 911 recording less than three months later through a routine records request. The evidence was there for anyone who bothered to look. CSAA did not bother.

Consider what CSAA would have heard if it had obtained the 911 recording before writing that denial letter. It would have heard its own insured's voice. It would have heard the admission on that call. And it would have had to decide: do we deny this claim knowing our insured admitted fault on a recorded line? CSAA avoided that dilemma by simply not looking. By declaring the investigation "concluded" before the evidence was reviewed, CSAA issued the denial without the inconvenience of unfavorable evidence. That is not an accident. It is a practice.

That denial letter forced an injured pedestrian into litigation against a commercial driver backed by one of the largest insurance organizations in the country. The litigation lasted more than four years. And what happened during those four years was not a fair fight. It was a continuation of the same institutional posture that produced the denial letter: deny everything, disclaim knowledge of the evidence, and use the weight of institutional resources to exhaust the injured person's ability to fight back.

Mr. Rosario now has three cases pending. This statement describes them, explains what the public record shows, and invites attorneys, journalists, and the public to examine the evidence for themselves.

## II. THE DRIVER AND THE INSURER

Subhi Abdelhalim is listed in the court records as an individual and as the operator of Sonoma Liquor, a commercial establishment in San Francisco's Tenderloin neighborhood. The Answer to the Complaint, filed September 14, 2021, identifies him as "Subhi Abdelhalim, individually, and dba Sonoma Liquor." This was not a casual driver in a private vehicle on an afternoon errand. This was a commercial operator, doing business in one of San Francisco's densest pedestrian neighborhoods. The Tenderloin is home to some of the city's most vulnerable populations. Pedestrians share its narrow streets and crosswalks with commercial traffic daily. The collision occurred in the City and County of San Francisco. The case was filed and tried in the Superior Court of California, County of San Francisco, as Case No. CGC-21-594102, *Rosario v. Abdelhalim*.

CSAA Insurance Exchange provided the liability coverage. It retained and funded every defense firm that represented Abdelhalim across the four-year litigation. It paid for the lawyers. It paid for the motions. It paid for the trial. The Answer to the Complaint was filed by Michael R. Chambers, an attorney whose header in the court filing bears a CSAA email address. From day one, the institutional connection between insurer and defense was visible in the court record. CSAA was not a passive insurer writing checks. It was the institutional engine that drove the defense strategy from the February 25, 2021 denial letter through the April 25, 2025 verdict. The injured pedestrian was not fighting one individual driver. He was fighting a system.

The defense passed through three law firms: initially Michael R. Chambers of Carbone, Smith & Koyama, based in Oakland (whose correspondence in the court file bears a CSAA email address); subsequently Phillips, Spallas & Angstadt LLP, which substituted in on May 4, 2023; and ultimately the attorneys who tried the case in April 2025. The churning of law firms itself raises questions. The transfer of the defense file between firms became the mechanism by which critical evidence was denied, ignored, or "lost" -- and the denial of that evidence is what corrupted the trial.

## III. THE EVIDENCE THAT WAS HIDDEN IN PLAIN SIGHT

The 911 call recording, approximately five body-worn camera videos, and computer-aided dispatch records were served on the defense during discovery in August and September 2022. This is not a contested fact. J. Jessup, Esq., who represented Mr. Rosario during a portion of the litigation, testified under oath at a February 18, 2025 pretrial hearing that these materials had been served on defense counsel and had been used during depositions. His testimony is in the Reporter's Transcript of the February 18, 2025 hearing, part of the court file. No one has contradicted that testimony. No one has called Jessup a liar. No one has challenged his account.

On May 4, 2023, Phillips, Spallas & Angstadt substituted in as defense counsel. The Substitution of Attorney is a public filing (Transaction ID 69953800). The defense file was transferred from the prior law firm. From that date forward, the new defense lawyers had the file. They had it for twenty months before the events described below.

What happened next is the core of the institutional fraud.

In January 2025, the defense filed motions in limine. Motion in Limine No. 6, signed by attorney Priya D. Navaratnasingham of Phillips, Spallas & Angstadt, stated that the recorded materials "were not produced in discovery." Motion in Limine No. 17 stated that the defendant had "no idea where this 911 call log or audio recording came from" and that it was of "unknown provenance." These motions are in the Clerk's Transcript. Anyone can read them.

At the February 18, 2025 pretrial hearing, Navaratnasingham stated in open court that the 911 call evidence was "news to me." The court's response, documented in the Reporter's Transcript, was: "I see. Okay."

The evidence had been served on the defense in 2022. The defense file had been in the new firm's hands since May 2023. Twenty months later, the lead attorney told the court it was "news to me." That statement was false. The record proves it.

At trial in April 2025, attorney Alberto Reyna of the same firm made additional representations. He stated that a supplemental police report was "not anything that we received as part of the subpoena." Regarding the 911 recording, he stated he was "not sure what recording" was being referred to. These statements are in the trial transcript. They were false. The record proves it.

And then the defendant himself took the stand and authenticated the 911 recording. Under oath, Abdelhalim confirmed: "Yes. That is my voice." The same recording his lawyers said was "news to me." The same recording they said had "unknown provenance." The same recording his insurer never bothered to obtain before declaring the investigation "concluded." The defendant confirmed it was real, it was his voice, and it was his call. That moment is in the Reporter's Transcript. It is one sentence. Five words. And it collapses the entire edifice of denial that the insurer and its attorneys had spent four years constructing.

The jury never heard this recording as primary evidence during the liability phase in the manner it should have been presented, because by the time of trial the defense's false representations had already shaped the court's rulings on the evidence. The jury should have heard the defendant's own words, on a recording the defendant himself authenticated, as central evidence of liability. Instead, by the time the authentication occurred, the evidentiary framework built on the defense's lies had already done its work.

## IV. HOW THE COURT WAS MISLED

The trial court made critical rulings based on false representations by defense counsel. This is the heart of the fraud-on-the-court case.

When defense counsel told the court that evidence was "not received" or of "unknown provenance," the court relied on those representations. The court accepted "news to me" without ordering any verification. No written production confirmation. No file inventory. No evidentiary hearing on whether the materials had actually been served. The court denied continuance requests that would have given the plaintiff time to establish the production history. The court excluded evidence based on the defense's representations.

If the defense had told the truth -- that the evidence had been served years earlier, that the defense file contained these materials, that the 911 recording was known and authentic -- the court would have ruled differently. The evidence would have come in. The jury would have heard the defendant's own words. The trial would have been fair.

Instead, the court was induced to build its evidentiary rulings on a false foundation. And once that foundation was laid, every subsequent ruling tilted against the plaintiff. The court applied different standards to the plaintiff's evidence and the defense's evidence. Vehicle Code duties were excluded as "not evidence" when the plaintiff offered them. The defense was permitted to argue from materials under different terms. The playing field was not level. The specific rulings are documented in the Reporter's Transcripts and are available for any attorney or journalist to review.

## V. THE JURY ASKED FOR THE LAW AND WAS TOLD NO

During deliberations, the jury sent a written note to the court. The jurors asked whether they should consider Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a) in deciding whether the defendant was negligent. These provisions govern the duty of a driver making a turn and yielding to pedestrians. In a case where a pedestrian was struck in a crosswalk by a turning vehicle, these are the governing statutory duties.

The court responded with a categorical prohibition. The jury was told it could not consider those provisions. The court stated that the statutes were "not part of the evidence" and were "not permitted to consider that as evidence in the case, period."

This was a fundamental legal error. Statutes are not evidence. They are law. Under the California Evidence Code, statutory law is subject to mandatory judicial notice. A trial court has a duty under the Code of Civil Procedure, when a jury asks a question of law during deliberations, to provide accurate information. Telling a jury that it cannot consider a governing statutory duty -- because statutes are "not evidence" -- conflates two entirely different legal categories. The jurors knew they needed that law. They asked for it. They were denied.

And then, in the same exchange, the court supplied a different Vehicle Code section -- section 360, a definitional provision -- to the same jury. Both statutes were in the same posture. Neither was "evidence" in the traditional sense. One was prohibited; the other was provided. That inconsistency alone establishes that the court's rationale was erroneous.

The jury returned a 9-3 verdict in favor of the defendant. Nine jurors voted for the defense. Three voted for the plaintiff. Under California law, this is the minimum civil majority. A single juror changing sides would have altered the outcome. In a case where the defendant authenticated a 911 recording containing his own admission, and where the jury explicitly asked to consider the governing statutory duties and was told it could not, that 9-3 verdict is not a measure of the evidence. It is a measure of what the jury was not allowed to consider.

## VI. THREE CASES, ONE PATTERN: INSTITUTIONAL CORRUPTION FROM DAY ONE

What happened in this case is institutional corruption. Not corruption in the sense of bribery or secret payments. Corruption in the sense that the systems designed to protect injured people -- insurance investigation, truthful advocacy, fair judicial proceedings -- were corrupted by a pattern of denial, concealment, and misrepresentation that began the day the denial letter was mailed and continued through the last day of trial.

Three separate proceedings have been initiated, each addressing a different stage of the corruption.

### Case One: The Appeal -- A173827

The appeal challenges the trial court's instructional error. When the jury asked during deliberations whether it could consider Vehicle Code sections 22107 and 21804(a), the court had a mandatory duty to provide accurate legal information. The court instead prohibited the jury from considering those provisions, based on the mistaken premise that statutes are "not evidence."

The standard of review is de novo -- the appellate court decides the legal question fresh, without deference to the trial court. The 9-3 verdict establishes that the case was close and that the error was prejudicial. The opening brief has been filed; it is part of the appellate record. The record on appeal includes the jury's written question, the court's response, and the 9-3 verdict form -- all available for review.

The appeal is pending in the First Appellate District, Division Three, as Case No. A173827. When the appeal succeeds, the judgment is reversed and the case goes back for a new trial -- this time with proper instructions and a very different evidentiary landscape.

### Case Two: The Equity Action -- CGC-25-631801

The equity action seeks to set aside the judgment on the ground that it was procured through extrinsic fraud on the court. This is not a claim that witnesses lied to the jury. This is a claim that defense counsel made false representations to the judge that corrupted the judicial process itself -- inducing the court to exclude evidence and deny procedures based on premises that were not true.

The named defendants include the driver, the insurer, the law firm that tried the case, and the individual attorneys who made the false representations. The complaint sets forth a three-part framework: the defense had the evidence (established by sworn testimony and the substitution filing), the defense told the court it did not have the evidence (established by the motions in limine and hearing transcripts), and the court relied on those false representations to make rulings that deprived the plaintiff of a fair trial (established by the Reporter's Transcripts of the rulings).

California law permits a party to bring an independent action in equity to vacate a judgment procured by extrinsic fraud. This is a well-established remedy. It is separate from, and independent of, the appeal. Multiple procedural and substantive avenues are available to prosecute the action and to defeat anticipated defense tactics. The case is pending in the San Francisco Superior Court as CGC-25-631801, with a Case Management Conference on May 27, 2026.

### Case Three: The Insurance Fraud Action -- CGC-25-631802

The fraud action targets the root of the problem: CSAA's February 25, 2021 denial letter. CSAA told an injured man it had "concluded" its investigation twenty-one days after he was struck in a crosswalk -- without obtaining the 911 call, the body-worn camera footage, or the dispatch records. That representation was false. CSAA knew or should have known it was false. The purpose of the false denial was to discourage the claim and shift the cost of evidence collection onto the injured person.

The causes of action include fraud under the California Civil Code and unfair business practices under California's Unfair Competition Law, Business and Professions Code section 17200. The remedies sought include compensatory damages, punitive damages, restitution, and injunctive relief. The action also seeks to recover, as consequential damages, the cost of the entire four-year litigation that the fraudulent denial forced Mr. Rosario to pursue.

The institutional dimensions of this claim are significant. If CSAA's practice is to issue categorical denial letters declaring investigations "concluded" before obtaining basic evidence, then this case is not an isolated incident. It is a business practice. The UCL claim permits injunctive relief that could affect how CSAA handles claims across California. The punitive damages claim reflects the institutional nature of the conduct. Discovery in this action will reveal CSAA's internal training materials, denial-letter templates, claims-handling metrics, and adjuster instructions -- the institutional machinery behind the denial letter.

The fraud action stands on its own. It does not depend on the appeal or the equity action. Even if the judgment is upheld and the equity action does not succeed, the denial letter's falsity is an independent wrong with independent damages. The claim file will show what CSAA reviewed before issuing the denial. If the file shows that CSAA reviewed nothing more than the insured's own account before declaring the investigation "concluded," the falsity is self-evident. If the file shows that CSAA requested records but issued the denial before receiving them, the recklessness is self-evident. Either way, CSAA's institutional practice is exposed.

The case is pending as CGC-25-631802, with a Case Management Conference on May 27, 2026.

## VIII. WHY THIS MATTERS BEYOND ONE CASE

This is not just about one pedestrian and one crosswalk and one liquor store owner's insurance policy. The questions raised by these proceedings go to the foundation of how the civil justice system operates in California.

**For every insured driver in California,** there is a question: When your insurance company says it has "concluded its investigation," has it actually investigated? Or has it simply decided to deny and move on, betting that you or the person you injured will not have the resources to find out?

**For every injured person who receives a denial letter,** there is a question: Should you believe it? When a multi-billion-dollar insurance exchange tells you it has concluded its investigation and your claim has no merit, should you accept that at face value? Or should you wonder whether the investigation was real?

**For every attorney who practices before the Superior Court,** there is a question: What happens when opposing counsel tells the court that evidence was "not received" and the court relies on that representation? **For every member of the public who trusts that the courts operate on truthful representations,** there is a question: What happens when that trust is violated? What recourse exists when the corrupting influence is not a rogue individual but an institutional practice, funded by a billion-dollar carrier, carried out by a succession of retained law firms, and documented step by step in the court's own transcripts?

These questions do not have easy answers. But they have a factual record. That record is in the Superior Court of California, County of San Francisco. It is available for anyone to examine.
