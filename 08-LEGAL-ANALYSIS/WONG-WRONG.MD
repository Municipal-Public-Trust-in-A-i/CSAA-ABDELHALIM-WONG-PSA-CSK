### Educational overview for appellate counsel (respondent)

**Black letter framework**

1. **Code of Civil Procedure § 614** — When the jury, during deliberations, desires to be informed on a **question of law**, the court must give the **information required**. That is not optional “help with evidence”; it is a duty tied to **legal** questions once the jury has asked.

2. **Evidence Code § 451, subdivision (a)** — California **statutes** are among the matters of which the court **must** take judicial notice. Statutes are **law**, not “items of evidence” that parties admit through the evidence gate like exhibits or testimony.

3. **Category distinction** — **Admissibility** controls what factual proofs and exhibits are before the jury. **Applicable legal standards** (including Vehicle Code duties the jury must apply) are **law**, supplied by the court through instructions or correct responses to jury questions—not treated as if they were exhibits that were or were not “received in evidence.”

**Structure of what went wrong (as appellant frames it)**

The alleged error is not merely “the judge declined an instruction.” It is a **two-layer** failure:

- **Layer 1 — Wrong premise:** Treating governing Vehicle Code sections as if they were **evidence** the jury could only consider if “in evidence,” rather than as **law** the court must correctly convey when the jury asks.

- **Layer 2 — Wrong remedy:** Instead of answering the jury’s legal question or directing them to an instruction that fully supplied the requested rule, the court allegedly issued a **prohibition**: the jury **may not** consider those statutes and **will not** be instructed on them—while grounding that in the **misclassification** described above.

Under that framing, the problem is **instructional/legal**, not a benign exercise of discretion about how to manage exhibits.

**Why prolonged debate on this narrow point is usually unpersuasive**

- **Review of the legal premise is not deferential.** Where the dispute is whether **statutes are “evidence”** in the sense used to forbid the jury from applying them, that is a **question of law** about the correctness of the court’s rationale. Appellate courts do not defer to the trial judge’s **legal** mistake merely because the ruling was labeled discretionary.

- **Discretion has boundaries.** Practice recognizes that trial courts have leeway in **how** they respond to jury questions (e.g., repeating or clarifying instructions). That leeway generally **does not extend** to a response whose **foundation is legally wrong**—for example, a categorical bar on applying governing statutory standards because they were not “in evidence.”

- **Merits of “debate” shrink once the record is fixed.** If the record shows the jury **affirmatively requested** the governing duty rules and the court **forbade** consideration on an **evidence-vs.-law** mistake, the respondent is often left arguing harmlessness or record nuance—not the central legal classification. Those issues are narrower and harder to win than reopening the basic **law vs. evidence** distinction.

**Bottom line for strategy**

From the appellant’s perspective, the appeal is structured as: **mandatory duty under § 614** + **wrong legal category (statutes as evidence)** + **affirmative prohibition during deliberations** → **reversible instructional error**, not a close call on case management. Any defense that does not **frontally** address that structure (for example, by showing a legally correct alternative basis for the ruling, or a record that defeats the appellant’s sequence of facts) risks looking like collateral argument rather than an answer to the **dispositive** frame.
