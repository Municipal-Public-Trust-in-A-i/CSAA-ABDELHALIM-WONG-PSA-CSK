## III. Why the April 16 Admission Exposes Fraud on the Court

### A. Destruction of "Good Faith" (Scienter)

In any fraud action, scienter—the conscious intent to deceive—is the hardest element to prove. Defendants routinely assert that a false statement was a "good faith mistake," a "misunderstanding," or a "clerical error."

The April 16 admission annihilates that defense. By explicitly naming "Dolan" as the source of the body cams, Navaratnasingham demonstrates **affirmative, specific, and historical knowledge** of the evidence's chain of custody. One cannot casually name the specific law firm that handed over a piece of evidence years earlier if one is genuinely ignorant of that evidence's existence. The dismissive phrasing—"for whatever, sure"—reveals that possession of these records was a **recognized, established fact** within the defense trial team.

Therefore, when she previously stated that the defense "did not receive the 911 call until the day of the MSC" and that it was "news to me," it was not a mistake about what was in the file. It was a calculated linguistic maneuver designed to exploit the judge's focus on subpoena returns and timeliness, deliberately suppressing the defense's actual knowledge that the materials had been provided by Dolan during discovery.

### B. Judicial Estoppel

California courts enforce the doctrine of **Judicial Estoppel**, which prevents a party from asserting a factual position in a legal proceeding that is contrary to a position they successfully urged earlier in the same proceeding.

**Position A (The Suppression):** "Our office did not receive the 911 call until the day of the MSC." "This is news to me." "We did not have those two items until the day of the MSC." "This is not anything that we received as part of the subpoena." These positions were urged to block admission of evidence, deny continuances, and exclude the supplemental report.

**Position B (The Admission):** "Sure. They were provided by Dolan. He has had them for whatever, sure." This position was taken when it became tactically convenient to acknowledge the exhibit list and discuss the defense's curated body cam presentation.

A party cannot have it both ways. By capturing both statements on the official reporter's transcript, Plaintiff has closed-circuit proof of judicial estoppel. The Court cannot uphold a verdict where the prevailing party successfully utilized two mutually exclusive factual realities to manipulate the trial's evidentiary rulings.

### C. The Statutory Breach: Candor and Artifice

**California Rules of Professional Conduct, Rule 3.3 (Candor Toward the Tribunal):** An attorney shall not knowingly make a false statement of fact or law to a tribunal. Rule 3.3(a)(1) also mandates that an attorney must **correct** a false statement of material fact previously made. By making the "subpoena" and "news to me" statements to block evidence, and later making the "Dolan" statement without correcting the former, Navaratnasingham engaged in an ongoing violation.

**Business and Professions Code § 6068(d):** An attorney must employ "those means only as are consistent with truth, and never to seek to mislead the judge or any judicial officer by an **artifice** or false statement of fact or law."

The "subpoena shield" was an artifice—a legally crafted illusion. By answering a question about possession with a statement about subpoena returns, counsel manufactured a factual void. Discovering this artifice retroactively is the ultimate professional betrayal: the Court's authority was weaponized to suppress the truth.

### D. Extrinsic Fraud (Fraus Illata Curiae)

**Intrinsic Fraud:** A witness lies on the stand about a fact (e.g., the color of a traffic light). The adversary system expects opposing counsel to expose this through cross-examination. The system is working as designed.

**Extrinsic Fraud:** Opposing counsel lies **directly to the judge** about possessing the body cam footage of the traffic light, causing the judge to exclude it from the trial entirely. The latter is an existential threat to the adversary system.

Here, the defense did not present false evidence for the jury to weigh. They erected a **collateral barrier** that prevented Plaintiff from fully exhibiting his case. By keeping Plaintiff and the Court "in ignorance" of the true evidentiary record via an affirmative artifice (B&P Code § 6068(d)), the defense committed extrinsic fraud. This grants the equity court the inherent power to vacate the judgment entirely under *Kulchar v. Kulchar* (1969) 1 Cal.3d 467 and *Aldrich v. San Francisco Superior Court* (1983) 151 Cal.App.3d 720.

## XII. WHY THE EVENTUAL ADMISSION OF THE VIDEOS DOES NOT CURE THE FRAUD, WHY THE COURT'S RELIANCE ON FALSE REPRESENTATIONS IS INDEPENDENTLY FATAL TO THE JUDGMENT, AND WHY THIS IS NOT HARMLESS ERROR

There is a critical misconception that must be addressed directly because the defense will inevitably attempt to exploit it: the argument that because the body-worn camera footage was ultimately played for the jury, and because the 911 audio was eventually admitted, the false representations about those materials were rendered harmless. That argument misunderstands the nature of the fraud, the nature of the harm, and the nature of the constitutional guarantee that was violated. The eventual admission of a piece of evidence does not retroactively sanitize a pattern of false representations made under penalty of perjury to the trial judge, upon which the judge relied to construct the procedural architecture of the entire trial. The fraud was never about blocking the evidence permanently. The fraud was about controlling the conditions under which the evidence entered the trial, degrading its impact, disrupting the plaintiff's ability to present a coherent narrative, and manufacturing a false factual landscape in which every ruling the court made was tainted by a premise that the defense's own later admission proves was a lie. That distinction is dispositive, and it is the reason this case demands equitable vacatur rather than appellate correction.

Begin with the fundamental principle. Fraud on the court is not measured by whether the defrauding party succeeded in permanently excluding a piece of evidence. Fraud on the court is measured by whether false representations made to the tribunal corrupted the judicial process itself, depriving the opposing party of a fair adversary hearing. The California Supreme Court defined extrinsic fraud in Kulchar v. Kulchar (1969) 1 Cal.3d 467, 471, as fraud that "prevents a real contest on the merits" by keeping a party "in ignorance of the action or proceeding, or in some other way fraudulently prevented from presenting his claim or defense." The word "presenting" is operative. The fraud here did not prevent the evidence from ever being mentioned in the courtroom. It prevented Plaintiff from presenting that evidence in a timely, orderly, and coherent manner, with proper foundation, within a procedural framework that reflected the truth rather than a fabricated narrative of non-receipt and unknown provenance. The distinction between "the evidence eventually got in" and "the evidence was fairly presented" is the entire substance of the equity claim, and the certified record proves that the latter never occurred.

Consider what the false representations actually accomplished. Now turn to the legal standard for harmless error and why it does not apply. In an ordinary appeal, the reviewing court applies the harmless error standard of People v. Watson (1956) 46 Cal.2d 818, 836 for state-law error ("reasonably probable" that a more favorable result would have been obtained absent the error) or Chapman v. California (1967) 386 U.S. 18, 24 for constitutional error ("beyond a reasonable doubt" that the error was harmless). Both frameworks assume a fair process in which an identifiable error occurred. They ask: given the totality of the evidence, would the jury likely have reached a different result without the error? But those frameworks presuppose that the process itself was legitimate and that the error was an aberration within an otherwise fair trial. When the error is not an aberration but the product of deliberate fraud -- when the exclusion, the delay, the procedural disadvantage were all procured by false representations made under penalty of perjury -- the harmless error framework collapses because the baseline against which harmlessness is measured is itself corrupted. You cannot ask "would the jury have reached a different result without the error" when the entire evidentiary and procedural landscape within which the jury deliberated was shaped by fraud. The question is not whether the jury would have reached a different result. The question is whether Plaintiff was deprived of a fair opportunity to present his case. Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042 held that "[w]hen attorney misconduct is sufficiently egregious, it may constitute a denial of due process, rendering the trial fundamentally unfair regardless of the strength of the evidence." The fraud here satisfies that standard. It involved written declarations under penalty of perjury containing false factual predicates, oral representations in open court directly contradicting sworn testimony given minutes earlier, a continuing failure to correct those representations over a period of months in violation of California Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 3.3(a)(1), and a casual admission that proved the prior statements were knowing lies.

The Court's reliance on the false representations is independently fatal to the judgment because it means the judgment was not the product of a fair judicial process. Under In re Marriage of Rosevear (1998) 65 Cal.App.4th 673, 682, "[a] court may be said to have abused its discretion when it acts on the basis of incorrect or misleading information." When a court's exercise of discretion is based on false factual premises supplied by an officer of the court, the resulting orders are void as exercises of discretion because discretion, by definition, requires an informed judgment based on true facts. A judge who denies a continuance because he believes the defense was "not blocking" and the evidence is of unknown provenance -- when in truth the defense possessed the evidence for twenty months, subpoenaed it by name, and curated it for trial -- has not exercised discretion. He has been manipulated into issuing a ruling that serves the interests of the party that lied to him. That is not the administration of justice. That is the corruption of it.

The reliance is also fatal because it cannot be undone within the existing judgment. Under Blanton v. Womancare, Inc. (1985) 38 Cal.3d 396, 403, a party is "entitled to rely on the candor of officers of the court." The Court relied on Navaratnasingham's candor. That reliance was betrayed. But the reliance is now embedded in the judgment itself. The continuance denial, the MIL grants, the supplemental report exclusion, the 911 deferral -- these rulings are part of the trial record. They cannot be "corrected" on appeal because the appellate court reviews rulings for legal error, not for whether they were induced by fraud. The appellate court will see that the trial judge denied a continuance and will review that decision for abuse of discretion based on the record as it appeared to the trial judge. That investigation requires fact-finding, evidence, testimony, and the equitable jurisdiction of a trial court. That is what the independent action in equity provides and what no other procedural vehicle can accomplish.
